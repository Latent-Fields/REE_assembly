# Thought — From Understandable Falsehood to Protected Causal Belief

## Graded belief, affective psychosis, non-affective psychosis, and the developmental necessity of corrigible maps

**Date:** 2026-08-23  
**Status:** Raw thought intake; exploratory clinical-computational synthesis and source for later claim harvesting. This is not an architectural commitment, a diagnostic model, or authorisation to implement the mechanisms named below.  
**Relationship:** Continues `2026-08-23_controllability_attribution_serotonin_pathway_heterogeneity.md`. The earlier thought develops suspicion, controllability, causal attribution, threat–responsibility separation, post-commitment interpretative non-closure, and pathway-specific control of alternatives. The present thought asks what happens when a causal interpretation becomes increasingly held, begins organising later perception, and is or is not reopened by reprocessing.

## Provenance and user contribution

This thought developed from Daniel's clinical and architectural observations that ordinary people often make emotionally intelligible misattributions; that many such beliefs later resolve; and that psychosis may differ not only in the initial false inference but in how later evidence is used.

The initiating formulations included:

> “More than this is that many people make these kinds of misattribution and seem to have brief seemingly delusional like experiences which might in my mind be considered better as understandable falsehoods given the emotional and threat related tone which then later usually resolve if reprocessed.”

> “In psychosis the reprocessing seems not to update the false belief but update the other beliefs around the fixed false belief meaning what should have been an error correction process becomes how elaborate supporting beliefs and misattributions can emerge.”

The next observation exposed the graded nature of belief itself:

> “There are gradations of belief retained and held beliefs are maintained more if firmly believed and these firmly believed ones influence how things are interpreted.”

This made affective psychosis newly intelligible:

> “Affective psychosis becomes completely understandable now.”

But Daniel immediately marked the limit of that explanation:

> “Non affective psychosis cannot entirely be understood in this manner.”

The developmental importance for the Reflective–Ethical Engine (REE) then became explicit:

> “Very important since the organism will start with no map to guide perception.”

This thought should preserve the reasoning and possible placement logic needed by that process, but should not duplicate the harvesting process, assign final claim identifiers, or directly alter version plans.

## Central synthesis

Belief is not a binary condition in which an organism either possesses or does not possess a proposition. A representation may be weakly entertained, repeatedly available, highly credible, behaviourally dominant, affectively protected, structurally central, or resistant to revision. These properties can vary independently.

As a belief becomes more firmly held, it does more than survive. It gains authority over attention, interpretation, prediction and action. It becomes part of the map through which new evidence is perceived.

This is necessary. An organism that begins without a map must allow what it has already learned to organise later perception. Otherwise experience could never become more coherent or efficient. Yet the same developmental necessity creates a route to pathological closure: an early or emotionally compelling false attribution can become one of the assumptions used to judge the evidence that should have corrected it.

The key proposed distinction is therefore not simply between true and false belief. It is between:

1. a false interpretation that remains an eligible target of later error correction; and
2. a protected interpretation under which later error is redirected into surrounding explanations.

In the second regime, the organism continues to learn. The problem is that learning occurs under an implicit invariant: the protected causal belief must remain true.

## The mapless organism and the unavoidable bootstrap

The first organism does not begin with a complete representation of objects, causes, self, controllability, threat or reliable observation. It must discover regularities in a stream whose relevant divisions are not yet known.

At minimum it must progressively learn:

- what persists through time;
- which observations belong to the same source;
- which changes follow its own actions;
- which changes are externally generated;
- what is controllable, uncontrollable or jointly determined;
- which patterns predict benefit, harm, opportunity or uncertainty;
- which contexts alter those relationships;
- how much confidence previous learning deserves.

This produces an unavoidable loop:

\[
\text{ambiguous input}
\rightarrow
\text{provisional map}
\rightarrow
\text{map-guided sampling and perception}
\rightarrow
\text{new interpreted evidence}
\rightarrow
\text{map revision}
\]

The loop is not itself pathological. It is how a mapless organism becomes capable of perception. A learned map must make some observations more expected, some distinctions more salient, and some interpretations easier to recruit.

The safety problem is calibration. The influence a representation exerts over later evidence should remain proportionate to its support, domain, provenance and revision history. Early usefulness must not silently become permanent truth.

This suggests a general formulation:

> **An organism born without a map must learn by allowing uncertain beliefs to guide perception. Epistemic safety therefore cannot consist of preventing priors from influencing experience; it must regulate how strongly and where they do so, while preserving routes by which later evidence can revise the maps that selected and interpreted that evidence.**

## Belief heldness is a vector, not one scalar

The phrase “firmly believed” may conceal several distinct variables. A useful descriptive coordinate system might include:

| Dimension | Question | Functional consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Credence | How likely does this seem? | Relative ranking among explanations |
| Precision or conviction | How much should contrary evidence move it? | Resistance to revision |
| Accessibility | How readily is it recruited? | Frequency of use in interpretation |
| Centrality | How many other representations depend on it? | Cost and reach of revision |
| Affective charge | What is at stake if it is true or false? | Attention, memory and protective urgency |
| Policy authority | How strongly may it govern action? | Behaviour under residual uncertainty |
| Interpretive reach | How many observations may it explain? | Capacity to absorb ambiguity |
| Provenance confidence | How well grounded is its source? | Trust in the evidence that formed it |
| Revisability | Can reprocessing reopen it? | Capacity for later correction |
| Temporal persistence | How long does it remain available without reinforcement? | Stability across state and context |

This is a conceptual decomposition, not a proposal that REE must implement ten explicit registers.

The dimensions explain why credence alone is insufficient. A low-probability threat may rationally gain high policy authority when the possible harm is severe. A high-confidence but peripheral belief may barely affect behaviour. A belief of moderate stated conviction may nevertheless dominate perception if it is central, affectively charged and repeatedly accessible.

The organism therefore requires a separation already implicit in the preceding thought:

> **The need to act on a possibility does not establish the truth of its causal explanation.**

It may flee while uncertain. It may approach while retaining alternatives. It may commit to a trajectory without licensing the interpretation that motivated it to become unrevisable.

## Understandable falsehoods

“Understandable falsehood” is useful here as a mechanistic phrase, not a diagnostic category and not a claim that emotional intelligibility makes an inference accurate.

An understandable falsehood is a belief or attribution that is wrong but locally comprehensible given some combination of:

- incomplete or ambiguous evidence;
- the organism's prior experience;
- current affective state;
- threat asymmetry;
- limited time for information gathering;
- the consequences of failing to act if the feared interpretation were true;
- sensitisation by previous harm;
- ordinary limits on causal inference.

People can temporarily organise experience around such interpretations. Reprocessing in a safer state, exposure to more informative evidence, discussion, sleep, changed context, or the failure of expected consequences may reopen the causal model. The falsehood then weakens or resolves.

Longitudinal evidence that psychotic experiences can be transient, and that persistent experiences differ partly by degree of exposure, stress reactivity and associated vulnerability, is compatible with a continuum of formation and persistence rather than a simple boundary between rational cognition and psychosis. This does not mean that all ordinary false beliefs are psychotic experiences or that clinical psychosis is merely ordinary error at greater intensity.

## Reprocessing can correct or accommodate

Suppose an organism has adopted a causal hypothesis \(H_c\) and later encounters evidence \(\epsilon\) that is poorly predicted by it.

In a corrigible regime:

\[
\epsilon \rightarrow \Delta H_c + \Delta H_a
\]

where both the central hypothesis and its auxiliary assumptions remain eligible for change.

In a protected-core regime:

\[
\epsilon \rightarrow \Delta H_a
\quad \text{while} \quad
\Delta H_c \approx 0
\]

The mismatch is not ignored. It is explained by altering auxiliary beliefs \(H_a\): context, hidden causes, observation reliability, another person's strategy, deception, exceptions, timing, or the meaning of the evidence. The total model can continue reducing local error while its central causal commitment remains fixed.

This candidate process can be called **Protected-Core Error Accommodation**.

The name is provisional. Its important claim is that what appears from outside as failure to learn may contain substantial learning. Prediction error is being used, but it is updating the wrong representational target relative to the source of the mismatch.

In a persecutory example, apparently benign behaviour need not reduce the hypothesis that another intends harm. It can instead support a new auxiliary interpretation: the behaviour is concealment, the timing has changed, other people are complicit, the observer is being misled, or the apparent contradiction is itself part of the threat. Each new mismatch can increase the explanatory envelope around the protected attribution.

This is not necessarily conscious rationalisation. It may be the consequence of which latent representation is eligible to receive error.

## From one false belief to an elaborated system

The proposed progression contains several separable stages:

| Stage | Main operation | Possible failure |
|---|---|---|
| Formation | A causal explanation is recruited | Threat, affect, salience or anomalous experience disproportionately favours one account |
| Fixation | The explanation gains precision and centrality | It ceases to remain an appropriately uncertain hypothesis |
| Accommodation | Later mismatch is processed | Error updates surrounding assumptions rather than the causal core |
| Elaboration | Auxiliary explanations accumulate | The protected belief gains interpretive reach and apparent coherence |
| Enactment | Behaviour follows the model | Avoidance, checking or defence changes what evidence becomes available |
| Interpersonal feedback | Others respond to the behaviour | Their confusion, withdrawal or defence is incorporated as confirmation |
| Maintenance | The system repeatedly explains its own consequences | Corrective interventions become rarer or less legible |
| Resolution | The central attribution is reopened | Core revision permits dependent beliefs to relax or reorganise |

This progression makes an important distinction between **error correction** and **error accommodation**. Both reduce discrepancy. Only the first necessarily improves the causal model that produced the discrepancy.

It also suggests that elaboration is not mere addition. A central belief with high interpretive reach can restructure the meaning of memories, relationships and later events. Once sufficiently central, revising it may require simultaneous reorganisation of a large dependent model. That creates hysteresis: the evidence required to reverse the belief may be much greater than the evidence that originally established it.

## The evidence ecology changes after commitment

Held beliefs influence evidence in at least three ways:

1. **Selection:** they change what the organism attends to, remembers, samples or avoids.
2. **Interpretation:** they change what ambiguous events are taken to mean.
3. **Enactment:** they alter behaviour and therefore alter how the environment and other agents respond.

Threat amplifies all three. Avoidance can prevent disconfirmation. Monitoring can make coincidences more visible. Defensive behaviour can evoke defensive responses. Memory may preferentially retain threat-congruent episodes. None of these requires the original attribution to have been accurate.

The resulting evidence is not wholly fabricated: real observations and real social consequences occur. The causal error lies in treating evidence partly produced or selected by the belief as independent confirmation of it.

This makes provenance more demanding than identifying whether a percept was internally or externally generated. REE may also need to preserve whether an observation was:

- passively encountered;
- actively sought;
- produced by a diagnostic intervention;
- plausibly evoked by REE's own behaviour;
- repeatedly replayed rather than independently observed;
- interpreted under a currently dominant affective or threat regime.

## Affective psychosis becomes computationally intelligible

The model makes mood-congruent affective psychosis understandable as a limiting regime of ordinary graded inference. Affect does not merely colour an otherwise completed belief. It changes which hypotheses are readily available, how costly different errors appear, which observations become salient, how much confidence is assigned, and how vigorously the resulting model governs action.

In mania, the affective field may increase expectations of:

- personal efficacy and controllability;
- unusually large causal reach;
- exceptional ability or status;
- special significance in ordinary events;
- favourable outcomes and low vulnerability;
- abundant opportunity and reduced cost of commitment.

In severe depression, it may increase expectations of:

- personal blame for negative outcomes;
- low prospective control and inability to repair;
- irreversible loss or catastrophe;
- deserved punishment;
- pervasive negative self-causation;
- reduced access to alternative futures.

This is not a simple manic “over-attribution” and depressive “under-attribution” axis. Depression can combine under-attribution of prospective agency or positive causal credit with over-attribution of negative responsibility to the self. Mania can inflate prospective control, significance and capacity without increasing every form of causal attribution.

Affective psychosis may then arise through a reinforcing sequence:

\[
\text{affective state}
\rightarrow
\text{biased hypothesis availability and precision}
\rightarrow
\text{mood-congruent interpretation}
\rightarrow
\text{committed action and selective evidence}
\rightarrow
\text{apparent confirmation}
\]

When the affective state resolves, the precision landscape may relax. Alternative explanations become available again and reprocessing can revise the belief. This can explain why some affective delusions resolve substantially with recovery of mood. If the belief has become sufficiently central, has reorganised autobiographical memory, or has produced durable interpersonal consequences, it may persist as residual psychosis even after much ordinary reality testing returns.

The phrase “completely understandable” is therefore valuable phenomenologically and computationally, but should not be read as a complete biological explanation. A study using positron emission tomography found elevated striatal dopamine synthesis capacity in both bipolar psychosis and schizophrenia, with positive psychotic symptom severity related to dopamine synthesis across the combined sample; in bipolar psychosis the relationship remained after adjustment for manic severity. This supports shared transdiagnostic psychosis machinery in addition to mood-specific organisation.

## Why non-affective psychosis is not exhausted by this account

The graded-belief and protected-core model can help explain the maintenance and elaboration of non-affective delusions. It does not fully explain why the initial evidential world may be anomalous.

Non-affective psychosis may additionally involve disturbances in:

- assignment of salience and significance;
- precision and volatility estimation;
- sensory or propositional source monitoring;
- reafference and the sense of agency;
- self-versus-world or self-versus-other attribution;
- temporal and contextual binding;
- stability of representations across time;
- semantic organisation and thought;
- the distinction between simulated and externally grounded content.

Here the explanatory belief may be an attempt to account for experiences generated by failures elsewhere in the inferential system: a voice experienced as external, a thought that lacks ordinary self-provenance, actions that do not feel self-caused, or neutral events assigned anomalous significance. Affect can then select threatening, grandiose, guilty or protective explanations, but affect alone did not generate the anomaly that demanded explanation.

The distinction can be stated compactly:

> **In affective psychosis, affect may supply much of the organising prior and the force with which it is held. In non-affective psychosis, altered salience, provenance, agency or representational stability may help generate anomalous evidence for which a belief is subsequently constructed.**

Non-affective does not mean affect-free. Fear, shame, anger, relief and excitement can strongly shape content and maintenance after anomalous experiences begin. The claim is only that affect is not always a sufficient account of formation.

Nor is non-affective psychosis one failure. Different combinations could produce superficially similar beliefs. A high-precision prior, an unstable volatility model, a source-tag failure and a protected-core update failure should not be collapsed simply because each can contribute to delusion.

This fits existing REE architecture if formation and maintenance are kept separate. MECH-094 and MECH-201 offer accounts of hypothesis-tag loss and synthetic-to-real confusion. Precision-control work offers routes by which errors or representations gain inappropriate authority. The present thought adds a downstream question that those mechanisms do not fully answer:

> **Once a causal interpretation has been committed, where is later prediction error allowed to go?**

## Evidence relevant to the proposed distinction

Several empirical findings constrain, but do not prove, this synthesis:

- In sequential disambiguation tasks, people with schizophrenia and delusions have shown reduced integration of evidence that undermines initially plausible interpretations. Related effects also occur with delusion-proneness or schizotypal traits in nonclinical samples, supporting a graded dimension rather than a process unique to established psychosis.
- Belief conviction, willingness to consider that one may be mistaken, and haste in reaching a conclusion appear related but separable. This supports treating heldness as multidimensional rather than one scalar.
- In an information-sampling task, greater delusion severity in schizophrenia was associated with stronger reliance on beliefs formed early in inference even while participants sought more information. Information hunger can therefore coexist with sticky updating.
- Another schizophrenia study found an “all-or-nothing” update pattern that reduced the precision and flexibility of beliefs across environments. This warns against describing psychosis as uniformly too much or too little updating.
- Paranoia has been associated with a stronger prior expectation that the environment is volatile in a non-social reversal-learning task. Socially threatening content can therefore be influenced by domain-general uncertainty learning.
- Emotionally salient, negatively self-referential material can increase hasty inference in delusion-prone people without active psychosis. Threat or affect can expose a vulnerability that is less visible in neutral tasks.
- Longitudinal general-population studies distinguish transient from persistent psychotic experiences, with persistence associated with greater stress reactivity, trauma and other vulnerabilities. The boundary of interest may partly concern persistence and reprocessing rather than the occurrence of an unusual inference alone.

None of these studies directly demonstrates Protected-Core Error Accommodation. The mechanism proposed here is an inference intended to connect observed belief inflexibility with the clinical emergence of increasingly elaborate supporting explanations. It requires direct testing.

## REE interpretation: beliefs as constraints on later learning

REE does not need a linguistic proposition store for this problem to exist. A “belief” here may be a learned latent expectation, causal mapping, controllability estimate, self-transition model, source assignment, stable hidden-state hypothesis or trajectory prior.

A descriptive belief state may be represented conceptually as:

\[
B_i = (p_i,\ \pi_i,\ c_i,\ a_i,\ q_i,\ r_i,\ v_i,\ g_i)
\]

where, illustratively:

- \(p_i\) is credence;
- \(\pi_i\) is precision or resistance to revision;
- \(c_i\) is structural centrality;
- \(a_i\) is affective charge;
- \(q_i\) is policy authority;
- \(r_i\) is revisability;
- \(v_i\) is provenance confidence;
- \(g_i\) is interpretive reach.

This is a coordinate system for asking questions, not an implementation sketch.

Several separations appear necessary:

- high policy authority under threat must not automatically raise causal credence;
- repeated use of a model must not count as independent confirmation;
- centrality must not make a belief evidentially privileged merely because revision is expensive;
- affective charge may control attention and memory without directly writing truth;
- commitment must preserve the uncertainty and alternatives present when action was selected;
- prediction error must retain enough causal address to reach the representation responsible for the failed prediction;
- post-commitment reprocessing must sometimes reopen the committed causal model rather than merely optimise around it.

The healthy regime is not permanent openness. Maps must stabilise enough to support competent perception and timely action. The requirement is **calibrated closure with recoverable revision**.

## Relationship to controllability and selfhood

The preceding controllability thought is especially important because confidence at commitment can later help determine whether an outcome should update the self-model, world-model or estimate of external control.

The sense of self is therefore not an optional narrative addition. A solitary organism must learn what follows from its own actions, what lies beyond its control, and how reliable its own predictions are. Misrouting can produce opposite distortions:

- excessive negative self-causation and responsibility;
- diminished prospective agency;
- exaggerated personal control;
- externalisation of self-caused outcomes;
- attribution of internally generated events to external causes;
- failure to represent joint or uncertain causation.

Affective states can bias this geometry. Source, comparator or salience failures can disrupt it more fundamentally. In both cases, later error routing determines whether the model recovers or elaborates.

## Relationship to serotonin-pathway heterogeneity

The present account strengthens, rather than replaces, the preceding warning against treating serotonin as one scalar control.

Maintaining alternatives can refer to several computationally different tasks:

- holding competing perceptual parses;
- preserving causal hypotheses after action;
- resisting affective or threat-driven collapse;
- reopening a central self-belief;
- preventing auxiliary accommodation from shielding a causal core;
- widening future trajectories after an existing model fails.

These operations may impose superficially similar representational load while differing in target, timescale, projection, required action and learning consequence. A controller that appropriately maintains perceptual alternatives could cause paralysis if globally applied to motor commitment. A controller that reopens an autobiographical belief may need a much longer timescale than one resolving sensory ambiguity.

The biological heterogeneity of raphe pathways therefore remains useful as a warning and source of hypotheses, not a demand to reproduce nuclei directly. The mechanism described here should not be assigned to “serotonin” without resolving which form of alternative maintenance or reopening is meant.

## Candidate experimental consequences

These are investigation prompts, not implementation instructions.

### 1. Mapless developmental calibration

Raise an organism from sparse sensory streams and measure whether early learned regularities increasingly guide perception while remaining revisable after environmental contingencies change. Compare useful stabilisation with premature closure.

### 2. Action without epistemic collapse

Create a low-probability, high-cost threat that rationally requires protective action. Test whether policy authority rises without an equivalent rise in causal certainty or responsibility attribution.

### 3. Belief-dimension dissociation

Manipulate credence, affective charge, centrality and policy authority independently. Test whether they produce distinguishable effects on action, attention, memory and later revision.

### 4. Core correction versus auxiliary accommodation

Construct an environment with one central causal hypothesis and several auxiliary assumptions. Introduce evidence that specifically disconfirms the central cause. Measure whether the organism reduces core confidence or instead increases auxiliary complexity while preserving the core.

### 5. Error-address lesion

Preserve the size of prediction error while disrupting only its target attribution. The expected signature is intact mismatch detection with systematic updating of adjacent representations rather than the representation responsible for the failed prediction.

### 6. Reopening versus proposition-by-proposition challenge

Compare two repair routes after a protected system forms: disconfirm auxiliary beliefs individually, or restore eligibility of the central attribution for revision. The hypothesis predicts that reopening the core permits coordinated relaxation of dependent beliefs, whereas challenging auxiliaries alone encourages further accommodation.

### 7. Affective-field induction

Alter harm, benefit, controllability and goal-access regimes without lesioning provenance. Test whether manic-like and depressive-like attribution geometries emerge and resolve with state change, and identify when they leave persistent residue.

### 8. Formation–maintenance dissociation

Induce source/provenance disturbance, aberrant salience, affective precision change and protected-core updating separately and in combination. Similar surface beliefs with different internal signatures would argue against treating “delusion” as one architectural failure.

### 9. Evidence-ecology accounting

Record whether apparent confirmation was passively encountered, actively sampled, evoked by the organism's behaviour, replayed internally, or inferred under a dominant threat state. Test whether provenance-aware accounting reduces self-confirming loops without preventing necessary defensive action.

## Boundaries and cautions

- “Understandable falsehood” is a proposed explanatory phrase, not a clinical category and not a reason to minimise distress, risk or impaired functioning.
- Emotional congruence does not establish truth, and emotional incongruence does not establish psychosis.
- Delusions are not uniformly absolutely fixed. Conviction and flexibility vary over time and can respond to treatment, relationships and changing evidence.
- Affective psychosis may contain mood-incongruent symptoms and shared psychosis mechanisms. It should not be reduced to mood-congruent reasoning alone.
- Non-affective psychosis is not affect-free and is not a single mechanism.
- Protected-Core Error Accommodation is a candidate explanation of elaboration and maintenance, not a demonstrated universal mechanism.
- Auxiliary explanations are not inherently pathological. All causal models require background assumptions. The failure concerns asymmetrical revision that protects a central attribution irrespective of appropriately targeted evidence.
- Belief centrality can make revision expensive for legitimate reasons. Corrigibility does not require indiscriminate instability.
- The coordinate descriptions in this thought are tools for conceptual separation, not instructions to create one variable per term.
- The clinical analogies must not be used as behavioural success criteria for REE. Experiments should test computational dissociations directly.

## Core synthesis

> **A viable organism begins without a map and must construct one while acting under uncertainty. Learned representations must increasingly guide attention, perception and action or they would never become useful; but their growing influence creates a developmental risk. An emotionally compelling or threat-congruent false attribution can become part of the machinery that interprets the evidence that should correct it. Ordinary understandable falsehoods may resolve when reprocessing is allowed to revise the suspected cause. In a protected-core regime, prediction error is not absent: it is diverted into surrounding beliefs, so error correction becomes error accommodation and an increasingly elaborate explanatory system can form around a fixed causal commitment. Affective psychosis becomes computationally intelligible when mood alters hypothesis availability, precision, self-attribution, controllability and the cost of error. Non-affective psychosis cannot be exhausted by that account because anomalous salience, provenance, agency and representational stability may help generate the evidence requiring explanation. Both routes may converge on the same maintenance question: after commitment, which representation is prediction error allowed to revise? REE therefore requires neither permanent doubt nor premature certainty, but calibrated map formation in which action can commit, belief can stabilise, and the causal assumptions organising experience remain recoverably corrigible.**
