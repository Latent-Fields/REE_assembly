# Thought: Other-Agent Provenance and the Bounded Animistic Prior

Status: processed
Intake: evidence/planning/thought_intake_2026-08-12_other_agent_provenance_bounded_animistic_prior.md
Claims registered: INV-102, MECH-504

**Date:** 2026-08-12  
**Status (descriptive):** Exploratory hypothesis and extension of the responsibility-as-epistemic-hygiene thought. This does not establish that the proposed mechanism exists in REE, is biologically correct, or requires a particular implementation. Processed 2026-08-25 -- see Intake link above.

REE's existing self/other distinction, Theory of Mind, social prediction and responsibility machinery may have an additional epistemic function.

The previous thought considered why an acting organism must distinguish **evidence generated independently by the world from evidence downstream of its own commitments**. An organism changes its environment by acting within it. Without causal provenance, self-generated consequences can become apparent evidence for the beliefs that caused those consequences.

But REE does not inhabit a world containing only itself and passive environmental processes.

**Other agents also change the world.**

More importantly, they can change it *because of what they predict*.

That creates a qualitatively different epistemic problem.

## Not all causes are epistemically equivalent

An observation might result predominantly from:

**exogenous world process**  
→ X happened.

**self intervention**  
→ I caused X to happen.

**other-agent intervention**  
→ another agent caused X to happen.

**joint interaction**  
→ X arose from the interaction between my behaviour, another agent's behaviour and the wider environment.

These distinctions matter because another agent is potentially a predictive causal process.

A passive environmental process can contain complicated conditional structure. But another agent can additionally act according to something resembling:

> **I predict that if I do X, you will infer Y and subsequently do Z.**

The observed X is real.

Yet X may be poor evidence for Y precisely because X was selected by another agent to make Y appear probable.

Thus **truth of observation does not guarantee innocence of evidence**.

## Other-agent provenance

An epistemically competent organism may therefore need to represent not merely:

> *What happened?*

but:

> *What kind of process caused this evidence to become available to me?*

Once another-agent causation becomes plausible, inference may need to condition upon the possibility that the observed evidence was generated by a system possessing:

- goals;
- preferences;
- beliefs;
- predictions;
- commitments;
- knowledge unavailable to the observer;
- a model of the observer;
- incentives to reveal or conceal information;
- capacity to intervene selectively upon the shared world.

This does not imply deception.

The same structure applies to many social phenomena.

An agent may deliberately generate evidence in order to:

- warn;
- teach;
- cooperate;
- request;
- reassure;
- threaten;
- deceive;
- signal competence;
- signal weakness;
- conceal resources;
- reveal resources;
- manipulate;
- coordinate;
- test another agent;
- change another agent's beliefs for that agent's own benefit.

The epistemic requirement precedes knowing which of these is occurring.

First the organism must recognise:

> **This observation may have been produced by another mind.**

## A bounded animistic prior

This suggests that something resembling an **animistic prior** may have a legitimate epistemic function.

Not:

> *Everything has a mind.*

Rather:

> **Some structures in my environment may have been produced by agents, and agency should remain among the candidate causal explanations when the evidence warrants it.**

In a world genuinely containing other minds, assigning zero prior probability to intentional causation would be maladaptive.

An organism unable to ask:

> *Did something do this?*

would systematically misclassify strategically generated evidence as ordinary environmental structure.

It could learn the statistical regularities produced by another organism while failing to recognise that those regularities are **conditional on another predictive system's changing internal state**.

That would make the learner particularly vulnerable when the other organism changed strategy.

Thus a bounded tendency toward agency attribution may constitute a form of **epistemic hygiene**.

But it must be bounded.

Too little agency attribution produces exploitable naïveté.

Too much produces intentional explanations for stochastic, mechanical or otherwise exogenous events.

The computational problem is therefore not to eliminate animistic inference but to **calibrate it**.

## Agency attribution as causal model selection

The relevant question may be framed as competition among causal hypotheses:

\[
C \in
\{
C_{\text{world}},
C_{\text{self}},
C_{\text{other}},
C_{\text{joint}}
\}.
\]

Evidence should update the relative probability of these causal classes.

The categories themselves need not ultimately be discrete. Mixed causation is likely common.

A fallen object might involve gravity, REE's previous action and another organism's intervention simultaneously.

But maintaining the distinction matters because different causal classes license different subsequent predictions.

If an event is predominantly exogenous, environmental regularities may remain stable when nobody is observing.

If it is self-caused, future evidence must be interpreted conditional on REE's own intervention.

If it is other-agent-caused, future behaviour may depend upon the other agent's changing beliefs, goals and model of REE.

If it is jointly generated, prediction may require modelling the coupled system.

## Recursive causation

Once two agents model one another, causal inference becomes recursively structured.

REE observes X.

But perhaps:

**another agent produced X**  
→ because it predicted REE would observe X  
→ because it predicted REE would infer Y  
→ because it expected belief Y to make REE perform Z.

Now the observation cannot be interpreted adequately without some model of the process that selected it.

This does not require infinite recursion.

Biological organisms presumably operate with severely bounded approximations.

A useful system may only need enough recursion to distinguish:

> *This happened.*

from:

> *Something may have made this happen.*

from:

> *Something may have made this happen because of what it expected me to do next.*

Even that modest hierarchy radically changes inference.

## Deception as adversarial evidence generation

Deception is an especially clear limiting case.

Another agent need not alter REE's internal model directly.

It can alter the **world REE samples**.

It may:

- place misleading cues;
- selectively reveal true observations;
- conceal disconfirming observations;
- manufacture correlations;
- behave differently when observed;
- manipulate another agent into generating evidence;
- exploit REE's known priors.

This means an observation can be:

**accurately perceived, accurately remembered, and correctly attributed to the external world**

yet still produce an incorrect inference because its **selection process** was strategic.

Ordinary sensory accuracy is therefore insufficient protection against deception.

The organism needs some capacity to infer the provenance of evidence.

## Cooperation and teaching are the same epistemic problem

Importantly, other-agent provenance should not become synonymous with suspicion.

Teaching has almost the same causal structure as deception.

A teacher deliberately selects observations because they predict those observations will change another organism's internal model.

The difference concerns goals and alignment, not the existence of strategic evidence generation.

Similarly, a warning signal deliberately changes another organism's evidence because the sender expects a resulting behavioural change.

Thus the same architecture that permits detection of manipulation may be required for:

- communication;
- teaching;
- trust;
- cooperation;
- cultural transmission.

The appropriate inference is therefore not:

> *Agent-generated evidence is unreliable.*

It is:

> **Agent-generated evidence must be interpreted conditional on the agent that generated it and the likely reason it was generated.**

## Relationship to selfhood and responsibility

The preceding responsibility thought suggested:

> **Selfhood allows the organism to identify evidence partly generated by its own commitments.**

This thought proposes the corresponding social principle:

> **Other-mind attribution allows the organism to identify evidence potentially generated by another agent's commitments.**

Together they produce a richer epistemic provenance system:

**What did the world do?**

**What did I do?**

**What did another agent do?**

**What did we cause together?**

And subsequently:

**What should I learn from the resulting observation given that causal history?**

This makes self/other distinction more than an identity or empathy mechanism.

It becomes part of causal inference.

## Relationship to ethics

There is also an important connection to REE's ethical architecture.

Recognising another entity as an agent changes both:

**how its actions should be interpreted epistemically**

and

**how its existence should be treated ethically.**

These need not be identical thresholds.

An organism might warrant epistemic treatment as an agent before REE has enough evidence to assign richer moral status.

Conversely, uncertainty about another entity's agency or experience may itself carry ethical consequences.

The architecture should therefore avoid collapsing:

**agent detection**

**Theory of Mind**

**sentience attribution**

**ethical standing**

into a single binary variable.

But these systems may share evidence.

## Developmental implications

A young organism probably should not begin with an elaborate mature Theory of Mind.

It may instead possess simpler priors such as:

> *Some changes happen without me.*

> *Some changes happen because of me.*

> *Some changes seem to happen in ways responsive to me.*

The last category could provide a developmental route toward other-agent recognition.

Repeated observations such as:

**I move → entity responds**

**I stop → entity changes behaviour**

**I approach → entity withdraws**

**I pursue → entity evades**

**I signal → entity changes state**

may gradually justify a richer causal model.

This suggests that REE may need experience with **responsive environmental entities**, not merely static hazards and resources, before sophisticated social inference can meaningfully develop.

The environment therefore becomes part of the developmental problem.

## Possible REE implication

REE already contains or proposes machinery for:

- self/other distinction;
- self-impact attribution;
- Theory of Mind;
- social prediction;
- empathy;
- commitment;
- causal responsibility.

The question raised here is whether these currently converge sufficiently upon **evidence provenance**.

A future implementation should not merely classify:

`self` versus `other`.

It may need to preserve something closer to:

**observation**  
→ causal provenance estimate  
→ agency probability  
→ inferred intervention structure  
→ conditional epistemic update.

This should not be built automatically merely because the idea is attractive. Existing mechanisms and claims should first be inspected for overlap and adequacy.

## Experimental consequence

A particularly informative experiment would not begin with sophisticated deception.

Begin with a world containing two superficially similar causal processes.

One environmental process produces a cue according to a stable stochastic rule.

Another entity produces the same cue distribution initially but **changes its behaviour conditional on REE's behaviour**.

A purely statistical environmental learner may initially model both successfully.

The crucial test occurs when interaction changes.

Can REE discover that one source is better modelled as a responsive causal process?

Later experiments could progressively introduce:

**responsive behaviour**  
→ **goal-directed competition**  
→ **cooperation**  
→ **signalling**  
→ **teaching**  
→ **concealment**  
→ **deception**  
→ **recursive strategic interaction**.

The objective would not be to reward anthropomorphic interpretation.

It would be to determine whether explicit other-agent provenance improves prediction and adaptive behaviour **specifically when another predictive system actually exists**, while avoiding false agency attribution when it does not.

## Core hypothesis

> **In a world containing other agents, epistemic competence requires preserving the possibility that observations were strategically generated by another predictive system. A bounded prior for agency may therefore constitute epistemic hygiene: too little causes strategically generated evidence to be mistaken for passive world structure, while too much causes agency to be hallucinated in exogenous processes. Other-mind attribution may consequently serve not only social and ethical cognition, but causal provenance and reliable belief updating.**

This is best treated as the **multi-agent counterpart to responsibility as epistemic hygiene**.

The first asks:

> *How should I learn from evidence that my own commitments helped produce?*

The second asks:

> *How should I learn from evidence that another mind may have deliberately caused me to encounter?*
