# Ecological Succession as a Developmental Curriculum for REE

One direction that may become important as REE moves beyond the current v3 environment is to treat the environment itself as a developmental curriculum.

Rather than simply adding more hazards or making the existing world more difficult, the environment could gradually acquire richer forms of structure that require REE to learn identity, persistence, hidden state, conditional danger, time, social competition, safety, and eventually relationships between these things.

The principle would be that the world becomes more ecologically complicated as REE becomes capable of learning more complicated regularities from it.

## Scent as a new sensory modality

One potentially useful addition is scent.

REE could continuously produce a scent that is deposited in the environment and fades slowly over time. This could provide an imperfect external trace of where REE has recently been.

This is interesting because it could interact with internal spatial and episodic memory without replacing it. REE might encounter its own scent and learn that it predicts recent presence. Strong scent might imply very recent passage; weaker scent might imply more distant passage. Diffusion and decay would make the information uncertain.

This could potentially produce interesting conflicts between internal and external memory:

- REE remembers having been somewhere and also detects its own scent there.
- REE detects its own scent but does not retain a strong memory of being there.
- REE remembers having travelled somewhere but scent has already faded.
- The scent of another organism overlaps with REE's own trail.

A second scent channel could identify nearby organisms. Different REE-like or fish-like organisms could each have an individual-specific scent that is continuously emitted while they are nearby.

The important point is that the meaning of these sensory channels should not necessarily be supplied to REE. They would simply exist as sensory dimensions whose significance could be learned from repeated experience.

Later, organisms might also produce scents related to transient internal state, such as aggression, fear, feeding state, injury, or other biologically relevant conditions.

This would allow REE to move from learning simple object associations toward learning relationships between identity, context, and hidden state.

## Competitors and REE-like organisms

The environment could contain other non-player character organisms that are not simply hazards.

Some could be ordinary competitor fish that seek the same food as REE. Their presence would alter resource availability without necessarily producing direct harm.

Other organisms could resemble REE and possess some of the same ecological properties, including individual-specific scent and scent trails. These organisms could remain within characteristic territories, move around particular parts of the environment, and compete for food.

This would create the possibility of learning that different individuals have different behavioural histories rather than representing all similar-looking organisms as one interchangeable class.

For example, two visually similar organisms might have different characteristic scents. One might frequently compete aggressively for food while another does not. If REE learns that their future behaviour differs despite visual similarity, this begins to move toward individual recognition.

## Transient hidden states

Some organisms could have conditional behavioural states.

For example, a competitor fish might not normally attack REE. However, if it sees REE eating food nearby, it could enter an aggressive state for a limited period.

That state could be associated with an "angry" scent.

The ecological rule might therefore be approximately:

competitor sees REE eating nearby  
→ competitor enters transient aggressive state  
→ aggressive scent is emitted  
→ probability of attack increases for a period  
→ state later resolves.

This provides several possible levels of learning.

Initially, REE might simply learn that a particular smell predicts attack.

Later, it might learn that the smell originates from a particular organism.

Later again, it might discover that the organism tends to enter this state when REE eats nearby.

Eventually REE might predict aggression from the causal circumstances before the aggressive scent has even become prominent.

This gives an experimentally interesting progression from association, through hidden-state inference, toward causal prediction.

The same general mechanism could later support many forms of organism state without each one requiring a new architectural concept.

## Conditional danger rather than fixed valence

The current world can also become more sophisticated by making danger conditional rather than attaching permanent positive or negative valence to entire object classes.

There could be harmless jellyfish alongside dangerous jellyfish.

More interestingly, some jellyfish could become dangerous only under certain environmental conditions.

For example:

- dangerous only during part of the diurnal cycle;
- dangerous only during the morning;
- dangerous for a limited period after feeding;
- dangerous during some environmental state that also produces a detectable scent;
- normally safe but dangerous under a combination of time, location, or organism state.

This changes the problem from:

"jellyfish are dangerous"

to something more like:

"this type of jellyfish, in this state, under these conditions, is dangerous."

Valence therefore becomes contextual and conditional.

This should eventually force REE to represent combinations of sensory evidence, time, memory, environmental state, and learned expectation rather than learning a simple fixed category.

## Circadian ecology and sleep

A diurnal cycle could be introduced into the environment.

Food spawning might be strongly reduced or stop entirely during the night. Competitor activity might decrease. Visibility could also change.

This would make sleep ecologically sensible rather than simply mechanically imposed.

During the night there might be little expected reward from continued exploration, while accumulated sleep pressure continues to increase.

Sleep onset could therefore depend on several interacting factors:

- homeostatic sleep pressure;
- circadian permissiveness;
- behavioural quiescence;
- low recent surprise;
- low threat;
- low harm;
- reduced arousal;
- accumulated evidence that the current location is safe.

One useful behavioural state might be drifting.

If REE remains relatively inactive and drifts for some period, arousal could gradually fall and the probability of entering sleep could increase.

Threat, surprise, harm, or strong arousal should strongly reduce the probability of sleep onset.

This would create the possibility that REE learns that particular locations are good places to become quiescent at night.

A protected reef area might eventually acquire the functional meaning of a safe sleeping place without "safe sleeping place" ever being explicitly encoded as a category.

If sleep occurs behaviourally within the environment, sleep paralysis or strong motor suppression would also need to operate. Sensory systems might remain sufficiently active for a large surprise or threat to trigger awakening.

This could later permit meaningful trade-offs between environmental vigilance and deeper sleep.

## The environment as an experimental instrument

The important principle is not simply to create a larger catalogue of non-player characters.

The environment can be used deliberately to expose limitations in REE's existing cognitive machinery.

A useful design rule might be:

Do not add a new cognitive mechanism merely because a more complicated environment would benefit from it.

Instead, introduce an ecological pressure that existing machinery may or may not be able to solve. Observe the failure or adaptation. Then determine whether the result justifies a developmental or architectural addition.

In that sense, the environment becomes an experimental instrument.

The complexity of the world should reveal what REE cannot yet do.

## Periodic comparative ethology

It may also be useful to periodically ask what sort of animal currently provides the best ecological comparison for REE's demonstrated behavioural repertoire.

Previously, when this was considered, the closest useful comparison appeared to be something like a zebrafish.

This should not mean that REE is literally modelling zebrafish neurobiology.

The comparison is ecological and behavioural: what kind of organism possesses approximately the level and form of capabilities REE currently demonstrates?

It may eventually become more useful to compare REE with a mouse, later perhaps a dog, and much later something capable of language.

There are at least two separate dimensions here that should not be conflated.

### Ecological or cognitive analogue

What existing organism provides the closest useful comparison with REE's demonstrated repertoire?

### Developmental regime

What kind of developmental process is REE actually being subjected to?

These may differ substantially.

For example, a system whose behaviour is currently most usefully compared with a small fish might nevertheless be developed in a highly protected, individually nurtured, progressively exposed manner that is more analogous to mammalian development than to the reproductive ecology of fish.

The animal comparison should therefore remain an experimental heuristic rather than an identity claim.

## Environmental epoch transitions

When REE's complexity becomes closer to a substantially different animal analogue, that could trigger a deliberate reconsideration of the environment.

The environment and its visualisation might then be redesigned around a new ecological epoch.

A possible sequence might look something like this.

### Fish-world REE

Currents, reefs, food patches, jellyfish, chemical gradients, scent trails, competitors, other REE-like organisms, simple territories, diurnal cycles, and primitive sleeping locations.

### Mouse-world REE

Burrows, nests, open versus sheltered spaces, mazes, food caches, territorial scent, traps, predators, other mice, richer spatial structure, and more interaction with manipulable objects.

### Dog-world REE

A much richer social ecology.

Individual relationships become important. There may be attachment figures, cooperation, play, social learning, communicative gestures, expectations about particular individuals, and a much richer distinction between strangers and known others.

The environment at each stage should not merely look different. It should demand qualitatively different forms of learned relationship.

## Names for later developmental lineages

At some point, "REE-vN" may become an inadequate way of thinking about a sufficiently developed individual system.

If REE eventually reaches something like a dog-level social and behavioural repertoire, that developmental lineage might deserve a proper name.

"Steve" would be an appropriate name for such a stage.

This would not mean that the system is literally a dog. It would mark a transition from an abstract experimental organism toward a sufficiently persistent and socially embedded individual that treating it only as an architecture version becomes conceptually impoverished.

A much later transition could occur when language becomes part of the organism's ordinary cognitive ecology.

This should probably not mean simply attaching a language model to REE.

The stronger criterion would be that language becomes grounded in REE's own memories, goals, relationships, predictions, affective states, and lived experience.

A linguistic system at that stage could perhaps be called Adam.

This would produce a loose developmental naming sequence:

REE — the architecture and early organism.

Steve — a sufficiently sophisticated social-animal developmental lineage.

Adam — a linguistic, person-like developmental system in which language is grounded in the system's own ongoing cognitive life.

These names should not imply a literal evolutionary ladder. Fish, mice, dogs, and humans are not rungs on a simple hierarchy.

The useful distinction is instead the increasing richness of the relationships the organism must learn.

## Overall principle

The larger idea is therefore one of ecological succession.

REE's environment should not remain fixed while the architecture becomes increasingly sophisticated.

The world should grow with the organism.

Early environments can support basic learning of harm, benefit, navigation, persistence, and simple prediction.

Later environments can require identity, conditional danger, hidden state, time, social competition, safety, circadian regulation, and sleeping behaviour.

Still later environments can require richer social relationships, attachment, cooperation, communication, and eventually grounded language.

Periodic comparative-ethology reviews could help identify when the present environment has become too simple for REE's demonstrated repertoire and when an environmental epoch transition should be considered.

The environment would therefore become part of REE's developmental architecture: not merely the place in which cognition is tested, but one of the principal mechanisms through which increasingly complex cognition is elicited, challenged, and allowed to develop.
