## 1. Trigger

The preceding thought on claim rotation proposes a practical rule:

> When a claim stalls, rotate it.

A canonical scientific claim may be represented architecturally, behaviourally, developmentally, biologically, or in transformation coordinates. A change of representation can make a useful experiment visible without changing the underlying evidence or multiplying truth states.

That immediately creates a harder question:

> **When are two representations genuinely representations of the same claim, and when has the rotation changed the proposition?**

The problem is deeper than wording.

Two formulations can sound equivalent while making different predictions. Conversely, two formulations can look very different while remaining experimentally indistinguishable over the domain that currently matters.

A claim-rotation system therefore needs a way to distinguish:

- harmless representational change;
- useful sharpening;
- hidden assumption introduction;
- genuine claim splitting;
- and semantic drift.

The central proposal of this thought is that **prediction under intervention provides the checksum**.

Two representations should be treated as equivalent only to the extent that they remain compatible in the experimentally relevant predictions they generate across a declared domain of observations and interventions.

This is not a proposal that truth is reducible to prediction. It is a practical criterion for keeping scientific representations attached to the same empirical object.

---

## 2. Representations of a claim

Let `C` denote a canonical scientific uncertainty or claim.

Two explicit representations might be:

```text
R_A(C)
R_B(C)
```

For example, an architectural representation might say:

> `z_world` preserves the information needed for trajectory selection.

A transformation representation might say:

> A resource-left versus resource-right distinction survives encoding in a form that can propagate into the appropriate difference in predicted consequences and action.

These statements may be intended as two views of the same commitment.

But intention is not enough.

Each representation licenses predictions under particular probes:

```text
R_A(C) -> P_A(I, O)
R_B(C) -> P_B(I, O)
```

where:

- `I` is an intervention or controlled perturbation;
- `O` is an observable or family of observables;
- `P_A` and `P_B` are the predictions made visible by the respective representations.

The useful equivalence question is therefore not:

> Do these sentences sound like paraphrases?

It is:

> **Across the declared domain that matters for this claim, do these representations continue to constrain reality in compatible ways?**

---

## 3. Predictive equivalence is domain-relative

Representations need not be equivalent everywhere to be usefully equivalent somewhere.

Define an experimental domain `D` containing the observations, interventions, contexts, scales and times relevant to the current scientific question.

A practical notion of predictive equivalence is:

```text
R_A(C) ~=_D R_B(C)
```

when the two representations generate compatible predictions across `D`, within the precision justified by the claim and available measurement.

This domain-relative qualification matters.

Two formulations may agree under ordinary observation but diverge when:

- a candidate action is reversed;
- an environmental contingency is permuted;
- a latent is frozen;
- a downstream consumer is replaced with a supervised oracle reader;
- a representation is compressed or re-based;
- the developmental phase changes;
- a sleep update is withheld;
- a goal or directive state changes;
- or the same representation is used outside its original training regime.

If no such intervention lies inside the declared scope of the claim, the two views may remain practically equivalent for that scope.

If the intervention is central to what the claim is supposed to mean, divergence matters.

So:

> **Empirical equivalence is not absolute. It has an envelope.**

That envelope should be named rather than silently assumed.

---

## 4. Predictive equivalence is not predictive precision

Two representations can remain compatible while differing greatly in how sharply they constrain an experiment.

Suppose one formulation says:

> E2 should matter for action selection.

Another says:

> If resource direction is reversed while candidate action identity is held fixed, the ordering of the corresponding candidate futures should reverse before E3 arbitration.

The second statement may be a legitimate operational refinement of the first rather than a new claim — if it follows from the first plus already accepted background assumptions.

But it is much more precise.

This yields a useful distinction:

```text
predictive equivalence != predictive precision
```

Two representations can refer to the same underlying claim while one exposes:

- a narrower timescale;
- a sign prediction rather than a vague magnitude prediction;
- a conditional dependence;
- a causal ordering;
- a specific intervention;
- a spatial locus;
- a developmental window;
- or a falsifiable inequality.

That sharpening is often exactly why rotation is useful.

The scientific gain may come not from adding information but from **finding coordinates in which the existing commitment makes a more discriminating prediction**.

---

## 5. When sharpening becomes a new claim

There is a boundary.

Suppose the sharper prediction follows from:

```text
C + accepted background model B
```

Then the sharper representation may be an inferential refinement of `C` under `B`.

But if the sharper prediction requires a new empirical assumption `A`:

```text
C + A -> sharper prediction
```

then the sharpened formulation is no longer merely `C`.

It is at least:

```text
C AND A
```

and `A` must not inherit `C`'s evidence merely because it was introduced during rotation.

This restates and strengthens the safeguard in GOV-ROTATE-1:

> A representation may expose consequences of a claim, but it may not smuggle new empirical assumptions into the old truth state.

A useful semantic checksum therefore asks not only whether two representations predict the same things, but **which auxiliary assumptions are required to make them do so**.

---

## 6. Observational equivalence and interventional equivalence

A particularly important case is the difference between observational and interventional equivalence.

Two representations may account for the same passive observations while implying different responses when the system is deliberately perturbed.

For example:

- “the latent contains directional information”;
- “the latent provides an action-usable directional representation.”

These can look similar under a generic decoder.

They separate under intervention:

- freeze the latent;
- replace reinforcement learning with a supervised policy-capacity reader;
- compare against a raw-field ceiling;
- preserve width while changing compression basis;
- alter the action-to-effect mapping;
- or test whether decision-relevant content is recoverable nonlinearly.

The intervention reveals what passive decodability does not.

This gives a general rule:

> **When two representations appear equivalent observationally, look for an intervention under which their implied causal structure differs.**

The resulting divergence can mean at least three things:

1. the representations were never actually equivalent;
2. the canonical claim was underspecified;
3. the rotation exposed a genuine new proposition.

All three are useful scientific outcomes.

## 7. Reality as semantic checksum

The phrase **reality as semantic checksum** is deliberately metaphorical.

A checksum does not tell us everything about a document. It tells us whether what we currently hold is still compatible with a reference constraint.

Similarly, experiment does not supply a perfect semantic oracle. But it can constrain whether two scientific representations remain attached to the same empirical commitment.

The loop is:

```text
claim
  -> representation
  -> prediction
  -> intervention / experiment
  -> observation
  -> prediction error
  -> representation or claim revision
```

If two purportedly equivalent representations repeatedly survive the same discriminating interventions with compatible predictions, their equivalence envelope expands.

If they diverge, reality has identified semantic strain.

The proper response is not automatically to choose one and discard the other. The divergence may reveal:

- a hidden assumption;
- a missing scope condition;
- a conflated mechanism;
- an unrecognized scale dependence;
- a distinction between observational and causal content;
- or two claims that should never have shared one truth state.

So the checksum is not a binary synonym detector.

It is a scientific discipline:

> **Representational equivalence earns its scope by surviving discriminating contact with the world.**

---

## 8. Representational drift versus wording drift

There are at least two different kinds of drift.

### Wording drift

The language changes while the empirical commitment remains effectively unchanged.

This is mostly harmless, provided provenance remains clear.

### Representational drift

The coordinate system changes in a way that gradually alters:

- which variables are treated as primitive;
- which relationships are assumed;
- which interventions matter;
- which observables count as success;
- what scale the claim applies at;
- or what causal direction is implied.

Representational drift can happen without any obviously new sentence.

A scientific programme is particularly vulnerable when attractive explanatory framings become progressively more concrete. A metaphor becomes a mechanism; a useful analogy becomes a dependency; a dependency becomes treated as established architecture.

This thought therefore recommends that important rotations carry an explicit account of:

- scope;
- auxiliary assumptions;
- observables;
- interventions;
- expected signatures;
- and known divergence points from the parent representation.

Not every claim needs this machinery. It is most useful when the rotation is doing real scientific work.

## 9. An equivalence envelope

A compact representation of the idea is an **equivalence envelope**.

For a pair of views, record the domain in which equivalence is currently licensed:

```text
EquivalenceEnvelope(R_A, R_B):
    contexts
    interventions
    observables
    temporal scale
    spatial / representational scale
    auxiliary assumptions
    tolerated prediction difference
    known divergence points
```

This is not proposed as a mandatory schema or another registry.

It is a reasoning primitive.

For example:

> architectural and transformation views are treated as equivalent for current resource-direction experiments under fixed environment dynamics and matched candidate sets, but equivalence has not been established under action-semantic remapping or developmental regime shift.

That sentence is much safer than “these are the same claim.”

It also suggests the next useful experiment.

## 10. Choosing experiments by representational disagreement

If two plausible representations of the same uncertainty make different predictions somewhere, that disagreement is valuable.

Instead of asking only:

> Which experiment most directly tests my preferred hypothesis?

ask:

> **Where do the current representations disagree most sharply while remaining experimentally tractable?**

This is closely related to ordinary discriminating experiment design, but with representational choice made explicit.

A useful probe maximizes some combination of:

- difference in predicted sign;
- difference in expected ordering;
- difference in causal dependence;
- difference in temporal locus;
- difference in generalization pattern;
- difference in required readout complexity;
- and difference in expected response to intervention.

Representational disagreement therefore becomes a source of experiment generation.

The scientific process need not know in advance which representation is “right.” It can use their divergence to ask a better question of reality.

## 11. Predictive-processing resemblance

This structure is conspicuously similar to predictive processing.

At a very abstract level:

```text
R_t(C)
   -> P_t
   -> A_experiment
   -> O
   -> epsilon_t
   -> R_(t+1)(C)
```

where:

- `R_t(C)` is the current representation of the claim;
- `P_t` is the prediction it makes salient;
- `A_experiment` is the epistemic action taken to test it;
- `O` is the resulting observation;
- `epsilon_t` is unresolved prediction error;
- and `R_(t+1)(C)` is the revised representation or, where necessary, revised claim structure.

The resemblance should not be overclaimed.

Scientific reasoning is not thereby shown to be mechanistically identical to cortical predictive processing, and REE's governance system is not literally a biological predictive-coding network.

The useful structural analogy is narrower:

> **When prediction error remains unresolved, changing representation can expose a form of the prediction that reality can discriminate more sharply.**

That is very close to what claim rotation is doing when it works.

## 12. Precision weighting in the scientific analogue

Predictive-processing language suggests another useful analogy: not every mismatch should update the claim equally.

The scientific equivalent of precision weighting is already present in REE governance through distinctions such as:

- readiness;
- dynamic range;
- non-degeneracy;
- evidence class;
- denominator choice;
- substrate stability;
- claim alignment;
- and whether an intervention actually reached the mechanism.

A prediction error observed under a broken readiness gate should not move the claim like one observed under a fully discriminating intervention.

This does not require importing a formal Bayesian precision model into governance.

It simply makes explicit that:

```text
prediction error x epistemic reliability -> update pressure
```

rather than raw mismatch alone.

This parallel helps explain why the forge's insistence on readiness and autopsy is not merely bureaucracy. It determines whether the observed discrepancy should have authority over the current representation.

## 13. The creature and the scientist face the same abstract problem

There is a useful recursion across the accompanying thoughts.

The creature may contain information but fail to make it behaviourally accessible.

The scientist may contain evidence but fail to represent the scientific uncertainty in a way that makes the next discriminating experiment apparent.

The creature may need to transform a rich latent into a decision-relevant geometry.

The scientist may need to rotate a rich claim/evidence structure into coordinates where a sharp prediction becomes visible.

The analogy is methodological, not mechanistic.

In both cases:

> more information is not necessarily the missing ingredient;

and:

> a better representation can change which action is easy to derive from information already present.

This is one reason the recent REE representational bottleneck is scientifically interesting beyond its local engineering importance.

## 15. Relation to the current `z_world` lineage

The resource-direction lineage provides a concrete illustration.

V3-EXQ-978 weakened the simplest information-loss story because directional resource information was already substantially decodable from `z_world` despite poor competence.

The question was rotated from:

> Is directional information present?

into:

> Is the representation adequate for an actual action consumer?

V3-EXQ-1002 then removed ordinary reinforcement-learning credit assignment by fitting a supervised reader and still found a large action-mapping deficit.

The next ambiguity was:

> Is this merely a 250 -> 32 capacity bottleneck, or is the learned representation itself inadequate?

V3-EXQ-1008 showed that PCA-32 of the same 250-dimensional input preserved much more of the oracle mapping than trained `z_world`, eliminating the simple width-bound explanation. At the same time, linear re-bases of `z_world` did not restore the missing mapping, splitting the broad “geometry mismatch” story and surfacing a more specific possibility that decision-relevant content is discarded during encoding or only recoverable nonlinearly.

This sequence is relevant here because the scientific object did not change each time.

The **representation of the uncertainty** changed, and each rotation exposed a more discriminating intervention.

Reality repeatedly acted as the checksum.

It also illustrates why equivalence must be earned rather than assumed. “Information present,” “action-usable representation,” “adequate 32-dimensional channel,” “linearly recoverable content,” and “nonlinearly recoverable content” are adjacent formulations, but they are not interchangeable claims.

The experiment lineage has progressively separated them.

## 16. A possible governance principle for later digestion

This is not yet a proposed change to claims.yaml or the governance rules.

A candidate principle for later digestion is:

> **A rotated representation remains attached to the same canonical claim only while its experimentally relevant predictions are compatible with the parent representation over an explicitly declared domain. If a discriminating intervention reveals divergence, governance must classify the divergence as a scope condition, hidden auxiliary assumption, underspecified parent claim, invalid rotation, or genuinely new proposition before evidence is allowed to transfer across the representations.**

A companion principle is:

> **Predictive sharpening does not by itself create a new claim. A sharper prediction may remain an inferential refinement when it follows from the canonical claim plus already accepted background assumptions. New empirical assumptions must be split rather than inherited.**

These should be treated as candidate formulations only until thought intake checks whether existing governance already covers them.

## 17. What would make this thought unnecessary or wrong

This framing should not become governance machinery merely because it is elegant.

It would be unnecessary if:

- GOV-ROTATE-1 plus ordinary claim-splitting already handles every practically important case;
- experiment design already records scope and auxiliary assumptions adequately enough that no semantic drift occurs;
- explicit equivalence envelopes add paperwork without changing decisions;
- scientific users can reliably distinguish operational refinement from new empirical content without additional tooling;
- or no historical case can be found where two purportedly equivalent representations would have transferred evidence incorrectly.

It would be misleading if:

- prediction were treated as the sole meaning of a scientific claim;
- observational agreement were mistaken for causal equivalence;
- a representation with sharper predictions were automatically treated as more true;
- lack of a known discriminating intervention were mistaken for proof of equivalence;
- or the predictive-processing analogy were upgraded into a mechanistic identity claim.

The practical success criterion is narrower:

> **Does explicitly checking predictive equivalence prevent semantic drift and generate better discriminating experiments without multiplying governance overhead?**

If not, retain the idea as a reasoning note rather than a formal rule.

---

## 18. Concise formulation

> **Scientific claims can be represented in multiple coordinate systems, but representational equivalence should be earned empirically rather than assumed linguistically. Two views remain the same claim only within a declared envelope where their experimentally relevant predictions remain compatible. Rotations may sharpen prediction without changing truth state; when they require new empirical assumptions or diverge under discriminating intervention, the difference must be made explicit. Reality functions as a semantic checksum by exposing where apparently equivalent representations stop constraining the same world.**

And the operational version:

> **When a claim stalls, rotate it. When two rotations disagree, experiment where the disagreement is sharpest. Let reality tell us whether we changed coordinates or changed the claim.**

---

## 19. Closing thought

A scientific programme can fail in two opposite ways.

It can become too rigid, keeping one representation of a problem long after that representation has stopped generating useful experiments.

Or it can become too fluid, changing representation so freely that empirical commitments drift without being noticed.

Claim rotation solves the first problem only if some discipline solves the second.

The proposed discipline is simple in spirit:

> rotate freely, but keep the rotations answerable to the same world.

The world does not care which coordinate system was elegant.

It supplies observations after interventions.

Those observations constrain whether our different representations were genuinely different views of one thing, or whether somewhere in the rotation we quietly started talking about something else.
