## Core question

An acting system experiences far more than it can preserve indefinitely. The important question is therefore not only how experience is represented, but:

> What becomes worth writing into the enduring organism?

This is the write-boundary problem.

## Hypothesis

If experience is organised through the constraints of the machine, then persistence should be selective rather than indiscriminate. Events should become durable in proportion to their expected usefulness for future regulation, prediction, agency, and causal understanding.

A useful write process may therefore be sensitive to:

- regulatory consequence;
- prediction error;
- controllability;
- causal novelty;
- repeated usefulness;
- unresolved ambiguity;
- future decision relevance.

## Why this matters

A world model that records everything equally will either saturate or become dominated by irrelevant detail. A world model that records only perceptual novelty may miss stable but consequential structure. A world model that records only reward-like outcomes risks collapsing the richness needed for counterfactual reasoning.

The write boundary must therefore solve a compression problem under organism-specific constraints.

## Working distinction

Three processes should be kept conceptually separate:

1. **Immediate encoding** — what is represented now;
2. **Selective persistence** — what remains available later;
3. **Representational reorganisation** — how prior experiences are regrouped or reinterpreted when new structure is learned.

These may occur on different timescales and may map differently onto waking updates, replay, and sleep.

## Predictions

If selective incorporation is regulation-sensitive:

- regulatorily surprising events should persist more strongly than equally novel but inconsequential events;
- controllability-changing events should be preferentially retained;
- replay should disproportionately revisit events whose future consequence model remains unresolved;
- events can become newly important after later learning and therefore be reweighted retrospectively;
- memory persistence should track future usefulness better than raw sensory salience alone.

## Developmental consequence

The write rule itself may develop.

Early in learning, the system may preserve broad classes of events because it does not yet know which distinctions matter. As its ontology improves, persistence can become more selective. This creates a possible developmental trajectory from noisy retention toward principled compression.

## Relation to replay and sleep

Replay need not merely reinforce stored traces. It may be the process by which the system decides what prior experience *meant* in light of newer structure.

Possible replay functions include:

- reassignment of experiences to newly learned categories;
- updating causal interpretation;
- separating self-caused from external outcomes;
- consolidating regularities across episodes;
- weakening obsolete associations;
- preserving unresolved counterfactual branches.

This makes sleep/replay a candidate site for ontology change, not just memory strengthening.

## Red team

A simpler explanation is that generic prediction error plus recency already yields the needed persistence profile. If so, a special write-boundary mechanism is unnecessary.

Another possibility is that selective persistence belongs entirely to hippocampal or episodic machinery while `z_world` should remain a momentary state representation. The experiment should therefore test interfaces rather than assume one storage locus.

A third possibility is that over-selective writing causes catastrophic narrowing: the agent remembers only what its current model believes matters and becomes unable to revise that model.

Any viable write rule therefore needs some mechanism for preserving anomalies and uncertainty.

## Minimal experiments

### Consequence versus novelty

Match two event classes for perceptual novelty while varying regulatory consequence. Measure later recall, latent influence, rollout accuracy, and behaviour.

### Retrospective revaluation

Present an initially neutral event that later becomes causally important. Test whether replay can increase its effective weight without re-exposure.

### Controllability transition

Present events that change from controllable to uncontrollable or vice versa. Test whether these transitions receive preferential persistence and whether obsolete causal models are weakened.

### Uncertainty preservation

Compare systems that aggressively compress ambiguous events with systems that retain unresolved alternatives. Test later adaptation when disambiguating evidence arrives.

## Decision rule

Do not add a new persistence mechanism until current replay, memory, and latent-update pathways are inspected.

The immediate question is whether the present system already exhibits selective persistence by consequence and whether failures around `z_world` reflect representation loss, write loss, or downstream retrieval loss.
