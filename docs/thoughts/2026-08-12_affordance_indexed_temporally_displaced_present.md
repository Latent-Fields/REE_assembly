# Thought: The Present Smeared Across Time — The Affordance-Indexed, Temporally Displaced Present

## Core proposal

The subjective present may not correspond to the objective physical instant conventionally labelled *now*.

Instead, the rich sensory and agentive experience of “now” may be a **short-horizon predictive construction displaced slightly into the physical future**, organised around the consequences of actions to which the organism is presently committed.

The displacement need not be large. It may ordinarily be only tens to hundreds of milliseconds: approximately the timescale over which immediate perception, motor preparation, action and expected sensory consequences can be integrated.

The important claim is therefore not merely that the brain predicts the future. Prediction is ubiquitous.

The stronger proposal is:

> **The state experienced as the present may itself be a prediction: an affordance-indexed estimate of the organism and world at the short future horizon at which presently committed action is expected to take effect.**

This may explain why the experienced world feels richly present despite sensory transmission and neural processing necessarily reporting a world that has already passed.

## The present is smeared across physical time

There may consequently be no single physical timestamp corresponding to subjective now.

Different sensory processes have different latencies. Different actions also require different times to become causally effective.

An eye movement, a reach, withdrawal, a step and a longer coordinated action do not share one implementation horizon.

For an affordance `a`, the relevant state may therefore resemble:

`ŝ(t + τ_a | a)`

where `τ_a` reflects the timescale on which that particular action can affect the organism-world relationship.

Subjective now may therefore be **smeared across physical time**, with different components temporally displaced according to their associated affordances and control horizons.

This does not imply that explicit representations of many different futures are consciously experienced. Rather, their integration may constitute the apparently unitary experienced present.

## Before commitment: branching is useful

Before action commitment, the organism faces multiple possible futures.

At this stage, branching is not a defect. It is where **choice can exist**.

Different candidate actions imply different predicted trajectories:

- `a1 → P(s(t+τ) | a1)`
- `a2 → P(s(t+τ) | a2)`
- `a3 → P(s(t+τ) | a3)`

The differences between those imagined futures provide the information upon which choice can operate.

Prematurely suppressing this branching would therefore impair deliberation.

## Commitment changes the prediction problem

Commitment fundamentally alters this geometry.

Once an action or trajectory has been selected, one major source of uncertainty about the immediate future has been reduced:

**the organism has constrained its own future behaviour.**

Commitment therefore does more than select an action.

> **Commitment creates predictability.**

Before commitment, the future depends partly upon what the organism *might* do.

After commitment, it depends partly upon what the organism is now *going to do*.

The selected branch can therefore become a relatively narrow **predictive tube** extending forward through physical time.

This suggests an important constraint: the subjective present cannot simply be projected onto an unresolved branch that may never occur. A sufficiently well-formed prediction must exist before commitment can support forward displacement of the experienced present.

The commitment point may therefore be closely related to the point at which one predicted future becomes sufficiently determinate to organise ongoing perception and action.

## The commitment horizon

Commitment does not determine the indefinite future.

Eventually the currently selected trajectory reaches a point beyond which consequential future action has not yet been determined.

This creates a **commitment horizon**.

Within that horizon:

- the organism's own immediate actions are substantially constrained;
- prediction can therefore be relatively precise;
- sensory evidence primarily updates and corrects an unfolding trajectory.

Beyond it:

- future actions remain unresolved;
- predicted futures increasingly branch;
- probabilities replace the relatively constrained committed trajectory;
- future choice remains open.

The experienced present may lie near the leading portion of this well-predicted committed region.

The future beyond that boundary may be cognitively represented, imagined or planned, yet phenomenologically feels different: it is **future**, rather than the richly sensory *now*.

This may help explain the otherwise peculiar subjective distinction between an immediate future that feels almost present and a future only slightly farther away that already feels comparatively immaterial.

## Nested commitments imply nested temporal horizons

Commitments can exist at several scales simultaneously.

A person walking toward a kitchen while carrying a cup may have:

- a highly constrained upcoming footfall;
- a several-second commitment to walking along a path;
- a longer commitment to reaching the kitchen;
- substantial uncertainty about what happens after arrival.

Subjective temporal organisation may therefore contain nested predictive horizons rather than one universal boundary.

This could allow a richly stable immediate present while retaining progressively greater uncertainty farther into the future.

## After commitment, affordances change function

Affordances need not play the same computational role before and after commitment.

Before commitment:

> **affordances define possible choices.**

After commitment:

> **affordances primarily support execution.**

Once a trajectory has acquired behavioural authority, continuously reopening competition among every alternative would make coherent behaviour impossible.

Instead, lower-level affordances can smooth the committed trajectory:

- adjust the reach;
- alter a foot placement;
- maintain balance;
- correct grip;
- navigate a small obstacle;
- compensate for prediction error.

Thus coherent agency requires almost opposite treatments of alternatives on either side of commitment:

> **Before commitment, alternatives must remain available. After commitment, alternatives must normally lose authority.**

## Prediction error and fast interrupt

Commitment cannot be irrevocable.

The committed predictive trajectory must continually be compared with incoming evidence.

Small prediction errors can be corrected without reopening the underlying choice.

Moderate discrepancies may modify execution while preserving the higher-order commitment.

But sufficiently consequential prediction violations require something qualitatively different:

> **interrupt the committed trajectory and reopen the branching future.**

The resulting control structure becomes:

**branching prediction → evaluation → commitment → constrained predictive trajectory → correction/smoothing → prediction violation → interrupt → renewed branching.**

A rapid defensive or orienting system may therefore have a special constitutional role. It need not immediately understand what happened. It need only possess sufficient authority to suspend the assumption that the committed predicted future remains valid.

Slower investigation can follow.

## Interruption may reveal the temporal displacement

If subjective now normally occupies a predicted point slightly ahead of incoming sensory evidence, an interruption creates an unusual problem.

The organism had already constructed something like:

**predicted-now:** `s(t + Δ)`

But incoming evidence shows that the committed trajectory is no longer valid.

The system must reconstruct its estimate closer to:

**corrected-now:** `s(t)`

Subjectively, this could occasionally appear as an event moving **backwards** or otherwise violating ordinary temporal order—not because physical perception literally travels backwards, but because the previously predicted present has been withdrawn and replaced by a less forward-displaced estimate.

This produces an empirical prediction:

> **Unexpected interruption of a highly predictable action-linked sequence should sometimes reveal the forward displacement of subjective now through temporal reversal, expansion, discontinuity or related timing illusions.**

The magnitude of such effects might provide information about the normal displacement itself.

## Existing psychophysics is compatible with important components

Several established phenomena are compatible with parts of this model.

Learned action-effect timing alters perceived event timing. When people learn a particular delay between an action and its sensory consequence, subsequent effects are perceived partly according to **when they were predicted to occur**, rather than purely according to physical sensory timing.

Voluntary actions and their consequences can become perceptually bound together in time, and this binding depends on predictive relationships between intended actions and outcomes. Sensorimotor temporal recalibration can even produce apparent reversal of action and consequence under some conditions. Predictive remapping around eye movements and postdictive reconstruction around saccades likewise show that perceptual timing is actively reconstructed rather than read from a simple timestamped stream.

These findings do not demonstrate that subjective now resides in the predicted future. They establish something required by the hypothesis:

> **subjective temporal organisation is influenced by learned predictions about the consequences of one's own actions.**

## Reconsidering neural decisions that precede reported conscious decisions

The theory also suggests a different way of thinking about the long-standing observation that neural activity related to voluntary action can precede reported awareness of deciding.

The conventional comparison implicitly treats reported subjective time as though it were an independent clock aligned with physical time.

But if subjective now is itself **action-dependent, predictive and reconstructive**, that assumption becomes questionable.

The classical Libet-style interpretation is already empirically complicated by later work on readiness-potential accumulation, probe-based intention timing, and the instability of retrospective clock reports.

The present theory suggests an additional conceptual distinction.

Early neural activity need not represent:

> **a completed unconscious decision awaiting later conscious discovery.**

It could instead participate in:

> **constructing candidate futures → narrowing alternatives → approaching commitment.**

Conscious actionable presence may become particularly coherent once commitment sufficiently constrains the organism's own immediate future to permit a stable forward prediction.

Thus:

**preparatory neural activity**

→ branching/narrowing of possible futures

→ **commitment**

→ reduced uncertainty about future self-action

→ stable action-conditioned prediction

→ **rich subjective actionable present**

→ execution.

If so, the temporal relationship between neural preparation and reported intention does not straightforwardly measure a delay between “the brain deciding” and “consciousness finding out.”

The subjective temporal reference against which the report is made may itself be one of the things generated by the action-control process.

## Prediction and postdiction need not be opposites

The theory does not require perception to be exclusively predictive.

A successful system should combine prediction with retrospective correction.

Prediction provides a coherent actionable present when the world behaves approximately as expected.

Unexpected evidence can subsequently revise that construction.

The experienced present may therefore be continuously negotiated between:

**prediction from the committed trajectory**

and

**correction from incoming evidence.**

This potentially connects predictive temporal effects, intentional binding, sensorimotor recalibration, chronostasis and other forms of postdictive temporal reconstruction without requiring them all to arise from one identical mechanism.

## Implications for REE

This theory should **not** be taken as evidence that the Reflective–Ethical Engine (REE) possesses subjective experience.

It instead suggests a potentially interesting computational analogue.

REE's E2 action-conditional prediction could provide differentiated possible futures.

E3 could provide competition and commitment.

A commitment mechanism could then reduce uncertainty concerning the organism's own future actions and permit predictive processing to proceed farther along the selected trajectory.

Lower-level mechanisms could correct execution without reopening the choice.

Fast prediction-error or threat pathways could acquire interrupt authority.

The architecture would therefore require more than:

> **generate futures → choose minimum cost.**

It would require something closer to:

> **generate branching futures → compare → commit → protect execution → predict through the committed trajectory → smooth deviations → detect violations → interrupt when necessary → reopen choice.**

This provides another reason why commitment may be architecturally important in REE beyond simply reducing behavioural indecision.

## Falsifiable consequences

The proposal generates several broad predictions.

The forward displacement of subjective now should increase when immediate action consequences are **highly predictable**, and weaken when they are uncertain.

Changing action-effect latency should recalibrate subjective temporal organisation even if the sensory events themselves are otherwise unchanged.

Commitment should alter temporal perception differently from mere action preparation.

Unexpected interruption should produce stronger temporal reconstruction than expected within-trajectory correction.

Well-learned actions should permit a more stable forward-displaced present than unfamiliar actions whose consequences remain poorly predicted.

The transition from pre-commitment branching to post-commitment execution should correspond to a measurable change in how alternative affordances influence control.

These predictions allow the hypothesis to fail.

## Central hypothesis

> **The rich subjective present is not necessarily the physical present. It may be an affordance-indexed, temporally displaced predictive construction lying slightly ahead of incoming sensory evidence. Before commitment, multiple probabilistic action-conditioned futures coexist and provide the space in which choice can occur. Commitment selects a sufficiently well-predicted branch, reduces uncertainty about the organism's own future behaviour, and permits subjective now to organise around the immediate predicted consequences of that trajectory. Within the resulting commitment horizon, affordances primarily support correction and smooth execution. Beyond it, the future branches again. Major prediction violations trigger interruption, collapse the existing predictive construction, and reopen choice.**

The consequence is a different picture of subjective time:

> **we may not experience the world precisely where it physically is. We experience the richly predicted point at which we are presently able to act upon it.**
