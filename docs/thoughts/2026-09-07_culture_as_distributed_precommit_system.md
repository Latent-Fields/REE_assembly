# Culture as a Distributed Precommit System

**Status:** Thought / hypothesis-generating synthesis  
**Date:** 2026-09-07  
**Scope:** Commitment, imagination, cultural learning, narrative, humour, fiction, drama, science fiction, social cognition  
**Relationship to REE:** Extension of existing commitment-boundary and default-mode architecture; not presently an architectural commitment.

**Companion evidence note:** [`2026-09-07_culture_as_distributed_precommit_system_evidence.md`](./2026-09-07_culture_as_distributed_precommit_system_evidence.md)

## Core thought

Humans do not need to believe a proposition in order to represent it, explore it, emotionally respond to it, compare it with existing beliefs, or learn something from it.

This separation may be one of the mechanisms that makes rapid cultural development possible.

Humour, fiction, drama, myth, science fiction, games, thought experiments and other cultural forms provide socially structured spaces in which propositions, worlds, identities and courses of action can be instantiated **without ordinary commitment to their truth or enactment**. They therefore allow individuals and societies to explore regions of belief and behavioural space that would be prohibitively dangerous, expensive, slow or socially disruptive to investigate through committed action alone.

In REE terms, culture may function as a **distributed precommit system**.

It does not merely transmit established beliefs. It generates, distributes, mutates and evaluates candidate trajectories before individuals or societies have to commit to them.

## 1. The architectural foothold already exists in REE

This proposal does not require inventing a new REE subsystem.

The existing commitment boundary already distinguishes hypothetical cognition from committed action. Before commitment, candidate trajectories may be simulated, rehearsed, imagined, compared and rejected. They remain hypothetical and do not acquire the responsibility-bearing status of enacted trajectories. After commitment, action becomes owned and its consequences become capable of leaving durable traces.

Relevant existing sources include:

- `README.md`, especially the commitment-boundary and residue sections.
- `docs/claims/claim_index.md`, especially INV-011, **Imagination Without Belief Update**.
- `docs/architecture/control_plane.md`, describing a default-mode-like regime with reduced sensory precision, suppressed commitment, and imagination/counterfactual exploration.
- `REE_CORE.md`, where E3 includes the commitment gate and control plane.

REE therefore already contains the fundamental distinction required by the present hypothesis:

> **A representation can be instantiated without being committed.**

Culture may exploit this distinction at scale.

## 2. Culture can supply trajectories that an individual did not generate

The important extension is that candidate trajectories need not originate entirely within the individual organism.

Language allows one mind to provide another with representational material.

REE's existing language architecture already anticipates part of this function. `architecture/language/language_and_learning.md` describes language as able to bias priors, accelerate generalisation, enable counterfactual reasoning without direct experience, and transmit socially acquired negative knowledge.

E1 already includes long-horizon outcome expectations together with narrative and counterfactual structure. Hippocampal-like machinery supports imagination and counterfactual exploration, while E3 can evaluate trajectories generated through deep narrative prediction and hippocampal replay.

This suggests a simple but important possibility:

> **Another mind can construct a trajectory and transmit enough of its structure for my own predictive system to instantiate it.**

I therefore do not have to personally undergo every relevant experience.

Someone else can supply the trajectory.

Culture can preserve it.

Thousands or millions of other minds can subsequently replay, modify, reject or elaborate it.

## 3. Fiction is not failed factual communication

A fictional narrative is unusual information.

The recipient is often explicitly informed that the described events did not occur. Nevertheless, the recipient constructs characters, environments, motives, causal sequences, emotional consequences and possible futures from it.

Experimental work supports the idea that epistemic framing can alter processing. Narratives presented as factual versus fictional have produced differences interpreted as reconstructive versus constructive processing in some studies, although later work has not consistently reproduced broad fact-fiction differences. This mixed literature is important: the useful prediction is unlikely to be that a fiction label globally switches cognition into a different system.

More recent work also suggests that fiction-derived memories can subsequently contribute representational material to novel imagined events when equivalent autobiographical experience is unavailable.

This makes fiction potentially valuable precisely because it is **not believed as ordinary fact**.

A useful hypothetical representation need not answer:

> Did this happen?

It may instead answer:

> Could something like this happen?
>
> What would follow if it did?
>
> What might someone in this situation feel or do?
>
> What actions would become available?
>
> Which consequences had I previously failed to represent?

The distinction matters greatly for REE.

## 4. “Imagination without belief update” may require decomposition

INV-011 remains important. Imagining an event must not ordinarily cause REE to believe that the event occurred.

But human cultural learning suggests that “belief update” may be too unitary a category if interpreted literally across every representational layer.

Fiction might appropriately leave some variables unchanged while modifying others.

For example, fictional information should ordinarily **not** directly update:

- autobiographical occurrence: *this happened to me*;
- historical occurrence: *this event actually occurred*;
- source truth: *the narrator's proposition describes reality*;
- responsibility attribution: *I performed this action*;
- responsibility-bearing residue.

Yet the same representation might legitimately update:

- possibility estimates;
- causal expectations;
- representations of other agents;
- possible motivations;
- policy repertoire;
- harm and benefit expectations;
- social contingencies;
- counterfactual vocabulary;
- the range of trajectories available for later simulation.

The appropriate architecture may therefore not be:

> **hypothetical → learning OFF**

but:

> **hypothetical → source-sensitive selective permeability**

The hypothesis tag protects particular kinds of commitment while permitting useful representational learning elsewhere.

This is an important distinction for future REE work.

## 5. Fiction as inexpensive experiential expansion

Direct experience is an extraordinarily expensive learning mechanism.

Some mistakes kill the learner. Others harm another person. Some social configurations occur too rarely for an individual to sample them adequately. Some future conditions do not yet exist. Some morally important situations should preferably never be deliberately reproduced merely for educational purposes.

Fiction allows an organism to traverse approximate representations of such states without incurring their full physical cost.

The contrast is roughly:

**Direct learning**

experience → consequence → model revision

versus:

**Cultural precommit learning**

received trajectory → hypothetical instantiation → causal/social/affective simulation → comparison with existing expectations → selective representational update → possible future commitment

This substantially enlarges the effective experience available to a finite organism.

## 6. Drama may go further than fiction

Drama and role-play introduce another possibility.

Reading about another agent primarily requires modelling its trajectory. Enacting another role may temporarily recruit parts of one's own action and social machinery under the constraints of another identity or situation.

This suggests a possible distinction:

> **Fiction:** simulate another trajectory.
>
> **Drama:** partially run one's own generative machinery under another agent's boundary conditions.

The distinction is speculative, but experimentally tractable.

If correct, enacted role-play should produce different effects on other-model formation, policy availability or affective prediction from semantically equivalent descriptive narratives.

Drama may therefore be a particularly powerful mechanism for exploring what another person could experience without permanently confusing self and other.

## 7. Humour as controlled norm violation

Humour may expose an especially interesting use of the precommit boundary.

Much humour deliberately introduces something inappropriate, impossible, taboo, contradictory, threatening or norm-violating while simultaneously signalling that ordinary interpretation and commitment should be relaxed.

The benign-violation literature provides a useful partial model: humorous material can involve something represented simultaneously as a violation and as sufficiently safe, distant or otherwise benign to tolerate.

In REE terms, humour may sometimes perform something resembling:

> activate norm → instantiate violation → inhibit ordinary commitment → explore alternative interpretation → evaluate discrepancy → resolve, reject or partially retain it

This permits the mind to enter representational regions that might otherwise be immediately excluded.

Humour may therefore have an epistemic function beyond entertainment.

It can test whether a supposedly fixed boundary remains coherent when violated.

## 8. Humour may also probe other minds

Humour is frequently social.

A joke can reveal not merely whether *I* can tolerate a representation, but whether *you* can.

That makes humour potentially a low-cost social assay of commitment.

A joke can implicitly ask:

> How seriously do you hold this norm?
>
> Can this interpretation be temporarily suspended?
>
> Do we share enough assumptions for this violation to remain benign?
>
> Where does your tolerance boundary lie?
>
> Are we members of the same interpretive community?

The response provides information about another agent's normative and representational structure.

Humour can therefore simultaneously explore one's own precommit space, probe another agent's commitment landscape, estimate social similarity, discover group norms, and negotiate whether a previously prohibited representation can become discussable.

This is potentially highly relevant to REE's developing social-model and similarity machinery.

## 9. Science fiction as long-horizon cultural rollout

Science fiction provides an unusually clear example of the proposed mechanism because its target states may not yet exist.

A society can begin modelling the consequences of artificial persons, autonomous weapons, pervasive surveillance, genetic modification, radical life extension, virtual worlds, reproductive technologies, ecological collapse, or contact with radically different minds before these states become ordinary lived reality.

Science fiction therefore performs something analogous to **long-horizon cultural rollout**.

A technological trajectory can be instantiated in narrative space before society commits to constructing it.

Different authors generate different rollouts. Readers evaluate them. Other authors mutate them. Political, ethical and scientific discourse can subsequently inherit structures first explored fictionally.

Culture may therefore have partially traversed a region of possibility space before technological reality reaches it.

In REE language, this resembles externally generated long-horizon candidate trajectories becoming available to E1/E3-like evaluation before the corresponding state has been directly sampled.

## 10. Culture as distributed counterfactual search

The broader hypothesis now becomes visible.

Individual cognition has finite search capacity.

Culture connects many such systems.

One person generates a hypothetical trajectory. Others receive it. Each reconstructs it through somewhat different priors, experiences and social models. Variants are generated. Some are rejected. Others become memorable. Some become stories, jokes, myths, metaphors, genres, philosophical problems or political propositions.

The resulting process resembles distributed search over possible worlds.

Culture therefore may do more than preserve knowledge.

It may provide civilisation with a **distributed precommit workspace**.

Within this workspace:

- representations can exist without endorsement;
- incompatible possibilities can coexist;
- dangerous possibilities can be explored without enactment;
- social consequences can be rehearsed;
- other minds can be simulated;
- existing norms can be violated provisionally;
- distant futures can acquire representational structure;
- failed trajectories can disappear cheaply;
- useful structures can persist without their originating propositions being literally true.

Only some outputs eventually cross into committed belief, policy, technology or behaviour.

Culture can therefore accelerate belief progress because **search is much cheaper than commitment**.

## 11. Cultural evolution may partly be search over commitment boundaries

Cultural change may not consist simply of one committed belief replacing another.

Novel ideas may instead spend substantial periods circulating in partially protected precommit forms.

They appear first as jokes, satire, fictional worlds, thought experiments, theatrical roles, speculative philosophy, imagined technologies, or impossible stories.

Repeated exposure makes the representation easier to instantiate. Consequences become more richly modelled. Previously unavailable language develops around it. Social responses become observable. The proposition may gradually cease to require as much representational protection.

Eventually something that could previously only be entertained fictionally becomes discussable propositionally, and something discussable propositionally may eventually become actionable.

This yields a possible cultural progression:

> **unrepresentable → representable only under protection → hypothetically discussable → socially negotiable → propositionally credible → behaviourally actionable → committed**

This should not be assumed to occur universally or monotonically. But it provides a mechanistic hypothesis for how cultures can approach disruptive changes without requiring abrupt collective belief transitions.

## 12. The same permeability creates pathology and manipulation

A completely impermeable hypothetical system would learn little from imagination.

A completely permeable one would be unable to distinguish imagination from reality.

The useful system must lie between them.

This immediately creates failure modes.

Repeated fictional trajectories may distort perceived likelihood. Emotionally powerful narratives may acquire inappropriate precision. Propaganda may exploit narrative simulation to alter other-models without adequate evidence. Humour may gradually reduce the effective cost of previously prohibited representations. Imagined threats may modify expectations of groups never directly encountered. Cultural availability may be mistaken for environmental frequency. Source information may decay while narrative structure remains.

REE's existing language failure analysis already identifies **spurious residue from narrative contamination** (`architecture/language/language_failure_modes.md`).

This is not necessarily an accidental weakness.

It may be the unavoidable price of a system capable of learning from counterfactual experience.

The architectural problem is therefore not to prevent hypothetical material from influencing cognition.

It is to ensure that **the kind and magnitude of update remain appropriate to the epistemic status of the representation**.

## 13. A source-sensitive update architecture

This thought suggests that future REE work should distinguish at least three properties that can otherwise be conflated:

### Representability

Can this state enter the generative system at all?

### Epistemic commitment

Does the system treat this representation as describing the actual world?

### Behavioural commitment

Has the system selected and owned an action on the basis of the representation?

These need not move together.

A fictional state may have high representability, low epistemic commitment and zero behavioural commitment while still modifying the future space available to the system.

Similarly, testimony might produce high representability, intermediate epistemic commitment and zero immediate behavioural commitment, while direct observation could carry another precision profile again.

This could make epistemic source tagging a major component of mature REE cognition.

## 14. Experimental implications for REE

The hypothesis is unusually testable.

A future REE capable of linguistic or symbolic scenario injection could receive semantically identical information under different source frames:

- **direct observation:** “You observe X.”
- **testimony:** “Another agent tells you X happened.”
- **fiction:** “Here is a fictional story in which X happens.”
- **counterfactual:** “Imagine that X happened.”
- **prediction:** “X might happen.”
- **joke:** X represented under a humorous/nonliteral frame.
- **memory:** “You remember X happening.”
- **committed experience:** REE actually performs or experiences X.

The important measurement would not be a single belief score.

We should inspect differential change in:

- world-state belief;
- source confidence;
- E1 expectations;
- E2 predictions where relevant;
- other-agent models;
- harm/benefit expectations;
- policy availability;
- hippocampal trajectory repertoire;
- E3 evaluation;
- precision allocation;
- residue;
- subsequent behaviour.

The predicted result is not:

> **fiction causes no update**

The stronger prediction is:

> **Epistemic framing determines which components of the model are authorised to update.**

Direct experience, testimony, fiction, humour and counterfactual reasoning may therefore produce recognisably different **update fingerprints**.

That could eventually become an assay of whether REE has developed a meaningful distinction between imagination, belief and action.

## 15. Predictions

If this account is approximately correct:

1. An agent unable to learn anything from hypothetical trajectories should require substantially more direct experience to develop flexible behaviour.
2. An agent allowed to update all structures equally from hypothetical trajectories should develop reality-monitoring failures.
3. Useful cultural learning should emerge between these extremes through source-sensitive update.
4. Exposure to diverse fictional trajectories should enlarge the space of subsequently generated candidate trajectories even when none of the fictional events are believed to have occurred.
5. Role enactment and narrative observation should produce partially overlapping but distinguishable update fingerprints.
6. Humorous framing should permit exploration of some norm-violating representations at lower immediate commitment or defensive cost than literal assertion.
7. Repeated hypothetical exposure may alter possibility priors before it alters truth beliefs.
8. Source-tag degradation should create characteristic errors in which retained narrative structures become increasingly difficult to distinguish from experienced or testified information.

These predictions allow the cultural hypothesis to become an experimental question rather than merely an interpretive metaphor.

## 16. Broader implication

The precommit/commit distinction may be important at more than one scale.

Within an individual, it separates imagined trajectories from owned action.

Between individuals, language allows trajectories to be transmitted.

Across societies, cultural forms preserve and mutate those trajectories.

Across generations, culture allows agents to explore situations that none of the current participants personally experienced.

Culture may therefore be understood partly as an extension of the cognitive machinery that makes it possible to think without immediately acting and to represent without immediately believing.

That capacity changes the economics of learning.

A system restricted to committed experience must pay for much of its knowledge with real consequences.

A system with counterfactual cognition can learn from possibilities.

A society with culture can learn from **other people's possibilities**.

The resulting search space is vastly larger.

## Central conjecture

> **Culture functions partly as a distributed precommit workspace in which minds collectively generate, exchange and evaluate candidate beliefs, identities, norms and futures before those representations acquire full epistemic or behavioural commitment. Fiction, drama, humour and science fiction are not peripheral products of cognition under this account; they are specialised cultural technologies for making otherwise costly regions of possibility space cognitively traversable.**

If REE's commitment architecture is approximately right, these cultural phenomena may be consequences of the same deeper computational requirement: an intelligent agent must be able to explore a possible world without first mistaking it for the actual one.

And once minds can exchange those possible worlds, individual counterfactual cognition becomes cultural evolution.

## Status and caution

The proposal above is a synthesis, not a demonstrated homology between REE modules and human cultural cognition. The architectural prerequisites are present in REE and several component claims have independent empirical support, but the distributed-precommit formulation itself remains a falsifiable hypothesis. The companion evidence note records the present evidence strength, counterevidence and proposed assay.
