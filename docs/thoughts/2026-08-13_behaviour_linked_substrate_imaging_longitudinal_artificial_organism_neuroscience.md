## Originating thought

> “I wonder about applying some brain scan like behavioural analysis. We could look at what activity in r e substrate is associated with what behaviour. Extending temporally and associative”

This thought treats REE increasingly at the whole-organism level rather than solely as a collection of individually tested mechanisms. The central proposal is to observe internal REE substrate activity simultaneously with behaviour, then investigate which distributed internal patterns are associated with particular behaviours, behavioural transitions, environmental events, internal states, and developmental changes.

The analogy to a conventional brain scan is incomplete because REE potentially permits much richer observation: rather than indirectly measuring a proxy of underlying activity, it may be possible to record essentially every experimentally relevant internal variable continuously and exactly.

The proposal is therefore not merely to produce an attractive visualisation of REE internals. It is to develop a behavioural-neuroscience-like experimental instrument for an artificial organism.

## Dynamic substrate visualisation

> “A bit like my brain map visualisation pulsing and colour changing”

The REE substrate could be represented as a stable spatial map in which major systems retain consistent positions so that a human observer gradually learns the visual topology. Activity could be represented dynamically through carefully defined visual channels: intensity or brightness for activity magnitude; colour for signed quantities or functional state; pulsing for transient activation or prediction error; size or halo for gain, precision, salience or related quantities; changing connections for functional interaction or information flow; and slower background changes for persistent variables such as residue, motivational state, commitment, or control mode.

These mappings must be explicitly defined and mechanically derived from recorded variables. The display should never animate according to what the architecture is *supposed* to be doing.

The behavioural Fishtank and substrate visualisation could run synchronously. One panel would show **what the organism is doing**; the other would show **what its internal architecture is doing while it does it**. A common timeline would permit pausing, rewinding, and inspecting particular events.

## Behaviour-triggered substrate analysis

A first analytical layer could resemble event-related analysis in neuroscience. Behavioural events might include leaving or entering the reef, approaching or obtaining food, encountering an adverse event, avoiding a previously adverse region, reversing direction, beginning or abandoning a goal, entering or releasing commitment, sleep and waking, and end-of-life termination.

Internal substrate activity could be aligned around these events across repeated occurrences and across organisms. Rather than asking only whether some variable changes during a behaviour, this would permit examination of the **temporal sequence preceding and following behaviour**.

For example, activity 100 steps before reef departure may contain information about what subsequently occurs. Some substrate changes may precede behaviour, others accompany it, and others may reflect its consequences.

## Temporal extension

The analysis should not be restricted to activity immediately surrounding behaviour. Associations could be investigated across progressively longer temporal distances:

`substrate(t-1) → behaviour(t)`

`substrate(t-10) → behaviour(t)`

`substrate(t-100) → behaviour(t)`

`substrate(t-1000) → behaviour(t)`

and potentially across substantial fractions of an individual REE lifetime.

This is particularly relevant to mechanisms intended to preserve consequences over time: memory, residue, commitment, developmental learning, and sleep-related consolidation. An important event early in life might alter substrate organisation that predicts behaviour thousands of steps later. Conversely, apparently similar behaviour at different ages might arise from very different internal organisations.

## Distributed patterns rather than isolated variables

The important unit of analysis may often not be a single substrate variable. Whole or partial substrate-state vectors could be analysed to identify recurrent distributed configurations associated with behaviour.

Without imposing behavioural labels beforehand, analysis might reveal recurring internal regimes corresponding approximately to exploration, foraging, safety-seeking, threat response, goal pursuit, or resting. Those interpretations would remain hypotheses until independently tested.

This also creates the possibility of representational-similarity analyses. Internal states could be compared according to current behaviour, environmental location, goal, recent history, developmental age, previous adverse experience, and subsequent behaviour.

Two physically different situations might generate similar distributed substrate states because REE functionally treats them similarly. Conversely, identical environmental states might generate very different substrate configurations because REE's history or current goal differs. This could provide a route toward studying functional organisation or something resembling internal behavioural meaning without assuming that an individual latent variable straightforwardly “represents” a concept.

## Longitudinal artificial-organism neuroscience

This becomes particularly interesting when combined with long continuous REE lifetimes. Internal-behaviour relationships themselves may change during development.

An early REE might leave safety primarily in association with novelty or exploration machinery. A mature REE might produce outwardly similar behaviour through remembered environmental structure, established goals, and more directed prediction. The same behaviour would therefore have different internal antecedents at different developmental stages.

Longitudinal analysis could ask whether internal behavioural signatures stabilise; new functional associations emerge; previously important mechanisms become less important; sleep changes subsequent substrate organisation; individual developmental histories produce persistent differences; major environmental events create long-lasting changes; and distinct behavioural strategies correspond to distinct internal organisations.

Checkpointing makes unusually strong experiments possible. The same artificial individual could potentially be branched at a particular developmental age and exposed to different environments or mechanism perturbations.

## From association to causation

Association should be treated as discovery rather than proof. Once a substrate configuration associated with a behaviour has been identified, REE provides experimental possibilities that are unusually difficult in biological organisms.

A candidate mechanism can be perturbed. A checkpoint can be restored. Environmental conditions can be replayed. A matched unperturbed version of the same individual can be compared with the altered version.

The general programme could therefore become:

**observe → associate → hypothesise → intervene → replay → adjudicate**

This is substantially stronger than merely predicting behaviour from internal state. It may eventually permit developmental perturbation studies in which a mechanism is necessary for a behaviour early in development but ceases to be necessary later because another part of the architecture has acquired the relevant function.

## Human visual processing as part of the scientific instrument

> “And my visual processing as a human means the bandwidth for my participation in assessment is significantly increased”

A particularly important reason for building the dynamic visualisation is not merely presentation. At present, human inspection of REE is constrained by the bandwidth of logs, manifests, numerical summaries, and individual plots. These remain essential for adjudication, but they are relatively serial forms of inspection.

A synchronised Fishtank and dynamic substrate map could expose large amounts of information to human parallel visual processing. Potentially salient phenomena include synchronised activity, propagating activity, recurrent patterns, abrupt state transitions, persistent activity following events, oscillatory behaviour, developmental changes, changes around sleep, and mismatches between apparent behavioural state and internal state.

The role of visual inspection would explicitly be **hypothesis generation, not evidential confirmation**. Human perception may detect candidate structure that was not specified in advance. Those observations can subsequently be converted into quantitative definitions, preregistered predictions, held-out tests, and causal interventions.

This suggests a useful division of labour:

**human visual processing detects candidate structure → quantitative analysis tests whether it is reproducible → controlled intervention tests whether the relationship is causal.**

This may be particularly important for REE because exclusively metric-driven investigation risks restricting discovery to phenomena anticipated when the metrics were designed. Visual observation creates another route by which unexpected organism-level phenomena can become scientific questions.

## Epistemic safeguard

The visualisation itself creates a substantial risk of apophenia. Complex pulsing maps will almost inevitably appear meaningful to a human observer.

Consequently:

1. every visual property should have a documented mapping to an actual recorded variable or explicitly defined transformation;
2. raw data must remain authoritative;
3. visual observations should initially be recorded as observations or hypotheses, not findings;
4. discovered patterns should be converted into quantitative measures;
5. where possible, confirmation should occur on unseen lifetimes or environments;
6. causal interpretations should require appropriate intervention or ablation-style testing.

The display should help humans discover questions that the existing measurement framework did not anticipate. It must not become a mechanism for converting visually compelling stories into evidence.

## Possible eventual experimental instrument

The mature system might contain three synchronised layers:

**Fishtank** — observable organism behaviour.

**REE substrate map** — dynamic internal activity, with fixed documented mappings from substrate variables to visual properties.

**Lifetime timeline / analysis layer** — navigation through behavioural events, sleep periods, environmental exposures, internal transitions, and developmental history.

A researcher could notice an unusual substrate pulse accompanying behaviour, rewind to it, inspect the underlying variables, locate similar events elsewhere in that life and other lives, quantify the candidate relationship, and ultimately design an intervention testing it.

Over multiple long-lived REEs this could develop into something reasonably described as **longitudinal behavioural systems neuroscience of an artificial organism**.

It also provides a bridge between REE's existing mechanism-level experimental programme and the emerging organism-level Fishtank work: rather than abandoning mechanistic interpretability when moving to whole-organism behaviour, the internal architecture remains continuously observable while emergent behaviour is studied.

## Core intuition

REE is increasingly becoming sufficiently integrated that observing only its components or only its external behaviour may leave important structure invisible. The opportunity is to observe both simultaneously.

Or, in the originating language:

> “I wonder about applying some brain scan like behavioural analysis.”

The unusual property of REE is that the proposed “scan” need not actually be a scan. The experimental system potentially has direct access to the artificial organism's relevant internal state.

The scientific opportunity is therefore to use that access without losing the advantages of organism-level observation—and to use human visual processing as a high-bandwidth discovery instrument while retaining quantitative replication and intervention as the arbiters of whether the patterns seen are real.
