# Reduced task-switching flexibility in parietal-cingulate and frontal circuits associated with brooding (Singh, Shaw & Becker, 2026, *Cognitive Neurodynamics*)

I want to flag this one as the single closest experimental analogue I found to what MECH-481 actually needs tested, more so than the trait-connectivity paper above (Lydon-Staley et al.), because of one design choice: this study builds an actual task-switching protocol that interleaves cued rumination with working memory and autobiographical memory tasks, rather than relying purely on resting-state connectivity and naturalistic mood sampling. That matters because MECH-481's scenario 5 is not really about whether rumination-prone people have different resting connectivity in general -- it is about what happens at the specific moment a coalition needs to dissolve because task demands have changed, which is exactly the transition this EEG paradigm is built to capture: participants get cued into a rumination-like state, then have to switch INTO a working-memory task, and the question is whether that switch succeeds cleanly.

The authors frame their results using dynamical-systems language -- an "attractor state" -- which is worth taking seriously as a formal vocabulary, not just a metaphor. A stable attractor has two defining properties: convergence over time (the system settles into the state and stays there) and resistance to perturbation (it is hard to knock out of that state once settled). They test both. Convergence: trait brooding was associated with reduced variance in posterior parietal/cingulate EEG signal over the course of rumination trials -- consistent with the system settling into a stable configuration rather than drifting. Resistance to perturbation: when the task switched from cued rumination to working memory (the "perturbation," since rumination and working memory are thought to engage anticorrelated default-mode and central-executive networks), high-trait-rumination individuals showed impaired disengagement -- persistent posterior cingulate/parietal beta power and reduced dlPFC recruitment -- exactly the signature you would expect if the DMN-dominant coalition failed to dissolve on cue.

This is about as clean a biological description of "coalition persistence failure" as I found anywhere in this pull, and it maps almost one-to-one onto MECH-481's own proposed sequence step (6)-(7): Reassess, then Dissolve/sustain/escalate. Where rumination-prone brains seem to fail is specifically at the dissolve step, not at recruitment -- they still enter the rumination-associated state (recruitment is not obviously impaired), the failure is in exiting it when the task changes. That is a genuinely useful empirical anchor for treating Gamma_t (persistence/completion/dissolution conditions) as its own separately-failable mechanism, exactly as REE's guardrails already insist it should be.

The honest caveat: this is EEG, which has real limits here. Posterior cingulate and dlPFC beta power are topographic proxies for "default mode network" and "central executive network" engagement, not a reconstructed whole-brain connectivity graph showing which specific coalition of regions is or is not dissolving. The attractor-state framing, while a reasonable and testable interpretation, is the authors' theoretical gloss on the data rather than something directly measured (no explicit state-space or stability analysis is reported, just the two indirect signatures -- convergence and resistance -- described above). I would not treat this as validating a specific computational model REE could lift wholesale, but as strong conceptual and directional support that dissolution failure is a real, measurable, dissociable-from-recruitment phenomenon, published recently and specifically in the task-switching paradigm REE's own architecture needs.

Confidence: 0.6. Source quality is solid but the venue is newer and less established than the NeuroImage/J Neurosci/TICS entries elsewhere in this pull, and EEG's spatial imprecision tempers confident network attribution. Mapping fidelity is comparatively high, though, because the experimental design (an actual demand-triggered switch, not just resting connectivity) is unusually well matched to what MECH-481's dissolution mechanism specifically claims.
