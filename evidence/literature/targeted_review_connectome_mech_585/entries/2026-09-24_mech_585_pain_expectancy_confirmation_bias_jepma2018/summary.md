# Behavioural and neural evidence for self-reinforcing expectancy effects on pain.

Jepma and colleagues paired cues with painful heat and tracked, trial by trial, what people expected, what they reported, and brain activity. Pain assimilated toward the cue's expectation, and the resulting pain fed back into the next expectation. Updating was biased: when pain was higher than expected after a high-pain cue, or lower after a low-pain cue, people updated more than for errors in the opposite direction. Models confirmed that expectations shape both perception and learning.

This is the main evidence against a reading of MECH-585 as a description of how the brain handles harm prediction errors. If the weight on a harm PE came from a calibrated estimate of the predictor's reliability, errors would not be weighted more when they confirm the prior. What the study shows is the opposite: a self-reinforcing loop in which the predictor is protected from disconfirming error.

It does not falsify MECH-585 as an architectural claim. The claim is normative for the agent, and this study can be read as showing what goes wrong without a calibrated reliability estimate and competence check, which is the claim's own motivation. It also does not measure a reliability estimate directly.

Confidence 0.45, weakens, specifically against citing biology as showing calibrated world-versus-model attribution.
