# Jia et al. (2011) -- common AND dissociable correlates of inductive reasoning components

I have included this entry principally because it cuts against the grain of the others, and a
literature pull that returned four supporting papers and no friction would not be worth much to
governance. Twenty right-handed adults performed three event-related fMRI conditions -- rule
induction, rule induction with application, and a perceptual judgment control -- allowing the
authors to separate two component processes of numerical inductive reasoning: identification
(inducing the rule) and extrapolation (applying it).

The result is deliberately two-directional, and the title says so. A fronto-parietal network was
more specific to identification; a striatal-thalamic network was more specific to extrapolation.
That part supports ARC-113: apprehending a candidate regularity and generalising it recruit
distinguishable machinery, and are therefore not one operation. But left superior parietal lobule
extending into precuneus, and left DLPFC, were *commonly* recruited across both components. The two
stages are not substrate-disjoint. They sit on a shared prefrontal-parietal core with
component-specific networks layered on top.

This is the most direct counter-evidence the search turned up, and it deserves to be stated at full
strength rather than defused. A reading of ARC-113 on which the stages are architecturally separate
modules sharing no machinery is not supported by this dataset. If someone designs the eventual REE
stage ablation around resource separability -- expecting each stage to own its own parameters or
its own pathway -- this paper predicts they will find substantial sharing and may wrongly conclude
the pipeline has collapsed.

The defusing argument exists, and I think it is correct, but it is an argument rather than a
finding, so I have kept the direction as mixed. Shared neural substrate does not by itself refute
functional non-collapsibility: two stages can be computationally distinct while implemented on
overlapping tissue, and ARC-113's stages are functional components of a cycle, not brain regions.
The claim is about whether the stages can be collapsed into one *reasoning module*, not about
whether they can share a parameter. So the honest reading is that this paper constrains the
falsifier design more than it constrains the claim -- it says the discriminating metric must be
functional (does removing this stage produce this specific failure?) and not anatomical or
resource-based.

Two further limits. The design contrasts induction against induction-with-application, so
extrapolation is never observed in isolation; component attribution rests on a subtraction, and a
genuinely non-decomposable process could yield a similar difference map. And the task is numerical
inductive reasoning alone, with no evidence offered that the decomposition transfers to other rule
domains. Confidence 0.58 -- peer-reviewed NeuroImage work posing exactly ARC-113's common-versus-
dissociable question, capped by N=20, 2011-era imaging, a single narrow task, and the subtraction
logic underneath the component claims.
