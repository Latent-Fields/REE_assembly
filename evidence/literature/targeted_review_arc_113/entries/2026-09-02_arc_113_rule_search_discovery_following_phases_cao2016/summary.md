# Cao, Li, Li & Li (2016) -- dissociable medial and lateral PFC roles across rule-learning phases

This study asks a narrower question than ARC-113 but asks it in the right structural key: does rule
learning pass through *temporally ordered phases* with different substrates, or is it one process
that simply gets easier? Twenty-one participants judged whether movements on clock-like card
displays followed a hidden rule, across four runs and twenty rules. The authors then partitioned
each rule's trials into three phases defined from the behavioural record -- rule-search (trials
before discovery, correctly judged incongruent), rule-discovery (the first of three successive
correct congruence judgements), and rule-following (correct congruent judgements thereafter).

The dissociation is a clean crossover. Posterior lateral PFC (BA 6, 9, 46) was more active during
rule-search than during discovery; anterior medial PFC (BA 10) and right anterior cingulate were
more active during discovery than search; and medial PFC was more active during following than
during discovery, while lateral PFC was more active during discovery than following. So as the
system moves from hunting for a regularity, through apprehending one, to applying it, the locus of
activity migrates from lateral to medial. Reaction times track the same ordering, falling from
1421 ms in search to 529 ms in following (F(1,19)=150.3, p<.001), with activations reported under
FWE correction at p<.001.

For ARC-113 this maps onto three consecutive stages: candidate regularity apprehension (search),
the apprehension-to-representation transition (discovery), and prediction plus behavioural
interaction under an acquired rule (following). The value of the entry is that it addresses the
*ordering* commitment specifically. Most of the dissociation literature shows that components differ;
this shows that they occur in sequence and that the substrate shifts systematically along that
sequence. That is the part of ARC-113 that its own notes identify as new -- the stages were largely
registered already, the ordered cycle was not.

The caveat is one the authors raise themselves, and it is a serious one for precisely this use:
the phase effects "were not dissociated from the passage of time". Search, discovery and following
necessarily occur in that order within a block, and search trials are nearly three times longer than
following trials, so an apparent stage dissociation could partly reflect time-on-task or difficulty
rather than distinct stage machinery. The authors also express uncertainty about whether
participants held explicit rule knowledge during the discovery and following phases, which means
the boundary between apprehension and application is behaviourally inferred rather than verified.
And rule-discovery elevated both medial *and* lateral PFC relative to its neighbours rather than
isolating a discovery-specific substrate -- consistent with a transitional blend of adjacent stages
rather than a cleanly separable third one.

Confidence 0.6. This is correlational task-activation fMRI: it shows different phases recruit
different regions, not that removing a phase produces a characteristic failure, which is what
ARC-113's falsifier actually demands. Small single-site N, an acknowledged temporal confound, a
narrow task with twenty hidden rules, and silence on eight of the eleven stages all cap it. I would
treat this as corroborating the ordered-sequence intuition rather than as evidence that could
survive being load-bearing on its own.
