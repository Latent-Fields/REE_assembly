# Sedative-hypnotics and Alzheimer's risk: the best class-level test IMPL-026 has, and it only half-agrees

This is a 2026 systematic review, meta-analysis and meta-regression of sedative-hypnotic exposure against incident Alzheimer's disease, searching PubMed, Embase and CENTRAL from inception to 16 August 2025. It matters to IMPL-026 more than any single-agent study, because IMPL-026 is not a claim about one drug -- it is a *classification*, and a classification is tested by whether its ordering survives contact with pooled data.

The headline is that overall sedative-hypnotic use was associated with higher odds of AD (OR 1.29; 95% CI, 1.10-1.53; I2 = 86.5%). Taken alone that is a tidy confirmation of the table's HIGH-RISK tier. But the authors then do the honest thing and show that when estimates are restricted to hazard ratios the association is "attenuated and not statistically significant" (HR 1.17; 95% CI, 0.87-1.58; I2 = 73.1%). An effect that survives in odds ratios and dissolves in hazard ratios is an effect whose magnitude is partly a property of study design rather than of pharmacology. IMPL-026 currently states its risk directions without that hedge.

The subgroup result is the one that should genuinely trouble us. Short-acting agents showed elevated risk; broad-acting benzodiazepines did not. IMPL-026 orders its rows by *predicted phase disruption* -- benzodiazepines sit in the highest-risk tier because they suppress Phases 1/2 (SWS) and Phase 4 (REM) together (MECH-173 + MECH-174). Half-life is simply not that axis. So the meta-analysis agrees with the table about the sign of the class effect while disagreeing about its internal gradient, and the disagreement is not noise: it is a different explanatory variable doing the work. Either the table's phase-disruption ordering is wrong, or half-life is a proxy for something the table has not represented (perhaps nightly duration of phase suppression, which would be a phase-framed reading of the same finding). I do not think the present evidence chooses between those, and I would rather record the tension than resolve it prematurely.

The limitations are the familiar ones for this literature, and the authors state them: severe between-study heterogeneity, limited power in subgroups, and -- the deep one -- an inability to separate a drug effect from confounding by indication. Insomnia is itself a prodromal feature of Alzheimer's disease. The people who get prescribed hypnotics are disproportionately the people already on the path, which manufactures exactly the association IMPL-026 wants to read mechanistically. The authors call for prospective work to determine whether the association reflects a genuine drug effect or underlying disease process; until that exists, every WORSENS in the table is carrying an unearned causal load.

I have marked this `mixed` rather than `supports`. It supports the existence and direction of a class-level signal, which is not nothing -- IMPL-026 was previously resting on zero literature entries of its own. It does not support the row-by-row confidence with which the table is written, and it supplies a concrete falsifier for one row. That is the most useful thing a first entry for this claim could do.
