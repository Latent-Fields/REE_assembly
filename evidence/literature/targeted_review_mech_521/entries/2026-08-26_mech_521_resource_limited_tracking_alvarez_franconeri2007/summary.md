# A resource pool with a variable realized count -- but contested (Alvarez & Franconeri 2007)

**Source.** Alvarez, G.A. & Franconeri, S.L., *How many objects can you track? Evidence for a resource-limited attentive tracking mechanism*, Journal of Vision 7(13):14.1-10, 2007. [DOI:10.1167/7.13.14](https://doi.org/10.1167/7.13.14), PMID 17997642.

**What the paper does.** Multiple-object tracking (MOT) had, until this paper, largely been read through Pylyshyn's FINST account: a small, fixed number of discrete "index" pointers (roughly 4) that sustain object tracking through motion, giving a hard ceiling on how many objects can be tracked at once. Alvarez and Franconeri parametrically vary target speed alongside target-set size and find a smooth, roughly logarithmic trade-off: at slow speeds, observers can successfully track as many as 8-9 objects; at high speeds, tracking collapses to as few as a single object. They argue this pattern is inconsistent with a hard fixed-slot ceiling (which predicts a flat performance plateau up to the slot limit, then a cliff) and instead reflects a flexibly divisible attentional resource, spread more thinly as the number of targets increases.

**Why it matters for MECH-521.** MECH-521's central claim is that occupancy -- the number of domains a scene is actually, currently carved into by a settling competition -- is a dynamically emergent quantity, distinct from and bounded above by an independently-set capacity. Read through the resource-pool interpretation, this paper is a close structural analog in an established, well-studied literature: a fixed total resource (analogous to capacity) that is divided among targets by task demands (here, speed), producing a realized, successfully-tracked object count (analogous to occupancy) that is not itself fixed and can fall well below the pool's nominal maximum. It demonstrates, in principle, that a capacity-like bound and a dynamically-realized count CAN be genuinely distinct regulators of the same underlying system, rather than the same number simply measured two different ways -- which is exactly the dissociation MECH-521 needs to be coherent.

**Why this entry is marked mixed rather than a clean supports.** The resource-pool interpretation offered by this paper is not the last word in its own literature. A subsequent line of work within the multiple-object-tracking field (object-spacing/crowding accounts associated with Franconeri and colleagues' later papers) argues that the same speed-count trade-off is substantially explained by inter-object spacing and visual crowding constraints rather than by a divisible attentional resource per se -- i.e., the very "resource pool" construct this entry leans on to map onto MECH-521's "capacity" is itself an actively contested explanatory device within cognitive science, not a settled fact about the visual system. This entry should therefore be read as demonstrating that a capacity/occupancy-shaped dissociation is at least a live, seriously-entertained hypothesis in an adjacent, well-studied domain -- not as independently confirming that such a dissociation is the correct account even within that domain.

**Confidence.** 0.5. Source quality is solid (a well-cited, methodologically standard psychophysics paper), discounted for the unresolved theoretical dispute described above. Mapping fidelity is moderate: MOT tracking accuracy is a downstream behavioral measure, not a direct readout of a perceptual settling competition's domain count the way MECH-521 frames occupancy, so the mapping is structural/analogical rather than mechanistic.

**Lit-only verdict, not governance.** Weak-to-moderate support for the general plausibility of a capacity/occupancy dissociation, drawn from an adjacent and only partially-settled literature. Should be read alongside the Fukuda et al. (2010) entry in this same directory, which offers a cleaner (though differently-framed) empirical dissociation between two working-memory parameters, and should not be cited alone as confirmation of MECH-521's two-regulator structure.
