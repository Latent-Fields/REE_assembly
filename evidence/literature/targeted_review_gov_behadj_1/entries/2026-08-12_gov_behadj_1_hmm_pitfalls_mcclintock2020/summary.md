# Uncovering ecological state dynamics with hidden Markov models (McClintock et al., 2020)

This is a comprehensive methodological review, by seven of the field's leading movement-ecology statisticians, of hidden Markov models (HMMs) as the standard rigorous tool for inferring discrete latent behavioral states -- resting, foraging, transiting, and so on -- from continuous animal tracking data. It covers model specification, how to incorporate environmental covariates, how to handle the autocorrelation inherent in movement data, and, importantly for this pull, an unusually candid accounting of the method's own open problems.

The paper is the closest direct match found in this literature pull to GOV-BEHADJ-1's informal proposal of discrete behavioral states (refuge, exploration, food-seeking, acquisition, return) connected by transitions, with reversal frequency, excursion duration, and similar quantities as candidate metrics. HMMs are exactly the established statistical machinery for formalizing that kind of claim, and the review is a natural pointer for what a rigorous version of the source thought's hypothesis-directed AND discovery-oriented channels would actually look like if built out into real infrastructure rather than described qualitatively.

But the review's real value for this pull is its honesty about failure modes, and it supplies material that belongs directly inside GOV-BEHADJ-1's own anti-Goodharting checklist. Three points stand out. First, choosing how many hidden states a model should discover has, in the authors' own words, "no foolproof or automatic way" -- an open methodological problem, not a solved one. Second, when a model's transition structure is left fully free to fit the data, the resulting "states" are entirely data-driven and may not correspond to anything biologically (for REE, functionally) meaningful -- the model will confidently report structure that is a statistical artifact of its own flexibility rather than a real behavioral category. Third, coarse-graining a continuous behavioral space into too few states can mask genuinely distinct behavioral categories, while unaddressed violations of the model's independence assumptions can further distort what looks like a clean state-transition inference.

Read together, these three points are a formal, quantitative-methods-literature restatement of exactly the Goodharting risk the source thought raises informally for hand-picked scalar metrics ("if smoothness becomes number of turns, the experiment quietly stops asking about coherence and starts being about turn count"). The lesson generalizes: the risk does not disappear when you replace a hand-picked metric with a formal unsupervised discovery model. It relocates -- from "which metric did I choose to report" to "how many latent states did I let the model discover, and did I validate that they mean anything." Any REE implementation of GOV-BEHADJ-1's "Discover" step using HMM-family models (or the MoSeq-style AR-HMM approach reviewed in a separate entry in this pull) would need its own explicit safeguard against this: for instance, cross-checking discovered states against independently interpretable internal-policy signals, which REE -- unlike a wild animal under GPS tracking -- can in principle access directly, an advantage this review's own domain does not have.
