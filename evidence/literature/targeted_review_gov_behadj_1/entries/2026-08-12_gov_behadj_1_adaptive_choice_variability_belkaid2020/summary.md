# Mice adaptively generate choice variability in a deterministic task (Belkaid et al., 2020)

The authors designed a decision-tree choice task with a fully deterministic reward rule: in principle, a mouse that discovered and repeated the single correct sequence of choices could achieve 100% reward. Instead, mice progressively increased the variability of their choice sequences across training sessions -- measured formally via Lempel-Ziv complexity -- and their success rate plateaued around 75%, matching what a near-random selection strategy would achieve, rather than continuing to converge on the deterministic optimum. Computational modeling showed this was best explained by mice tuning an internal randomness parameter rather than following (or failing to follow) a memorized sequence. Critically, the authors then ran a discriminating perturbation: after training, they switched the task to genuinely probabilistic (75%) rewards. If the mice had learned a specific rewarded sequence, this switch should have changed their behavior once the sequence stopped reliably paying off; if they had instead learned a general randomness-generation strategy already well-suited to reward uncertainty, the switch should produce no behavioral change. The mice did not change their behavior -- direct evidence that the variability was an actively regulated strategy, not incidental noise or unlearned inconsistency, and Markov-order analysis of their choice sequences confirmed no further exploitable sequential structure remained.

This paper is close to a worked, empirically validated demonstration of the exact methodological point GOV-BEHADJ-1's source thought raises in its "variability must not automatically be classified as noise" section. The thought poses the question as: not "did behaviour become less random?" but "did randomness become differently organised?" Belkaid et al. answer a structurally identical question for a real organism, with a rigorous design built specifically to distinguish adaptive regulation from passive noise -- a reward-structure manipulation that should, on a naive "more randomness is worse" account, have driven variability toward zero, paired with a discriminating switch-test perturbation that cleanly separates "learned a specific sequence" from "learned a randomness strategy."

The paper is also a strong worked example of GOV-BEHADJ-1's "Perturb" step done well. Rather than adding another descriptive metric of variability, the authors constructed a manipulation (the probabilistic-reward switch) precisely designed to make two competing explanations of the same observed behavior -- memorized sequence versus regulated randomness strategy -- predict different outcomes, and then let the data adjudicate. That is a close-to-textbook instance of the source thought's own principle: "a good behavioural experiment changes the world in a way that causes competing explanations to make different predictions."
