# Genetics of mouse behavior: interactions with laboratory environment (Crabbe, Wahlsten & Dudek, 1999)

Three independent laboratories tested the same panel of inbred mouse strains (plus one null mutant) on six standard behavioral assays -- open field activity, elevated plus maze, and others -- while deliberately equating apparatus, protocol, handler training, and as many environmental variables as feasible. The strains differed markedly on every behavior, so a real genotype effect existed. But the study's headline finding was the second result: the SIZE of several of those genotype effects depended systematically on which lab ran the test. A mutation that looked strongly behaviorally consequential in one lab looked weaker, or differently patterned, in another, despite the experimenters' best efforts to eliminate exactly that source of variation.

This paper is one of the most direct historical instances of the failure mode GOV-BEHADJ-1's source thought explicitly asked the literature review to find: an "apparently meaningful behaviour" -- here, a clean genotype-behavior relationship -- that partially came apart under a more rigorous, multi-context replication than the one used to first characterize it. It matters for REE Assembly because the natural first reaction to a puzzling cross-context result is to look for a subtle procedural difference and try to eliminate it; Crabbe et al.'s finding is that even when the investigators DID eliminate every procedural difference they could identify, systematic context-dependence remained. Standardization is necessary, not sufficient.

The REE-specific translation is closer to home than it might first appear. REE Assembly already has its own documented, confirmed instance of exactly this class of problem: `torch.multinomial` returns a different sampled action from a bit-identical probability tensor at the same seed on darwin-arm64/torch 2.10.0 versus linux-x86_64/torch 2.11.0+cpu (see the project memory `reference-cross-machine-class-contract-divergence`). That is a machine-class analogue of Crabbe et al.'s "lab effect": the same underlying mechanism (a genotype; a policy) produces a behaviorally different outcome depending on unmeasured context (a physical laboratory; a hardware/library stack) that was not part of the intended experimental manipulation. GOV-BEHADJ-1's proposed workflow -- particularly its "Replicate" and "Generalise" steps -- would, if formalized, need to treat machine class and substrate version as candidate confounds worth deliberately varying, not just seeds and environment layouts, given this precedent.

The mapping caveat is real and should not be minimized: this is a wet-lab, physical-environment confound, and the specific mechanisms behind a "lab effect" (handler scent, room acoustics, subtle equipment wear, time-of-day) have no literal analogue in a deterministic or pseudo-random computational substrate. What transfers is the structural lesson, not a specific technique to adopt. Confidence is set at 0.65 -- high source quality (a landmark, still-cited Science paper with an unusually strong multi-site design for 1999) offset by only moderate mapping fidelity, since the paper's own domain is wet-lab genetics rather than computational ethology or simulation. It is included here specifically because the source thought asked for historical cases where controls revealed a behavioral effect was less clean than it first appeared, and this is one of the most citable examples in the behavioral sciences literature.
