# Resulaj, Kiani, Wolpert & Shadlen 2009 — Changes of mind in decision-making

This is the entry that decides the whole pull, so it is worth being careful about what it does and does not say. Resulaj and colleagues had people decide about noisy motion by reaching to a target. The trick is that no new sensory information arrives once the reach has begun — and yet, on a reliable fraction of trials, the hand changes course mid-flight. The decision was made, the motor program was launched, and then it was revised. Their model is a bounded-accumulation account in which the evidence still travelling through the processing pipeline at the instant of commitment keeps accumulating, and can cross a second bound that either reverses or reaffirms the initial choice. The model captures both how often minds change and the way that frequency tracks task difficulty and whether the first decision was right or wrong.

The reason this matters for MECH-090 is precise. V3-EXQ-592f confirmed that REE's beta-gate commitment latch is *admission-only*: once beta is elevated, the readiness predicates that guarded entry have no authority to release. The implicit question the autopsy left open was whether a committed program *should* be releasable on degraded readiness at all, or whether admission-only is the biologically correct commitment (option (iii)/B3c). Resulaj answers that question directly and in the negative. Biological commitment is not a one-way latch. Even after a motor program is initiated, continued internal evidence — exactly the kind of "is this still the decisive winner?" signal that REE encodes as the R-c `score_margin` — can reverse the action online. So the 592f gap is a genuine architectural incompleteness, not a mis-routed expectation. The four existing latch-release pathways (positive completion, threat escalation, schema invalidation, rule-completion) do not include the one this literature says must exist: a maintenance-time, decisiveness-driven revision.

There is an equally important caveat, and it is the one that should discipline how any fix is built. The reversal here is *graded and evidence-driven* — it is the continuation of a bounded accumulation, not the tripping of a flag. This converges with the warning already on record from the goal-disengagement pull (Brandstätter, Klinger): the trigger for abandoning an in-progress commitment is a contested, accumulating process, not a costless single-timestep switch. A naïve implementation of autopsy option (a) — "release when nav_competence has been below floor for K consecutive ticks" — would discard exactly the continued-accumulation dynamics that Resulaj shows are the actual mechanism. The honest design reading is that the maintenance-time release REE is missing should be a *graded decisiveness-accumulation* coupling at the beta-gate level, not a Schmitt trigger.

The failure signature cuts both ways, which is why I have logged confidence at 0.82 rather than higher. Changes of mind improve accuracy when the initial decision was wrong, but they can also reverse a correct-but-hard decision. A maintenance-time release that fires on transient low decisiveness will sometimes abort a correct commitment that merely passed through a hard patch — the premature-disengagement pole. That is not an argument against the mechanism; it is an argument that the mechanism needs the same reengagement/hysteresis discipline the decision literature and the disengagement literature both demand.

Confidence reasoning: Nature, foundational, heavily replicated, with a mechanistic model rather than a correlation — source quality is high (0.92). Mapping fidelity is high (0.80) because the structural primitive the paper demonstrates (post-commitment evidence can reverse an initiated action) *is* the 592f gap, not merely adjacent to it. Transfer risk (0.35) reflects the move from a sub-second perceptual reach with a single post-decision bound to REE's multi-tick commitment latch — the primitive transfers cleanly, the bound geometry and timescale do not.
