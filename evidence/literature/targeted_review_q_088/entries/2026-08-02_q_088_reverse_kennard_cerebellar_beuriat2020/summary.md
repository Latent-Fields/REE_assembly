# Cerebellar Lesions at a Young Age Predict Poorer Long-Term Recovery (Beuriat et al., 2020)

**Claim tested:** Q-088 -- does developmental disruption of a mechanism dissociate from mature-state lesion, and if so, in which direction?

## What the study found

Beuriat and colleagues followed 45 survivors of posterior-fossa (cerebellar) tumours, split into three bands by age at surgery (<=7, 7-13, >13 years) and matched on tumour type, size and location. Using a general linear model that controlled for tumour volume, radiotherapy, preservation of the deep cerebellar nuclei and the delay between surgery and assessment, they found that **younger age at cerebellar damage independently predicted worse** long-term outcome across every domain measured -- daily functioning (quality of life, performance status), motor functioning (ataxia rating, Purdue pegboard) and cognition (full-scale IQ). They read this as evidence for an early critical period during which the cerebellar "learning machine" is essential, and explicitly frame it against the folk "Kennard Principle" that younger brains recover better: here the young brain does *worse*.

## Why it matters for Q-088, especially the "or the reverse" clause

Q-088 does not merely ask whether develop-without and mature-lesion protocols dissociate; it flags that the dissociation can run either way -- "mature lesion says tolerable, redevelopment says not, *or the reverse*." Much of the intuition behind letting the cheap mature lesion stand is an unstated monotonicity assumption: that a mature lesion bounds the developmental cost, so if the mature system tolerates removal, a developing one will too. This study is a clean human counterexample. For a critical-period structure, disrupting it *during* development was more damaging than disrupting it later. Translate age-at-lesion to the develop-without-versus-mature-remove axis and the lesson is direct: the mature-lesion arm can *under*-estimate the true cost of a developmentally-necessary mechanism, which is exactly the circumstance in which the expensive develop-without protocol earns its place. A REE probe that reasoned "mature lesion is tolerable, therefore develop-without will be at least as tolerable" would be systematically wrong for this class of mechanism.

## Caveats, kept in front

The analogy is partial in an important way: this is early *lesion of a developing structure*, not *development without a mechanism from the outset*. Those differ -- an organ damaged mid-construction is not the same as one built without a component. It is also observational human clinical data with a modest sample and unavoidable residual confounding; cerebellar-tumour surgery drags in radiotherapy and collateral tissue loss that a clean REE ablation would not have, and indeed the model shows outcome depended on deep-nuclei preservation and radiotherapy as much as on age. That last point is itself a design lesson for Q-088: a develop-vs-mature dissociation can be manufactured or masked by uncontrolled covariates, so the probe must hold the substrate and curriculum fixed across the two arms and vary only the presence/timing of the mechanism.

## Confidence

0.55. I lean on it for one specific, decision-relevant thing -- the *direction* of dissociation is not fixed, and can invert the naive bound -- rather than for magnitudes. Source quality is moderate (careful confounder control, but observational and small), and the mapping is a partial one. It complements the genetic and artificial-network entries by adding the human, whole-system case where the developmental protocol returns the harsher verdict.
