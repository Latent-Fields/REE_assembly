# Larger communities create more systematic languages (Raviv, Meyer & Lev-Ari, Proc. R. Soc. B, 2019)

## Why this entry exists

The `2026-09-20_arc_099_missing_constraints_galke2022` entry in this directory ended by saying that before anyone rewrites the V6-entry gate on the strength of a four-page workshop paper, the primary group-size work it leans on should be pulled and read directly. This is that paper, and reading it changes what the workshop paper can be used for.

## What the paper did

144 Dutch-speaking adults were split into 24 fully connected microsocieties -- twelve of four members, twelve of eight -- and spent between four and a half and five and a quarter hours each inventing a language from nothing. They could type only a hyphen, five vowels and ten consonants; no Dutch, with an experimenter in the room enforcing it. In each of sixteen rounds, participants were paired off, and within a round the pair alternated between producing a label for a target scene and guessing which of eight scenes their partner meant. Partners were exchanged at the start of every round. The scenes varied along three dimensions -- shape, angle of motion, and an idiosyncratic fill pattern -- so the meaning space *afforded* a compositional carve-up along shape and angle but also permitted a purely holistic strategy of naming each scene by its fill pattern. The meaning space grew from eight scenes to twenty-three over the first six rounds.

The design's discipline is the reason it is worth reading. In the real world, community size travels with network sparsity, geographic spread, contact with outsiders, and proportion of adult second-language learners, and most of the field had assumed one of those, not size, was doing the work. Here all of them are held constant. Total interaction volume is equalised across conditions. What differs between a four-person and an eight-person group is how many distinct people you talk to and, consequently, how little history you share with each of them.

## What it found

Linguistic structure -- the correlation between meaning distances and string distances in a participant's lexicon -- rose over rounds (beta = 4.55, p < 0.0001), and rose faster in larger groups (condition x round beta = 1.92, s.e. = 0.63, t = 3.06, p = 0.004). Final languages in larger groups were significantly more structured (beta = 0.11, s.e. = 0.04, t = 2.93, p = 0.006). Larger groups also varied far less between themselves (beta = -0.015, p = 0.0002): every large group trended the same way, while some small groups developed elegant two-axis directional grammars and others never got past holistic labels.

Two things did *not* differ. Communicative success was statistically indistinguishable (p = 0.078 over time, p = 0.083 at the end), and so was convergence (p = 0.18) -- the large groups reached the same level of shared lexicon despite having twice as many people to agree with and half the shared history with each.

Then the mediation. The authors measured input variability directly (how many distinct label variants were circulating for each scene) and shared history (how many times each pair had met). Taken separately, both predicted the next round's change in structure. Entered together, only input variability survived: beta = 0.011, p = 0.012, against shared history at beta = -0.008, p = 0.17.

## What this does to ARC-099

Three things, and the third is the one that matters.

**The group-size effect is robust and causal.** The first of the three questions this pull was chartered to answer gets a clean yes. It is not a correlational typological finding dressed up; it is a manipulation with the usual confounds stripped out, adequately powered at 24 independent groups, with open data. Galke et al. 2022 were entitled to lean on it.

**But this paper cannot license ARC-099's partner-variation item, because partner variation is a CONSTANT here, not a manipulation.** Every group in both conditions exchanged partners every round and alternated producer/guesser roles within every round. So were the two constraints Galke et al. say are missing from the inventory: role alternation is present in both conditions and never varied. This is worth stating plainly, because it is easy to read the workshop paper's citation chain as though the primary study had *tested* role alternation. It did not. It built role alternation into the paradigm and then varied something else. The same is true of partner variation: what varied was the NUMBER of partners, not whether partners varied at all.

**And the mediation relocates the condition.** If input variability is the mechanism, then the checkable V6-entry precondition is not "the ecology contains more than one partner" but something closer to "the ecology delivers variability in the signals an agent receives for a given referent, at a rate the agent cannot simply memorise". Those come apart. An ecology with eight partners whose policies have converged delivers no input variability at all, satisfies ARC-099's partner-variation item on its face, and should be expected to produce nothing. That is the same satisfiable-but-inert failure the Galke entry worried about, reached by a different route -- and this one is visible in the primary data rather than asserted.

There is a fourth observation the inventory should probably absorb. The expanding meaning space is not incidental scenery; the authors describe it as deliberately implemented "in order to introduce a pressure for developing structured and predictable languages". ARC-099's fourteen items include coordination pressure but nothing about the meaning space growing faster than an agent can individuate. In this experiment that pressure is doing visible work, and it has no counterpart in the contract.

## On bounded memory

The chip that commissioned this pull asked whether the primary sources support bounded memory as a causal constraint. This one does not, and is mildly awkward for it: the experimenter explicitly "stressed that this was not a memory game". The only memory argument in the paper is in the discussion, where the authors connect their result to the psycholinguistic finding that "participants typically don't generalize over variants when they are able to memorize all of them individually, but do generalize when there are too many variants to remember". That is a plausible and attractive mechanism -- it would explain why input variability rather than partner count is the mediator -- but here it is an interpretation of someone else's literature, not a manipulation in this experiment. Recorded as suggestive, not established.

## Limitations

Two group sizes, four and eight. No monotonicity, no threshold, no evidence about behaviour at the scale a V6 ecology would run. The effect on final structure is modest in absolute terms (0.11 in raw correlation units) and took a substantial experiment to resolve, which should temper expectations about how visible a group-size signal would be in a small V6 probe. And the participants are adults with intact language faculties inventing a *new* system -- so the study is evidence about what ecology conditions elicit structure from agents that already satisfy ARC-099's substrate inventory, and is silent on whether that inventory is complete. That silence is important: it means this paper cannot adjudicate the question Galke et al. raised, only sharpen it.

## Confidence

0.78, `mixed`. Source quality 0.88, mapping fidelity 0.72, transfer risk 0.42. Mixed rather than supports because the same analysis that confirms the effect also says ARC-099 has the condition at the wrong grain. The transfer-risk number is the honest one to look at: the inference REE needs from this paper -- that a social property separating human microsocieties will separate configurations of an artificial ecology -- is exactly the inference the neural-agent literature has so far failed to make go through, which is the subject of the Rita 2022 entry alongside this one.
