# Behrens et al. (2018) -- the case for one map-maker

## What the paper argues

This review makes the strongest available case for Q-083's domain-general arm. Its thesis is that the map-like representations we found first in spatial navigation are not a spatial specialisation at all -- they are what a general knowledge-organising mechanism looks like when you happen to point it at space. The authors assemble the parallels: grid-like coding in continuous conceptual spaces, hippocampal coding of social hierarchies, structure that transfers across task spaces, and artificial agents that develop brain-like map representations when given the right learning principles.

## Why it belongs against Q-083

Q-083 registers the domain-agnosticism of rule apprehension as the genuine architectural fork and notes it is untested. This paper is the position statement for one arm of it, from a group with the empirical and modelling credentials to make it stick. Its claim is exactly REE's second option: not N apprehenders, but one that inherits domain structure from its inputs.

It also lands on REE's own architecture. V3's hippocampal machinery is already relational rather than Cartesian -- organised around action objects and candidate trajectories. If Behrens and colleagues are right, that machinery is the natural home of a domain-general apprehender, and the domain-agnosticism REE has assumed on grounds of parsimony has independent biological support. That is a better position than assuming it because it is simpler to build.

## Where I think the argument is thinner than it reads

Two things temper this, and they push in the same direction.

First, the non-spatial domains carrying most of the weight are the ones that most resemble space: continuous concept spaces with a metric, dominance hierarchies with an order, structured task spaces with a transition graph. That is a real generalisation beyond navigation, but it is generalisation across domains that share the property the mechanism was built to exploit. Q-083's list includes ethical and social regularity apprehension, and the hardest version of the question is whether a domain with no obvious metric gets organised the same way. The evidence thins exactly there.

Second, and more fundamentally, there is a format-versus-process gap. Showing that the same representational format appears across several domains is weaker than showing that one apprehension process produced all of them. Parallel domain-specific learners converging on the same code would look identical in the data reviewed here. Q-083 asks about the process -- whether a rule apprehended in one domain accelerates apprehension in another -- and this paper does not test that.

I note the honest tension with the other two entries in this folder. Frost et al. and Hobeika et al. both land on a hybrid; this paper lands closer to the pure domain-general arm. I do not think that is a contradiction so much as a difference in what each is measuring -- format and principle for Behrens, performance and localisation for the other two -- but I would rather record the tension than smooth it.

## Confidence

0.66. Strong source, argued rather than demonstrated, and supporting the generality of the representational format more securely than the generality of the apprehension process that Q-083 actually asks about. Q-083 stays substrate-blocked: V3 has one domain, and no amount of literature makes a transfer test runnable in it.
