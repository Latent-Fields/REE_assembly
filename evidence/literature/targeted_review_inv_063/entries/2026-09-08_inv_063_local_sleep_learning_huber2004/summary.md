# Local sleep and learning (Huber, Ghilardi, Massimini & Tononi, 2004)

## What the paper did

Huber and colleagues had healthy adults perform a visuomotor rotation-adaptation task -- reaching to
targets under a systematically rotated cursor mapping -- and recorded whole-night sleep with
256-channel high-density EEG, comparing it against both a baseline night and a night following a
kinematically matched motor control task that involved the same movements without the adaptation
demand. The question was whether sleep's homeostatic slow-wave response is a global quantity, set
simply by how long the organism has been awake, or whether it has a local component that tracks
where in the cortex plastic change actually occurred.

It has a local component. After learning, NREM slow wave activity increased specifically over right
parietal and sensorimotor regions -- the areas independently known to be recruited by this task --
and not globally. The control task, matched for movement, did not produce the increase. Crucially
for us, the size of each subject's local SWA increase correlated with how much their task
performance improved when they were retested after sleep.

## How this maps onto INV-063

INV-063 asserts a chain: waking entropy intake produces the material for offline processing, and
when intake falls the offline functions are starved. This paper is the cleanest human evidence for
the first link in that chain -- that what the waking period contained, and not merely how long it
was, determines what the following sleep does and how much the organism gains from it. That is the
premise the whole claim stands on, and it is worth recording that it is well established rather than
assumed.

But I want to be exact about what this does not do, because the temptation to over-read it is
strong. The dependent variable here is slow wave activity: a measure of how much offline processing
happens, i.e. the *budget*. INV-063's distinctive assertion is about what happens to offline
*function* when the budget is held fixed and only intake varies. Huber et al. never pin the budget --
they could not, and would not have wanted to; the moving budget is their finding. So this paper is
equally consistent with INV-050 (intake drives offline quantity, which drives benefit) as it is with
INV-063 (intake drives offline function directly), and the two are exactly what the claim's P2 gate
exists to separate. Read honestly, it supports the family and not the member.

There is a second thing the paper says that cuts slightly against the claim's specific wording. The
SWA--performance relation is reported as a graded correlation across subjects. Twenty years of this
literature has looked hard at experience-dependent offline processing and has consistently described
proportional relations, not thresholds. Nobody reports a floor below which the benefit collapses. If
we take that seriously -- and I think we should -- INV-063's pre-registered F2 outcome (monotone
degradation with no knee) is the outcome the biology would predict, and the word "minimum" in the
claim title is the part carrying the real risk.

## Confidence

0.62. The source quality is about as high as this literature offers: a within-subject design with a
matched control, published in Nature, replicated and extended since. What holds the number down is
mapping fidelity (0.5) rather than any doubt about the finding. The paper answers "does waking
experience shape offline processing?" with a clear yes, and INV-063 is asking a narrower and harder
question -- "does offline FUNCTION have a FLOOR in intake, with quantity controlled?" -- that this
design is structurally unable to address. I would cite it in support of the claim's premise and
would not cite it in support of the claim's threshold.
