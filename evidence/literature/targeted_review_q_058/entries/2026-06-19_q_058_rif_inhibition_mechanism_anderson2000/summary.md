# Retrieval-Induced Forgetting: Evidence for a Recall-Specific Mechanism (Anderson, Bjork & Bjork 2000)

According to PubMed ([DOI 10.3758/bf03214366](https://doi.org/10.3758/bf03214366)), this is one of the foundational papers establishing that *competition* and *inhibition* are not the same thing in memory — exactly the distinction Q-058's second sub-question turns on. Subjects studied six members from each of eight taxonomic categories. In the competitive-practice condition they practised recalling some members using category-stem cues ("fruit or__"); in the non-competitive condition they were re-exposed to the same members for the same number of repetitions but recalled the category name instead ("fr__ orange"). Both conditions strengthened the practised items equally. But only the *competitive* recall condition impaired later recall of the unpractised members. The authors conclude that retrieval-induced forgetting is not driven by the strengthening of practised items (which would predict equal forgetting in both conditions) but by an inhibitory process specific to the situation of recall.

The relevance to Q-058 is the clean dissociation it draws. REE's existing competitive machinery is largely relative-strength normalisation — softmax/WTA over candidates (MECH-090/ARC-030/MECH-254). This study shows that strength-based competition and active inhibition produce *different* downstream consequences: mere strengthening of a winner did not suppress its competitors, but actively retrieving against them did. That is direct behavioural support for the idea that a true inhibitory de-commit is mechanistically distinct from a softmax reweighting — which is the heart of Q-058's question about whether some couplings need an explicit (signed, inhibitory) edge rather than the local softmax, and bears on the MECH-090/MECH-105 decommitment distinction.

The two failure signatures I recorded follow from this. First, and most useful for REE: an architecture that models competition *purely* as relative-strength normalisation will systematically miss the inhibitory suppression that only appears when the loser is actively retrieved against. If REE is seeing non-conversion or persistence problems, "the softmax isn't enough; you may need an explicit suppressive edge" is a concrete hypothesis this paper licenses. Second, the effect is *recall-specific* — engaged by the act of retrieval — which is a small but real piece of evidence for Q-058's third sub-question that competition modes are not interchangeable across waking action versus passive or offline regimes.

I have set evidence_direction to **supports** with confidence 0.6. The paradigm is foundational and heavily replicated (which is why I include it as the inhibition-vs-competition anchor), but it is human verbal-memory behaviour, the inhibition is *inferred* from recall accuracy rather than measured at the circuit level, and the mapping to REE inter-field couplings is a functional analogy. It makes the conceptual case that inhibition ≠ competition; it does not, on its own, tell REE which specific couplings need the signed edge. The meta-analysis companion entry (Murayama et al. 2014) is what calibrates how strong that case actually is.
