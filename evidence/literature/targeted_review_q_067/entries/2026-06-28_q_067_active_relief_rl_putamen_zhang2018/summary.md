# The control of tonic pain by active relief learning

**Source:** Zhang, Mano, Lee, Yoshida, Kawato, Robbins & Seymour (2018), *eLife*. [DOI: 10.7554/eLife.31949](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.31949)
**Claim tested:** Q-067 (relief/safety decomposition of the escape-affordance bridge)
**Evidence direction:** supports · **Confidence:** 0.72

According to PubMed, Seymour's group set out to show how the brain learns to *get itself out of* ongoing pain — not how it predicts the absence of threat, but how it learns which actions terminate an aversive state that is already present. Across two human experiments (a static and a dynamic escape paradigm under tonic pain), they modelled active relief-seeking as a reinforcement-learning process and found the signatures you would expect if relief is genuinely a learned, error-driven quantity: relief prediction errors localised to dorsal putamen, and an associability (uncertainty) signal in pregenual anterior cingulate cortex that did double duty — setting the learning rate for relief and parametrically turning the level of ongoing tonic pain up or down. Relief, in other words, is not a fixed reflex; it is an active RL term with a trainable rate gated by uncertainty.

For Q-067 this is the cleanest available template for one specific arm of the decomposition: the relief component, which REE proposes to instantiate as MECH-375 Q_relief — action-contingent escape credit. The mapping is unusually direct. The dorsal-putamen prediction-error signal is the trainable relief-credit term that REE would layer on top of its fixed first-action-class escape bridge; the pgACC associability signal is the uncertainty-modulated learning rate on that term. The deeper point the paper makes — that effective escape requires *learning* about relief rather than executing a static affordance — is precisely the argument for why a fixed bridge can be insufficient and why a trainable Q_relief successor is the right repair. If relief were a static affordance, you would not see an error signal or an uncertainty-gated rate; you would see a fixed response. The brain does the former.

The honest limitation is symmetric with the strength. This study isolates the relief / active-escape arm and contains no safety predictor — no cue that signals prospective threat-absence, no conditioned-inhibition test. So it strengthens the case that the *relief* half of Q-067's decomposition is a real, learnable RL term, but it cannot tell us whether relief alone closes the gap when the fixed bridge fails, nor whether the missing structure is instead the safety predictor (MECH-376 P_safety). It speaks to "is the relief component genuinely trainable and worth building?" (yes, with high fidelity) rather than to Q-067's actual discriminator ("when the bridge is insufficient, which half is missing?").

I have set confidence at 0.72. Source quality is high — eLife, converging behavioural, physiological and imaging evidence, an explicit and fit computational model. Mapping fidelity is strong for the Q_relief arm but does not extend to the relief-vs-safety contrast, which is the governance question. Transfer risk is moderate: the domain is human tonic-pain offset, and REE's escape bridge is a committed navigational/action escape, so the action-contingency is engaged in spirit (active escape-seeking) but not in exact form. Read alongside Mohammadi et al. (2014), which establishes that relief and safety are *separable*, this paper establishes that the relief arm specifically is *learnable by RL* — together they license building Q_relief and P_safety as distinct trainable successors, while leaving the "train which one first" adjudication to the V3-EXQ-603i bridge experiment.
