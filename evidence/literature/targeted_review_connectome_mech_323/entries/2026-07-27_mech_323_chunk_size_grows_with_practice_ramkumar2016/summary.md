# Ramkumar, Acuna, Berniker, Grafton, Turner & Kording 2016 -- Chunking as the result of an efficiency computation trade-off

MECH-323 carries a chunk-size budget of 2-5 elements per level, inherited from the ARC-071 R5 verdict and resting on Sakai 2003. Sakai supports the range at motor grain; the question this pull was asked to settle is whether anything supports it as a stable architectural constant. Ramkumar et al. give the clearest answer available, and it is no -- not because the range is wrong early on, but because chunk size is not the kind of thing that stays put.

The argument is a computational one with a longitudinal test behind it. Planning a movement is an optimal control problem whose complexity grows exponentially with horizon; breaking a compound movement into chunks planned over shorter horizons reduces that cost, at the price of achievable efficiency. The prediction is a schedule rather than a constant: start with many short chunks because compute is the binding constraint, then merge them as practice lowers the impediments to more complex computation. Two macaques learning a ten-element sequential reaching task over months do exactly this. "The length of individual chunks increases. As chunks become longer over the course of learning, movements are optimized over increasingly longer horizons."

For MECH-323 that reframes the parameter's type. The budget is currently a scalar cap; the paper says it is the output of an optimisation whose inputs are the agent's compute budget and its accumulated practice. Two consequences follow that a fixed 2-5 cannot express. First, a hard lifetime cap at 5 forbids the most robust longitudinal signature in this literature, so an REE agent would be unable to reproduce the observed learning trajectory even with every other trigger condition correct. Second, two agents with different rollout budgets should settle at different chunk sizes -- which means a single global maximum is the wrong SHAPE of parameter, not merely the wrong value. The natural fix is to keep 2-5 as the initial budget, expose the ceiling as growable, and tie it to the deliberation cost that MECH-321 exists to relieve.

That last point is the incidental find I would flag hardest. MECH-321's depth cap and MECH-323's size budget are currently coupled by fiat -- the `implementation_note` says the cap "mirrors ARC-071's `chunk_max_depth` so the inverse operations stay symmetric". Ramkumar et al. supply a principled reason they should be related: both are settings on one compute-versus-efficiency trade-off, decomposition buying tractability and composition buying efficiency. That is a much better warrant for coupling them than symmetry-for-its-own-sake, and it predicts the two parameters should move together in a particular direction as the agent's compute budget changes, which is testable.

There is no upper bound in the paper, so it cannot be used to justify replacing 5 with any other number. What bounds chunk growth empirically is diminishing returns -- chunk structures stay at roughly 50-60% of the complexity of executing the sequence as a single unit, and the monkeys never collapse the whole sequence into one chunk. An accumulator that grows chunks monotonically with no returns-based brake would eventually do exactly that, which this trade-off rules out.

Marked `weakens`, scoped narrowly to the fixed-constant reading of the budget rather than to small-chunks-early, at confidence 0.68. Source quality is high (Nature Communications, longitudinal primate, explicit generative model). Mapping fidelity is held at 0.60 because this is macaque reaching kinematics -- ten center-out and return movements in a five-second trial -- so the chunk grain is motor, the same transfer gap the existing Sakai entry already flagged for ARC-071. Its force here is structural rather than numeric, which is the part I would expect to transfer: the specific lengths do not port to policy primitives, but the finding that the quantity is dynamic and compute-bound is a claim about the parameter's shape, and shape travels further than magnitude.
