# Shenhav & Greene (2010) -- "Moral judgments recruit domain-general valuation mechanisms to integrate representations of probability and magnitude"

## What the paper did

Shenhav and Greene set out to test whether moral decision making -- specifically, judging whether it is acceptable to sacrifice one life to save a larger group -- relies on the same neural machinery as ordinary economic decision making, or on dedicated moral-specific circuitry. They designed moral dilemmas that were computationally isomorphic to economic decisions: subjects evaluated trade-offs involving variable magnitude (how many lives saved) and variable probability (chance of the sacrifice actually preventing the deaths), the same two quantities that structure standard expected-value decisions in behavioral economics.

## Key findings relevant to ARC-121

The result closely tracked the functional architecture of ordinary reward valuation. Ventromedial prefrontal cortex and ventral striatum -- the identical regions identified as the domain-general "valuation system" in Bartra, McGuire & Kable's later meta-analysis (this same directory) -- were specifically sensitive to the "expected moral value" of an action: the expected number of lives lost or saved. Separately, right anterior insula tracked outcome probability specifically, mirroring probability-coding regions found in economic decision studies. In other words, the moral decision was decomposed and computed using the SAME functional division of labor the brain uses for ordinary material-reward decisions, not a separate, dedicated moral evaluation system.

## Translation to REE

This is close to a direct test of ARC-121's specific claim. ARC-121 names harm/ethics evaluation explicitly as one of the mechanisms that should converge on REE's shared epistemic-state object rather than maintaining isolated local state. Shenhav & Greene's finding -- that a genuinely moral, life-and-death judgment recruits the same vmPFC/ventral-striatum valuation hub used for economic decisions, with the same computational decomposition into magnitude and probability -- is strong, direct human-neuroscience support for exactly this claim. It suggests harm/ethics evaluation is not architecturally walled off from the rest of REE's evaluative machinery; it is a consumer of the same shared valuation-adjacent state.

## Limitations and caveats

The moral dilemmas studied here were deliberately engineered to be structurally isomorphic to economic decisions -- explicit probabilities, explicit magnitudes, a forced-choice utilitarian framing. This is close to the best-case design for finding shared valuation circuitry, and the result may not generalize to more intuitive, rapid, less quantified moral reactions (an immediate visceral response to witnessing a harmful act, with no probability/magnitude structure to compute over), which dual-process accounts of moral cognition suggest may lean more heavily on amygdala-dominant, less deliberative circuitry. The authors themselves also frame the finding as moral cognition REPURPOSING an evolutionarily older, self-interested valuation substrate rather than the substrate being a neutral, general-purpose computation from the start -- a nuance worth keeping alongside ARC-121's framing.

## Confidence reasoning

Strong, well-cited, directly relevant empirical evidence -- this is about as close as the current literature gets to a head-on test of whether harm/ethics evaluation shares REE's proposed common epistemic-state object. Mapping fidelity is high because the finding's shape (same regions, same functional decomposition as ordinary valuation) is exactly what ARC-121's claim predicts; transfer risk is kept moderate because the paradigm's design likely favors finding shared-circuitry results.
