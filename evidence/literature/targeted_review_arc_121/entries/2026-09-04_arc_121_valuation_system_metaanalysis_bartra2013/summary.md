# Bartra, McGuire & Kable (2013) -- "The valuation system: a coordinate-based meta-analysis of BOLD fMRI experiments examining neural correlates of subjective value"

## What the paper did

This is a quantitative, coordinate-based meta-analysis pooling 206 published fMRI studies of subjective-value (SV) signals in decision making. Rather than a single new experiment, it asks a structural question across the whole field: is there a consistent, domain-general set of brain regions whose activity tracks subjective value regardless of what kind of reward is being evaluated (money, food, other primary rewards), or does each reward domain recruit its own separate valuation circuitry?

## Key findings relevant to ARC-121

The meta-analysis identifies two distinct patterns. First, a genuinely domain-general "valuation system" -- centered on ventromedial prefrontal cortex and anterior ventral striatum -- shows consistent positive SV effects across reward types, at both the moment of choice and the moment of outcome. This is the empirical backbone of the "common currency" hypothesis: the brain does not maintain separate, walled-off valuation machinery for every kind of reward: it routes them through a shared hub. Second, a separate set of regions (anterior insula, dorsomedial prefrontal cortex, dorsal and posterior striatum) shows a different signature -- both positive and negative SV effects, more consistent with a salience or arousal signal than a value signal per se.

## Translation to REE

ARC-121 asserts that REE's mechanisms -- prediction, replay, harm/ethics evaluation, planning, and learning -- increasingly converge on maintaining and consuming ONE shared epistemic-state-adjacent object rather than each inventing its own local, isolated state. This meta-analysis supplies the general biological precondition that makes ARC-121's architectural intuition plausible in the first place: ordinary reward valuation in the brain is demonstrably NOT domain-specific machinery bolted on separately for each reward type, but a shared hub. If even basic reward valuation shared a common substrate across domains, that raises the prior that harm/ethics evaluation -- itself a form of valuation, just over aversive rather than appetitive outcomes -- might tap the same hub rather than maintaining a private evaluative stream.

## Limitations and caveats

This meta-analysis is scoped entirely to reward valuation studies (monetary and primary rewards); no moral, harm, or ethics judgment paradigms are part of its input corpus. It therefore cannot by itself confirm that harm/ethics evaluation specifically routes through the vmPFC/ventral-striatum hub it identifies -- it establishes only that such a hub exists and is genuinely domain-general within the reward domain. The companion entry in this directory, Shenhav & Greene (2010), closes exactly this gap by studying moral judgment directly and finding it recruits this same circuitry. It is also worth flagging the second, salience-coding region set the meta-analysis identifies as a caution against assuming every affect-adjacent brain region is part of one shared "epistemic state" object -- some evaluative processing is domain-general, but not all of it, and the boundary matters for how literally ARC-121's claim should be read.

## Confidence reasoning

Very high source quality: this is the standard, most-cited quantitative reference for the common-currency valuation-hub hypothesis, based on a large, well-powered meta-analytic corpus. Mapping fidelity is moderate rather than high because the paper's own scope stops short of testing harm/ethics evaluation directly -- it is necessary but not sufficient evidence for ARC-121's specific claim about that consumer, which is why it is paired here with a direct moral-judgment study.
