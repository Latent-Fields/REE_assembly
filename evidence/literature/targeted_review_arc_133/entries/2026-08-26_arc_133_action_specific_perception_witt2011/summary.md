# Action-specific perception: suggestive, but contested (Witt 2011)

**Source.** Witt, J.K., *Action's Effect on Perception*, Current Directions in Psychological Science 20(3):201-206, 2011. [DOI:10.1177/0963721411408770](https://doi.org/10.1177/0963721411408770).

**What the paper does.** This is a review, by one of the field's principal contributors, of the "action-specific perception" literature: a body of psychophysical work claiming that a perceiver's capacity to act on the environment -- shaped by energetic state (fatigue, carried load, physical fitness, age/health) -- changes how the SAME physical scene is visually estimated. The headline findings: hills look steeper to fatigued, overweight, or heavily-laden observers than to fit, unburdened ones looking at the identical slope; gaps and distances are judged farther when traversal is harder (heavier backpack, ankle weights). The proposed mechanism is that the visual system incorporates the energetic cost of the relevant action into its estimate, purportedly as a resource-conservation strategy.

**Why it matters for ARC-133.** If genuine, this is a case where a bodily/energetic-state variable changes perception of a fixed external scene -- loosely adjacent to ARC-133's sharpest claim that the same scene carves differently under different interoceptive states. It is the closest thing found in this pull to a general-perception (not just valuation) instance of state-dependent processing of an unchanging stimulus.

**Why this entry is weak, and marked mixed rather than supports.** Two separate problems, and both need to be on the record rather than glossed over. First, even accepted at face value, the phenomenon is magnitude/property remapping (how steep, how far) of a scene whose object identity and boundaries are not in question -- it is not a test of individuation (one object or two), which is what ARC-133 actually needs. Second, and more serious: this is a genuinely contested literature. Firestone & Scholl's "Cognition does not affect perception: Evaluating the evidence for 'top-down' effects" (Behavioral and Brain Sciences, 2016) and related work argue that several of these findings -- hill-slant and load-distance effects specifically among them -- are better explained by response bias and demand characteristics (participants inferring the experimental hypothesis, e.g. "wearing a heavy backpack" cues them to report greater steepness, without any change in actual visual experience) than by genuine perceptual change. This review, authored from within the action-specific-perception program, does not engage that critique, and the underlying dispute has not been definitively resolved in either direction as of this pull.

**Confidence.** 0.4. Source quality is discounted specifically for the unresolved controversy (not for the review's own execution, which is competent and well-cited). Mapping fidelity is low because the effect, even taken at face value, targets magnitude estimation rather than object individuation. This is deliberately the weakest-confidence entry in this ARC-133 pull and should be read as suggestive plausibility, not as a load-bearing empirical anchor.

**Lit-only verdict, not governance.** Weak, contested, indirect support for the general plausibility that internal state can modulate perception of an unchanging scene. Does not evidence individuation, and should not be cited alone as support for ARC-133's core claim -- the Cabanac (1971) and Connolly et al. (2016) entries in this same directory carry more weight for the value-precondition and independent-dimension pieces of the claim respectively.
