Lamm, Decety and Singer pooled fMRI studies of empathy for pain, running an image-based meta-analysis over nine datasets and a coordinate-based meta-analysis over 32 studies. They found a consistent core of bilateral anterior insula and medial/anterior cingulate that responds both to one's own pain and to another's. On top of that core, picture-based paradigms (seeing hands or feet in painful situations) recruited inferior parietal and ventral premotor areas plus somatosensory cortex, while abstract cue-based paradigms recruited mentalizing regions: precuneus, ventromedial prefrontal cortex, superior temporal cortex and temporoparietal junction.

For MECH-405 the shared core is the relevant part: it is the affective, interoceptive side of pain, not the sensory side, that is reused for others. That lines up with the claim's choice to route through the agent's motivational-affective streams. This result also generalises the Singer et al. 2004 finding that anterior insula and rostral ACC, but not the sensory pain regions, respond to a partner's pain.

The meta-analysis also shows the limits. Overlap at fMRI resolution does not establish that the same representation is used, and the co-activated networks vary with how the other's state is signalled. Those added networks look like the 'other-model binding' part of the claim, and they are not a thin gate: they are substantial systems whose recruitment depends on the input.

Confidence 0.6, supports for the shared affective core, with the caveat that the claim should acknowledge input-dependent attribution machinery.
