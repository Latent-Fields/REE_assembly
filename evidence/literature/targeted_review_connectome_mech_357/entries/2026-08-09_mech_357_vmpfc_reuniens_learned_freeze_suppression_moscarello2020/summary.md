# Moscarello (2020) -- Prefrontal cortex projections to the nucleus reuniens suppress freezing following two-way signaled avoidance training

**Learning & Memory, 2020. DOI: 10.1101/lm.050377.119. PMID: 32071258.**

What did they do? Rats learned a two-way signaled avoidance task over three days, then -- 24 hours later, with avoidance made impossible -- were re-exposed to the tone in a novel context while the authors selectively silenced ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC), just its projection terminals in the nucleus reuniens (NR, a midline thalamic relay), or NR itself, using chemogenetics and pharmacology across three separate experiments.

What did they find? All three manipulations did the same thing: rats that had successfully learned to avoid, once you take away their vmPFC->NR pathway, freeze to the tone as much as animals that never learned to avoid at all. The suppression of freezing that avoidance-trained rats normally show is not just "less afraid" -- it is an active, circuit-dependent brake that can be switched back off. The author's own framing is useful: this pathway looks like "a common pathway recruited by memory phenomena that suppress freezing," not a brake that is simply always on.

Why does this matter for MECH-357? MECH-357's whole reason for existing as a claim distinct from REE's other threat-response mechanisms (SD-037's orexin override, MECH-281's urgency interrupt) is that its freeze-suppression gate is supposed to track a slowly LEARNED avoidance-efficacy trace, not fire reflexively off raw threat signals. This paper is close to the shape of evidence that would support exactly that: take the suppression pathway away from an animal that has already learned to avoid, and it reverts to the freezing profile of an animal that never learned -- i.e. the suppression is doing work that depends on learned competence having accrued, not on the threat cue alone.

It is genuinely useful evidence, but it should not be over-read. The pathway here runs through the nucleus reuniens -- a thalamic relay tied to memory retrieval and cortex-hippocampus communication -- not through the amygdala or PAG that MECH-357's gate is modeled as acting on directly. And the design tests a single post-training snapshot rather than tracking the suppression signal's strength continuously as the animal learns; the "this is learning-dependent, not tonic" argument leans partly on citation of separate extinction-literature findings rather than on this study's own within-subject trajectory. So it supports the general shape MECH-357 needs (suppression contingent on learned competence) without confirming the specific circuit.

The most interesting thing about this entry is what it does sitting next to the Ho et al. (2025) entry in this same directory: Ho found an infralimbic freeze-suppression signal that is present almost immediately and flat across learning; Moscarello finds a vmPFC-sourced suppression that behaves as though it depends on learning having happened. Both are credible, well-controlled findings about closely related but non-identical circuits. That tension -- not a tidy resolution -- is the honest state of the literature on whether prefrontal freeze-suppression during avoidance is a pre-wired switch or a learned one, and MECH-357's specific falsifiable prediction sits right in the middle of it.
