# Recovery of "Lost" Infant Memories in Mice (Guskjolen et al. 2018)

According to PubMed ([DOI 10.1016/j.cub.2018.05.059](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2018.05.059)), this *Current Biology* study from the Frankland and Josselyn groups asks a sharp question about infantile amnesia: when an early-life memory is "forgotten," is the trace destroyed (storage failure) or merely rendered inaccessible (retrieval failure)? The authors trained infant mice on contextual fear, used a channelrhodopsin (ChR2) strategy to permanently tag the dentate gyrus neuronal ensemble active during encoding, and then, in adulthood — long after the memory was behaviourally gone — optogenetically reactivated that tagged ensemble. The reactivation recovered the "lost" memory up to three months after training, and recovery was accompanied by broader reactivation of tagged hippocampal and cortical ensembles.

This is the most directly useful paper I found for Q-057, because it cleanly separates the two failure modes that Q-057 is trying to disentangle. The natural retrieval cue could not release the memory, but the trace was demonstrably still there — an artificial cue with enough reach recovered it. In REE terms, that is a *cue-authority* failure, not a storage failure: the memory exists but the available cue lacks the authority to drive action. That is precisely the first sub-question of Q-057 ("does the cue-authority problem reflect immature single-cue authority rather than mature convergent-weak-input recall?") and it bears directly on the third sub-question about developmental gates standing between an encoded trace and its ability to influence action release. The mapping to MECH-362 (the CA3/DG developmental anchor) is natural.

There are two honest caveats, and one of them is itself informative. First, the recovering cue here is optogenetic — a supra-physiological, artificial reinstatement. The study proves the trace is recoverable; it does *not* prove that a natural, convergent-but-weak input would have sufficed. So it supports the storage-vs-retrieval dissociation strongly but speaks only indirectly to the *single-cue-vs-convergent-input* distinction that REE instruments. Second, and more interestingly as a failure signature: recovery required broad reactivation across hippocampal *and* cortical ensembles, not a single CA3 readout. If REE's account locates cue-authority maturation purely in a CA3 pattern-completion step, this result is a gentle warning that the missing ingredient may be distributed reinstatement rather than a single-locus readout.

I have set evidence_direction to **supports** with confidence 0.7. The source quality is high (clean causal optogenetics, strong lab), and the central dissociation is exactly on Q-057's target — which is why this is the anchor entry for the claim. I held confidence below 0.8 because the artificial cue and the rodent-fear context make the transfer to REE's action-release gating an analogy rather than a measured correspondence, and because the distributed-reinstatement requirement complicates a clean single-cue reading.
