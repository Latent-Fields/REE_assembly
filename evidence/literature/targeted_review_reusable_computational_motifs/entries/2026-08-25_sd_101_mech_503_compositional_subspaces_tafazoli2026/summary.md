# Building compositional tasks with shared neural subspaces (Tafazoli, Bouchacourt, Ardalan, Markov, Uchimura, Mattar, Daw & Buschman, 2025/2026, *Nature*)

Of the three papers the source thought names, this is the one whose task design is most deliberately built to test the exact question SD-101/MECH-503 raise, rather than discovering the phenomenon as a side effect of studying something else. The authors train monkeys to switch between three tasks that are compositionally related by construction -- they deliberately share sensory and motor structure across tasks -- and then ask whether the brain represents that shared structure as shared neural subspaces, or rebuilds a dedicated representation per task. The verbatim abstract, pulled from Princeton's institutional repository after the Nature and biorxiv full-text pages were both gated or rate-limited, answers cleanly: task-relevant sensory and motor information is represented in subspaces that ARE shared across the three tasks, and performing a given task involves transforming activity in the shared sensory subspace into the shared motor subspace appropriate to that task.

What makes this entry distinct from the other two in this pull, and useful specifically for MECH-503's SELECT step (as opposed to its BIND step), is the account of how the animal decides which subspace pairing to engage. The authors describe monkeys as adapting to task changes by "iteratively updating an internal belief about the current task" and then, based on that belief, engaging the relevant shared subspaces. That is a biological existence proof for something structurally close to what MECH-481's typed-demand classification already does in REE's architecture, feeding into MECH-503's proposed role-selection step: some process infers what task/demand is currently in force, and that inference gates which reusable structure gets engaged, before content gets routed through it. The sensory-to-motor subspace transformation itself is a reasonable stand-in for what MECH-503 means by "content binding" -- task-relevant content (here, sensory information) is routed through a shared, task-appropriate transformation rather than each task building its own dedicated pipeline from scratch.

Two limits are worth being explicit about, because they bear directly on how far this generalizes to MECH-503's stronger falsifiers. First, "compositionally related by design" cuts both ways: it is exactly the right setup to demonstrate that shared subspaces CAN be reused across tasks that share structure, but it does not test whether the brain (or REE's eventual substrate) can recombine existing subspaces for a task it was never trained on at all -- MECH-503's Test C (recombination on a genuinely novel task) needs the latter, and this paper's task set does not reach that bar since novelty here is task-switching among trained tasks, not zero-shot composition. Second, based on what I could confirm from the abstract and available secondary sources, this is a representational/correlational demonstration -- population geometry and subspace-engagement patterns tied to the inferred task belief -- not a causal one; I did not find a lesion, microstimulation, or optogenetic result confirming that disrupting a shared subspace produces the task-specific behavioral deficit the correlational picture would predict. That is a real gap relative to what MECH-503's coalition x binding factorial (Test D) is ultimately asking for.

Translating to REE: this paper is the strongest available anchor in the pull for the SELECTION half of MECH-503's proposed sequence specifically, and reasonable but partial support for the BINDING half via the sensory-to-motor transformation result. It complements rather than duplicates the other two entries -- Osako et al. gives within-trial re-binding of a single subspace's content, Driscoll/Shenoy/Sussillo gives a purely computational demonstration of motif reuse with a causal lesion test, and this paper gives the clearest primate account of how selection among shared structures might be gated by an inferred task context, which is closer to what a "typed demand classifier feeding a motif selector" would need to look like biologically.

Confidence: 0.72. Strong venue and a task design purpose-built for exactly this question raise mapping fidelity; held below the Driscoll et al. entry's confidence because the strongest evidence here is correlational rather than causal, and the "compositionally related by design" task set does not test genuinely novel-task recombination.
