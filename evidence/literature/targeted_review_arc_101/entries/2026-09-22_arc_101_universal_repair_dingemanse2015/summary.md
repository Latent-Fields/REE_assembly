# Dingemanse et al. (2015) -- repair is universal, frequent, cheap, and grammar-independent

Twelve languages, eight unrelated families, naturalistic conversation throughout. The finding is
that other-initiated repair -- one participant signalling that something did not land, the other
fixing it -- is present everywhere, runs at roughly once every 1.4 minutes, uses the same three
functionally distinct kinds of repair initiator in the same kinds of sequential position, and
finishes in about the time it takes to say the thing being repaired. Repair is not a rare
breakdown-handling routine. It is continuous background infrastructure running underneath ordinary
talk.

ARC-101 names repair as one of five enabling conditions for language bootstrap, and the honest
question is whether that listing is earned or merely plausible. This paper is what earns it. The
force of the result is the invariance rather than the frequency: when a mechanism keeps the same
functional shape across eight unrelated grammatical systems, the most natural reading is that the
mechanism is not a product of any of those grammars. That is exactly the property ARC-101 needs
repair to have. If repair were grammatically parameterised, it could not be part of the engine --
it would be something the turbocharger installs.

The limitation is one of study design and I would rather state it plainly than let the supports
direction imply more than it should. This is a synchronic, cross-sectional corpus study of adult
speakers of fully-formed languages. Everything it establishes is about the steady state. A feature
that is universal in mature languages is just as consistent with repair being a CONSEQUENCE of
having language as with repair being a precondition for acquiring one -- and the second reading is
the one ARC-101 asserts. Nothing in this dataset discriminates between them, because the regime in
which the discrimination would happen, a system with no language yet, is not sampled.

There is also a mapping subtlety worth flagging for governance. REE's "repair" would be a mechanism
by which an agent detects and corrects a coordination failure in a signalling channel. Human
conversational repair is that, but it is also richly metalinguistic -- open-class initiators like
"huh?", restricted requests, restricted offers. The three-type taxonomy is defined over utterances.
Whether a proto-communicative system with no utterance-level structure can support a functionally
analogous three-way distinction is an open question, and a REE implementation that produces only
one undifferentiated trouble signal would not straightforwardly be the same mechanism.

Confidence 0.74. High source quality carries it; the synchronic-for-developmental substitution
holds it down. The practical consequence for REE is that repair should be treated as a first-class
required component of the V5 social ecology rather than something added once signalling works --
this evidence says repair is what a working communication system is made of, not what gets bolted
on afterwards.
