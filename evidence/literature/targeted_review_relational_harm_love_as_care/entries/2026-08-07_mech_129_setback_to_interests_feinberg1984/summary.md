# Feinberg (1984), *Harm to Others* -- harm as setback to interests

## What the source argues

Joel Feinberg's *Harm to Others* is the first volume of his four-volume *The Moral Limits of the Criminal Law* and remains the reference account of what "harm" is. Its central move is to prise harm apart from physical hurt. To harm a person, on Feinberg's analysis, is to **set back one of their interests** -- to leave them worse off, with respect to some interest they have, than they would otherwise have been. A hurt (pain) may accompany a harm but is neither necessary nor sufficient for it; conversely one can be harmed by an event one never feels.

Feinberg then divides interests into two kinds. **Welfare interests** are the minimal, generic prerequisites of pursuing *any* goals at all: bodily health and integrity, a modicum of material resources, security, a baseline of liberty. **Ulterior interests** are the specific projects, goals and aspirations -- a career, raising a child, finishing a book -- that give a particular life its direction. Welfare interests are the floor on which ulterior interests stand; damage low enough sets back everything above it.

## Why it bears on MECH-129

MECH-129 claims that in a multiagent world harm must be represented as *two distinct signal types*: harm-to-agent (a direct cost/physical signal localised to the agent's own trajectory) and harm-to-agency (obstruction of another agent's goal-pursuit, with no direct physical harm). Feinberg supplies the philosophical warrant for the second category even existing. If harm just were physical hurt, "harm-to-agency" would be a category error. Because harm is a setback to an *interest*, blocking another agent's goal -- setting back their ulterior interest in reaching it -- is a genuine harm, exactly as MECH-129 requires, and it is a *different* setback (different object, different truth-conditions) from damaging the agent's own body.

The mapping is unusually clean at the level of the taxonomy: REE's harm-to-agent (SD-010 harm_bridge, SD-011 dual nociceptive streams) tracks setbacks to the agent's own **welfare** interests, while harm-to-agency tracks setbacks to another agent's **ulterior** interest in an ongoing goal-pursuit. That two interests of different types are set back under different conditions is precisely the "distinct signal types requiring distinct architectures" thesis, restated in the vocabulary of moral philosophy fifty years earlier.

## Limits of the mapping, honestly

Two caveats keep this at 0.7 rather than higher. First, Feinberg's welfare/ulterior split is *adjacent to* but not *identical with* MECH-129's agent/agency split. A bodily assault sets back a welfare interest yet is still harm-to-*agent*, not harm-to-agency; only the goal-pursuit reading of ulterior-interest-setback is harm-to-agency. So Feinberg licenses the *shape* of the distinction without handing REE the exact cut. Second, Feinberg's analysis is **counterfactual-comparative** -- harmed means worse off than one *would otherwise have been* -- which imports the baseline-selection problem: worse off than *what* default? A computational goal-interference signal inherits this directly (obstruction relative to the other agent's un-interfered-with trajectory), and choosing that baseline badly makes the signal over- or under-report. This is the same baseline hazard that dogs low-impact AI measures, and it connects this entry to the Turner *Attainable Utility Preservation* entry in this pull.

Finally, Feinberg's harm-as-*wrong* is his non-normative harm-as-setback wrapped in a normative layer (the setback must be unjustified and rights-violating). MECH-129's raw goal-interference signal corresponds only to the non-normative core; the consent and justification gates (discriminants 3-4 in the claim) are exactly the normative layer Feinberg adds on top. A substrate that fires on any obstruction, without a consent gate, over-reports harm in precisely the cases -- consented-to competition, justified interference -- that Feinberg explicitly excludes from harm-as-wrong. The direction of evidence is nonetheless clearly **supporting**: Feinberg establishes that the harm-to-agency category is real, principled, and distinct, which is the premise MECH-129 needs.
