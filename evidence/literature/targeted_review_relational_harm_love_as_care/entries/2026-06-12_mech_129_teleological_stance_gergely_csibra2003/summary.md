# Teleological reasoning in infancy: the naive theory of rational action (Gergely & Csibra 2003)

**Claim:** MECH-129 (harm-to-agency as a relational signal distinct from harm-to-agent)
**Direction:** supports · **Confidence:** 0.68 · **Class:** theoretical_review

## What the paper did

Gergely and Csibra synthesise a decade of infant-cognition results into a single inferential
principle. One-year-olds, they argue, do not yet read minds, but they do something powerful and
specific: they apply a *teleological stance*. Given an action, an inferred goal-state, and the
situational constraints, infants expect the agent to take the *most efficient available means* to
the goal. The classic demonstration (Gergely et al. 1995, "Taking the intentional stance at 12
months") is the jumping-over-a-barrier habituation: infants who saw an agent leap over a barrier
to reach a target later looked longer when the agent leapt over empty space (the barrier removed)
than when it took the now-available straight path -- because the leap was no longer the rational
means. The paper's central theoretical move is that this *non-mentalistic* rationality principle
is continuous with the later, mentalistic theory of mind: the same efficiency assumption that an
infant applies to behaviour, the older child applies to beliefs and desires.

## Why it matters for MECH-129

Woodward gives MECH-129 the represented goal; Gergely and Csibra give it the *inference rule that
operates on that goal*. MECH-129 needs more than "the other has a goal" -- it needs to compute
whether a given trajectory (mine, or the pair of ours) serves or defeats that goal. That is
discriminant (2): "whether your action obstructs it." The principle of rational action is
precisely a detector for departures from efficient goal-pursuit. If I model the other as pursuing
their goal by the most efficient means, then my own interfering trajectory shows up as a forced
*inefficiency* in their pursuit -- a deviation from the rational path that the teleological
computation flags. This is the biologically-attested machinery that turns a represented goal
(Woodward) into a detectable obstruction (MECH-129's harm-to-agency signal). It also matters that
this competence is *early and non-mentalistic*: it tells us the harm-to-agency substrate does not
require full belief-attribution, which is consistent with the project's commitment (empathy notes;
ARC-010) to build the OTHER_SELFLIKE stream as an extension of existing self/world/impact
machinery rather than a bolted-on mind-reading subsystem.

## Limitations and caveats

The non-mentalistic character of the teleological stance is both its strength and its ceiling.
It grounds discriminants (1) what the goal is and (2) whether an action obstructs it, but by
construction it says nothing about (3) whether an obstruction is incidental or constitutive, or
(4) whether the other consented -- both of which require representing the other's commitment or
mental state. The plan is right to route those to RHM-3 and the mirror-modelling layer; this paper
should not be cited for them. There is also a built-in failure mode that REE inherits if it adopts
the rationality principle: efficiency-based goal attribution misfires when an agent is behaving
inefficiently for a reason the observer cannot see (a hidden constraint, a second goal). A
goal-interference signal built on this principle will sometimes read a justified detour as
thwarted agency. That is a real risk worth carrying forward into the RHM-1 substrate design. I
rate this "supports" at 0.68: it is the right inference rule, attested in biology, but it is a
review rather than a single experiment and its reach stops at the consent discriminant.
