# Manassi, Murai & Whitney (2023) -- Serial dependence: a meta-analysis and review

According to PubMed (PMID 37642639; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1167/jov.23.8.18)), this meta-analysis pulls the sprawling serial-dependence literature into one operational definition. The core phenomenon: what we perceive, decide, and remember about a feature or object is systematically pulled *toward* what we saw a moment ago. The authors show it is defined by four tunings -- it decays with time, depends on relative spatial location, occurs only between similar features or objects, and is modulated by attention -- and they frame the net effect as an *illusory stability*: an online smoothing that makes a noisy, ever-changing input feel like a continuous world.

This entry is the deliberate counterpoint in the MECH-456 dossier, and I have marked it `mixed` rather than `supports` on purpose. MECH-456 is a claim about *switching* -- re-binding the self-anchor to a competing world-configuration when that competitor overtakes. Serial dependence is the force in the *other* direction: perceptual stickiness, a hysteresis that resists switching. So why include it, and why does it support the claim at all? Because the claim's own design rationale invokes exactly this counterweight: rebinding must overcome 'the stability counterweight... motivating a threshold rather than chatter.' A binder that re-bound on every transient fluctuation would be useless -- it would chatter. What makes rebinding a sensible mechanism is that it fires only when a competitor overtakes *decisively*, clearing a hysteresis band. Manassi and colleagues give the empirical warrant that biological perception really does carry such a band, and that it is tuned to feature-similarity -- which even predicts that the competition should be scored between comparable configurations, not arbitrary ones.

So the honest reading is two-directional. On one hand, serial dependence is prima-facie evidence for stability *against* rebinding -- a skeptic could cite this very paper as pressure on MECH-456. On the other, it is precisely the phenomenon that justifies the *threshold* structure MECH-456 builds in, and without which a rebinding rule would be pathological. Both readings are true at once, which is what `mixed` is for. The value of the entry is that it forces the claim to own its counterweight rather than pretend rebinding is free.

The transfer caveats are real. Serial dependence is characterised on low-level continuous features -- orientation, numerosity, face identity -- in human psychophysics, not on discrete self<->world-configuration bindings, and the individual differences are large enough that the paper cannot tell us *how much* hysteresis a binder should emulate. The threshold analogy is architectural, not a measured property of any binder. I have set source quality high (0.88 -- this is a careful meta-analysis) but mapping fidelity only 0.5, reflecting that it grounds a design constraint and an opposing force rather than the switch itself, for an aggregate 0.6.
