# Wen, Charles & Haggard (2023) — what "single-pass" buys and what it misses

According to PubMed, this study ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2023.105622)) speaks to the word in SD-031 that is doing the most quiet work: **single-pass**.

The method is careful in a way that matters for reading the result. Participants judged which of two visual objects they controlled, with the proportion of participant-driven versus pre-recorded motion staircased to hold detection accuracy at 70%, and the analysis used bias-free signal-detection measures (d', meta-d') across 40 participants. That design separates genuine sensitivity from judgment criterion — the confound the authors note has muddied much of the earlier subjective-rating literature on agency.

The finding relevant here is a decomposition. First-order detection of control rests on **two** distinct processes: one based on instantaneous sensory predictions for the current movement, and one based on detecting a regular motor-visual relation *across a series* of movements. SD-031's residual — `z_world_observed - E2_world(z_world_{t-1}, a_actual)` — is squarely the first kind: a per-timestep prediction error. So this supports SD-031's chosen mechanism as real and operative, while bounding its sufficiency. A single-pass residual has no way to represent "over the last twenty steps my actions have reliably co-varied with this object's motion," which is the second process.

I want to flag one thing before this gets recorded as an architectural gap, because it would be easy to over-read. The temporally-extended component may already live elsewhere in REE. SD-031's own functional_restatement names ResidueField-style causal tracking as a downstream consumer of the residual, and accumulating a per-object causal association across time is close to what the second process does. So the correct statement is that SD-031 alone does not implement cross-trial regularity detection — not that REE lacks it. Which of those is true is a substrate question that should be checked against the code rather than assumed from this paper.

A second result is worth carrying forward for a different reason. Metacognitive sensitivity was *negatively* correlated with the predictive-process component, which the authors note is inconsistent with the prior hypothesis that control detection relies on metacognitive monitoring of a predictive circuit; they conclude the predictive mechanism likely operates unconsciously. That the result runs against the authors' own expectation raises rather than lowers my confidence in it. For REE it is quietly reassuring: it removes any implied requirement that a metacognitive or reportability layer sit above the comparator for attribution to work. SD-031 implementing attribution as a low-level, non-introspectable signal is consistent with the human data, and that is one less thing the design has to justify.

The caveats are the familiar ones plus one specific to the analysis. The controlled consequence is again visuomotor object motion rather than the broader world-state consequences z_world targets — the same scope limit as Ohata et al. And the two processes were separated by individual-differences and structural equation modelling rather than by manipulating each directly, so the decomposition is correlational; the components may be less cleanly separable than the model implies.

Taken with the other two SD-031 entries, a consistent picture emerges, and it is more useful than any single paper. Ohata et al. supply positive warrant for a *separable, parietally-preferenced* attribution signal. Christensen and Grünbaum warn that extending a movement-validated comparator to environmental events is the contested step. Wen et al. show the instantaneous comparator is genuine but partial. None of these blocks SD-031. Together they say the same thing about its pending validation: it should not settle for a residual-magnitude gap between self-caused and externally-caused events, because a simpler motor-signal account or a cross-trial regularity account could produce that gap too. It should show the single-pass residual discriminating *better than* those simpler alternatives.
