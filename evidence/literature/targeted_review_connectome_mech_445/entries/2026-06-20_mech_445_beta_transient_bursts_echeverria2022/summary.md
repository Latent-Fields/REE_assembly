# Beta is bursty, not tonic, even when the motor state is sustained (Echeverria-Altuna et al. 2022)

According to PubMed, Echeverria-Altuna and colleagues (2022), *Transient beta activity and cortico-muscular connectivity during sustained motor behaviour*, *Progress in Neurobiology* ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pneurobio.2022.102281)), is the empirical counterweight to the Engel & Fries status-quo story, and that is exactly why it belongs in this pull. If beta coupling literally *held a motor state in place* the way MECH-445's latch language suggests, then during a continuously sustained motor act beta and its downstream coupling should themselves be sustained. The authors tested that directly. Using MEG and EMG in 18 people performing a sustained isometric grip, they asked whether cortico-muscular beta connectivity becomes (or stays) continuous when the motor demand is continuous. It does not. Motor beta activity and beta connectivity with the contralateral muscle occurred as **short transient bursts**, and -- the result I find most constraining -- sustained motor requirements did not prolong beta-event duration relative to rest.

The reason I logged this as **mixed** rather than as support or refutation is that it cuts against only one *reading* of MECH-445, not the mechanism itself. The naive reading -- "closure fires, beta goes high and stays high, the latch is held aloft for the whole commitment epoch" -- is not what the muscle-coupling data show. But the finding is fully compatible with a different and, frankly, more defensible operationalisation: that a closure-driven commit elevates the *rate* or *occupancy* of beta-like binding events, not their tonic level or their individual duration. Under that reading, "the closure elevates/binds the latch" means the closure raises the probability that the latch-binding signal is engaged at any given moment, which is precisely how transient bursts would implement a maintained state. So the paper does not weaken the claim; it tells us how to *cash out* the word "elevation" if we want the mechanism to be biologically literate.

The transfer caveat is important and I do not want to paper over it. This study measured cortico-*muscular* beta during a peripheral gripping task. MECH-445 concerns an internal commitment latch (MECH-090's bistable variable) and a `closure_commit_beta_coupling` path that is an engineered construct, not a literal beta rhythm. The jump from "beta-to-muscle coupling is bursty during gripping" to "a central commitment latch is bound by bursty rather than tonic coupling" is a real inferential step, and it is possible that central commitment dynamics behave differently from the peripheral motor-output channel these authors recorded. I am treating the result as a *constraint on interpretation* -- a reminder to instrument burst rate / latch occupancy rather than a tonic level when the V3 experiments probe MECH-445 -- not as a measurement of REE's latch.

Confidence 0.5. Source quality is high (0.8): modern single-trial burst analysis, MEG+EMG, a clean sustained-state manipulation. Mapping fidelity (0.5) and transfer risk (0.5) are both held at the midline because the paper speaks authoritatively to the *temporal form* of beta coupling but not to the central commitment process MECH-445 actually posits. Its practical value is calibration: it warns the project against reading "beta coupling elevation" as a tonic shelf, and points instrument design toward burst-rate / occupancy readouts of the latch.
