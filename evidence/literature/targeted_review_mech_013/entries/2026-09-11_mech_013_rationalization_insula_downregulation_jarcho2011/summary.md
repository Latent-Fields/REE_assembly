# The Neural Basis of Rationalization (Jarcho, Berkman & Lieberman, 2011)

MECH-013's first failure mode says language can be "used to suppress or explain away harm signals".
The word *suppress* is doing real work there. It implies that some aversive signal exists and is
turned down, which is different from a verbal system simply telling a convenient story alongside it.
Of the papers I found, this is the only one that measures anything at the signal level, so I have
included it despite its weaknesses.

Twenty-one people were scanned while choosing between pairs of names and paintings they had rated
about equally. Afterwards they re-rated everything. In the 12 who showed reliable decision-related
attitude change (the only ones analysed), greater later change was associated, *at the moment of
choice*, with more activity in right inferior frontal gyrus, medial prefrontal cortex, precuneus and
ventral striatum, and with *less* activity in bilateral anterior insula. The authors read this as
reappraisal-like emotion regulation engaged quickly at commitment rather than through slow
post-decision deliberation.

That is the right shape for the suppression reading of MECH-013: a control signal goes up, an
aversive or interoceptive signal goes down, and the pairing predicts the later rationalisation. In
REE terms it suggests treating rationalisation as a precision operation. A regulatory gain reduction
would be applied to the harm or residue stream at the commit step, which is the claim's own "precision
misrouting" pathology, rather than as a later rewrite of the residue record. That prediction is
concrete enough that an eventual REE substrate with a symbolic justification pathway could look for
it.

But I want to be honest about how thin this is. Nothing in the task is linguistic and nothing is
ethical. People were choosing baby names and posters, so the insula signal that fell is generic
decision conflict, and calling it a "harm signal" is reverse inference on one of the brain's most
multi-purpose regions. Twelve selected responders is a very small sample. Most seriously, the
rate-choose-rate design is the one Chen and Risen (2010) showed can produce apparent choice-induced
attitude change purely as an artefact of choices revealing pre-existing preferences, and this study
has no rate-rate-choose control. Its neural correlates may partly track the artefact.

Confidence 0.38. The paper supplies a plausible mechanism shape for the suppression reading, which
none of the behavioural entries can. It does not show that language does the suppressing, or that the
suppressed signal has anything to do with harm. Governance should not count it as more than a pointer
to where a suppression effect would have to be looked for.
