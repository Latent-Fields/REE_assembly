# Crying as a graded, honest-but-spoofable distress signal (Zeifman 2001)

According to PubMed, Zeifman (2001), *An ethological analysis of human infant crying: answering Tinbergen's four questions*, *Developmental Psychobiology* ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/dev.1005)), is the entry that supplies the half of Q-059 the laughter papers leave untouched: the crying / distress-vocalisation guardrail itself. Working through Tinbergen's four questions -- proximate causation, survival value, ontogeny, and phylogeny -- and drawing on distress and begging vocalisations across birds, mammals, and nonhuman primates as well as cross-cultural records of infant care, Zeifman argues that human infant crying evolved as a **primarily acoustic, graded signal**. Two of her conclusions are the load-bearing ones here. First, crying is a *fairly reliable, if imperfect*, indicator of need for parental care, and its primary function is to promote caregiving. Second -- and this is the part that makes it a genuine analogue to the laughter problem -- the signal carries a built-in "potential for corruption through dishonesty."

Q-059 asks whether REE needs a crying / distress-vocalisation guardrail counterpart to its laughter mechanism (MECH-110 / MECH-364). Zeifman is the evidence that the candidate is real and well-characterised: distress vocalisation is a bona fide social-guardrail signal whose job is to recruit care and repair under threat or need. And it has exactly the dual nature that, on the laughter side, forces adjudication. Just as non-Duchenne laughter can be produced strategically to mimic the affiliative form, a distress call is honest *on average* but exaggerable -- a graded amplitude that can be dialled up dishonestly. The structural lesson for a REE guardrail is the same one the laughter entries reached from the other direction: a guardrail keyed on signal *intensity* alone is exploitable, so adjudication has to weight provenance and context, not just how loud the distress reads. The two signals look, in this light, like a complementary pair -- laughter easing tension and building bonds, crying recruiting care and repair under distress -- which is presumably why Q-059 pairs them.

The caveat I most want on the record is a relationship-structure mismatch, because it is the easiest way to over-map this paper. Infant crying operates within an **asymmetric care relationship**: the signal recruits a caregiver, an agent in a different role with a standing disposition to respond. Q-059's adjudication concern, by contrast, is largely *peer-level* social-field dynamics -- repair versus damage among comparable agents. The honest-signalling logic (graded, reliable-on-average, spoofable) transfers cleanly across that gap, but the *function* does not: "recruit a caregiver" is not the same operation as "repair a peer relationship," and a REE design that imported crying as a peer-level guardrail without noticing this would be committing a quiet category error about what relationship the signal actually regulates. The paper is also, like the others in this pull, silent on REE's substrate and on the open `v4` / `substrate_conditional` build decision -- it speaks to whether the question is well-grounded, not to whether the guardrail should be built now.

Direction **supports**, confidence 0.6. Source quality is solid (0.78) for a well-regarded comparative synthesis organised around Tinbergen's framework. Mapping fidelity is moderate (0.6): it nails distress vocalisation as a graded, spoofable guardrail signal, but in a care-recruitment frame rather than the peer-repair frame Q-059 ultimately needs. Transfer risk (0.45) reflects both the breadth of the animal-to-human evidence base and the infant-care-to-REE-social-field jump.
