# Hanseeuw et al. (2020) -- confidence changes years before the memory diagnosis, but amyloid predicts it

According to PubMed, this study followed 1,070 participants across the preclinical-to-dementia spectrum, with baseline florbetapir amyloid PET and an average of 4.3 longitudinal visits, operationalising memory awareness as the discrepancy between participant and study-partner report on the Everyday Cognition memory domain. Amyloid burden predicted how fast awareness declined. In participants who progressed from cognitively normal to MCI, awareness was *heightened* up to 1.6 years before diagnosis and then fell. In those progressing from MCI to dementia, awareness was low at the outset and continued to fall, reaching frank anosognosia around 3.2 years before dementia onset. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/ana.25649)

This is the most directly relevant paper I could find for INV-047, and it does something genuinely valuable: it puts numbers on the claim's most exposed assertion, that a metacognitive/confidence change comes *first*, before episodic memory failure. The timing evidence is real and it points the right way. A confidence-related signal moves measurably years ahead of the memory-defined clinical transitions, on its own trajectory. If one wanted evidence that AD decline is staged rather than uniform, the existence of a channel that deviates on a different schedule from episodic recall is exactly the shape of it.

Two things stop me putting this higher, and both deserve to be stated rather than buried.

The first is that the earliest deviation has the wrong *sign*. INV-047 says early precision/confidence **loss**. What the data show first is hypernosognosia -- people become *more* aware of their memory changes before MCI, and only later does that reverse into anosognosia. The timing matches the claim; the direction does not. That is not fatal, and there is a reading on which it is even interesting (a precision channel that first over-weights and then collapses is a more specific dynamic than monotone loss, and arguably a better fit to a control system losing calibration than to one losing signal). But it is not what the claim says, and I would rather flag it now than have a later reviewer discover the claim was written past its evidence.

The second is sharper: **amyloid predicts the awareness trajectory**. INV-047's rhetorical structure is a contrast -- the staged profile is asserted to be *not* explained by diffuse amyloid damage, on the grounds that diffuse damage predicts uniform degradation. This paper shows amyloid burden predicting a specifically-timed, staged metacognitive change, including in cognitively normal participants. So the dichotomy the claim leans on is not clean. Diffuse pathology and staged phenotype are not the mutually exclusive alternatives INV-047 treats them as; a spatially diffuse insult can perfectly well produce a staged phenotype if the downstream functions have different reserve. That is a real problem for the claim's second half and it is why I have marked this mixed rather than supporting.

The transfer caveat is the usual one for this claim and it is not small. "Awareness" here is a self-minus-informant discrepancy on a clinical scale. INV-047 imports "precision/confidence" from predictive processing. Those are not the same quantity, and the discrepancy measure additionally conflates metacognitive precision with reporting bias, insight, and informant accuracy. Confidence 0.68: the measurement is strong and the timing genuinely informative, but it lands on INV-047 as a partial correction rather than a clean confirmation -- which, given the backlog flagged this claim for a directional conflict, is probably the useful outcome.
