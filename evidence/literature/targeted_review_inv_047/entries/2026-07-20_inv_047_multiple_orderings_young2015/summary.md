# Young et al. (2015) -- there may not be one sequence to explain

According to PubMed, this paper takes the event-based model's central simplification -- that every subject follows the same event sequence -- and relaxes it two ways. A generalised Mallows model lets subjects deviate from a central ordering with an estimated variance; a Dirichlet process mixture of such models allows distinct clusters, each with its own sequence and its own variance. Fitted to ADNI data by Gibbs sampling, the Mallows model estimates substantially more cross-subject variation in ordering than the classic model does, and the mixture model detects three subgroups of the sporadic AD population following different event sequences. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-319-19992-4_56)

I included this because it goes after something INV-047 assumes rather than something it asserts, and that is usually where claims are most exposed.

INV-047 says "*the* specific staged cognitive decline profile of Alzheimer's disease", and then asks what explains it. Every word of that presupposes there is one profile. If sporadic AD is in fact a mixture of subpopulations with different orderings, then the singular profile is a population artefact -- an average that no individual patient traverses -- and the thing INV-047 sets out to explain by reverse-mapping onto a sleep phase dependency ordering may not be a real object at the individual level. That is a more serious problem for the claim than a disagreement about which stage comes first, because a wrong ordering can be corrected while a non-existent explanandum cannot.

There is a second, quieter implication worth recording. The classic single-sequence model *understates* ordering variance. Any staging result INV-047 or its dependencies lean on that came from the classic model carries more confidence in its recovered order than the data support. That is a systematic, directional bias, not noise, and it should probably temper how hard the whole INV-045/046/047 cluster leans on published staging orders.

I have kept this at 0.58 and marked it a weakener rather than a refutation, for a reason that genuinely limits it: the events here are imaging and biomarker abnormalities in brain regions, not the cognitive stages INV-047 enumerates. Heterogeneity in the ordering of regional atrophy does not entail heterogeneity in the ordering of cognitive failure. It is quite possible for the cognitive phenotype to be more canalised than the neural substrate underneath it -- several different atrophy routes converging on a similar experiential sequence is not an exotic hypothesis, and would be rather interesting if true. So the challenge here is by analogy, in form rather than in content. It is also a conference methods paper, with the lighter clinical validation that implies.

The constructive reading: before INV-047 can be adjudicated at all, someone has to establish whether the cognitive staging it describes is a single sequence or a mixture. That is a well-posed empirical question with an existing methodology attached -- this paper supplies the method -- which makes it a probe worth running rather than an unknown to be hedged around. It is also the question that would tell us whether the conflict flagged against Firth's memory-first ordering is a real disagreement or two subgroups being compared.
