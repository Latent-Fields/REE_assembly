# Mahowald et al. (2024) -- Dissociating language and thought in large language models

**What it is.** A Trends in Cognitive Sciences review that splits "linguistic competence" in two. *Formal* competence is knowing the rules and patterns of a language. *Functional* competence is using language to reason, model situations, track goals and read other minds. The split is grounded in human neuroscience: the first depends on the language network, the second on other systems (the multiple-demand network, the theory-of-mind network, the default network). The review then surveys LLM behaviour. LLMs are close to human-level on formal competence. Their functional competence is uneven and often needs fine-tuning or external tools.

**Why it matters for ARC-114.** ARC-114 exists to answer one objection: "LLMs reason, so language suffices." This paper answers it in the field's own terms. What emerges from language alone is mostly formal competence plus whatever reasoning traces language happens to carry. The functional work that looks like reasoning is exactly what does not come for free, which fits language being an interface onto reasoning rather than its seat. The paper's recommendation, modular systems that couple a language model to separate reasoning machinery, is close to how a REE language stream would sit on top of E1/E2/E3.

**Honest caveats.** The LLM half ages fast. Reasoning-trained models since 2024 have narrowed some of the gaps catalogued here. If functional competence keeps improving in pure next-token systems without added modules, the review's engineering prediction weakens. That would not overturn the human-substrate evidence, but it would weaken the "language alone cannot" framing. ARC-114 should rest on the human dissociation evidence (Kean 2026, Fedorenko 2016/2024) and use this paper as the bridge to the LLM case, not as its foundation. The compensation leg is again not addressed.

**Confidence.** 0.66: the best-aimed paper for ARC-114's positioning job, discounted for time-sensitivity.
