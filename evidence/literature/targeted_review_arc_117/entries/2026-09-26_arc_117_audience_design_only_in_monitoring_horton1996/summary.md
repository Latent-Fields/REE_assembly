# Horton & Keysar (1996) -- When do speakers take into account common ground?

**What they did.** Speakers described objects to a listener in a referential communication task. The researchers varied what the two parties shared, via physical co-presence, and whether the speaker was under time pressure. The question was when the listener's perspective gets into the utterance: at the planning stage, or only later as a check on what has already been planned.

**What they found.** Without a deadline, descriptions took the listener's shared knowledge into account. With a deadline, they did not. The authors concluded that audience design is not part of initial utterance planning. Speakers plan from their own perspective and then *monitor* the plan for violations of common ground.

**What it means for ARC-117.** This is the most direct pressure on ARC-117 in this pull, and it is mixed rather than against. On the one hand, the receiver model plainly does work, because unhurried speakers tailor what they say. On the other, the first draft looks sender-local. Horton and Keysar's speaker is a proposal generator with a receiver-model editor attached, and the editor only runs when there is time. ARC-117 as worded names a single selection objective and cannot tell the two architectures apart.

**Why this matters for the falsifier.** It makes a concrete prediction about the planned experiment. The receiver-objective advantage should depend on how much computation or time the sender has. With a tight budget, the two arms might tie, and that would not refute a claim about what selection converges to given resources. I would add a budget sweep to the design so that a tie at low budget is not read as FAIL. The broader literature has since contested "monitoring only", with earlier partner effects found under some conditions. So I hold the specific architecture loosely, but the budget-dependence seems robust.

**Confidence.** 0.5. Old and contested, but it shows where the falsifier could mislead.
