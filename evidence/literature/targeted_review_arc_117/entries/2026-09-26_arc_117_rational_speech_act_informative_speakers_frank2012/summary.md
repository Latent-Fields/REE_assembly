# Frank & Goodman (2012) -- Predicting pragmatic reasoning in language games

**What they did.** They set up tiny reference games: a few objects, a few features, one word to pick them out. Then they built a model in which the speaker chooses a word to be maximally informative to a listener, and the listener does Bayesian inference over which object the speaker must have meant. This became the Rational Speech Act (RSA) framework.

**What they found.** The model fitted human speaker and listener judgments closely, with no free parameters. People behaved as if speakers pick the word that best lets the listener recover the referent, and listeners reason about that choice.

**What it means for ARC-117.** ARC-117 says message selection minimises the receiver's residual uncertainty: an utterance approximates a task-relevant sufficient statistic for the receiver, not a compression of what the sender found salient. RSA's speaker is that objective written down. In information-theoretic terms, to choose the word that maximises the listener's posterior on the intended object is to choose the word that most reduces the listener's surprisal. And it makes the kind of prediction ARC-117 prides itself on. The model says *which* word gets said, not just that talking happens.

**Where the mapping thins.** The reference games are closed worlds with a handful of objects, while ARC-117's sender states are richer and partly affective. The listener model is written by the modellers. An agent would need to learn it, and that learned receiver model is exactly the missing substrate the claim names. Nor is this the falsifier's comparison. The paper does not pit a receiver-objective speaker against a sender-salience speaker under a bound channel. In many scenes the two coincide, because the most distinctive feature is often also the most eye-catching.

**Confidence.** 0.6. This is the right formal precedent, with a strong fit and a large confirming literature behind it, but it was not tested against the alternative ARC-117 must beat.
