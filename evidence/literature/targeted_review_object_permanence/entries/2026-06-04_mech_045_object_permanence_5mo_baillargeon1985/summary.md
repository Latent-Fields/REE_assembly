# Object permanence in five-month-old infants (Baillargeon, Spelke & Wasserman 1985)

## What the paper did

Baillargeon, Spelke and Wasserman habituated 5.5-month-old infants to a screen rotating
through a 180-degree arc, like a drawbridge. They then placed a box in the screen's path
and showed two test events. In the *possible* event the screen rotated up until it reached
the (now hidden) box and stopped, as a solid box would require. In the *impossible* event
the screen rotated through its full 180-degree arc, as though the box had ceased to exist.
Infants looked reliably longer at the impossible event. The standard inference: the infants
were surprised, and to be surprised they must have been representing a box they could no
longer see — and representing it as solid, occupying space, and obstructing the screen.

The result mattered because it cut against Piaget's claim that object permanence is a late
sensorimotor achievement, knowable only through manual search (the infant lifting a cloth to
retrieve a hidden toy, which fails reliably before ~8 months). Baillargeon's infants
demonstrated a representation of the hidden object roughly three months before they could
act on one.

## Why it grounds MECH-045 / ARC-006

This is the cleanest empirical anchor for the *existence* of the capability MECH-045 asserts:
an entity's representation must survive its disappearance from the senses and keep
constraining what the agent expects. ARC-006's "persistent, bindable structures" presupposes
exactly this. Crucially for the REE object-representation thread, the study separates
**permanence-as-representation** from **permanence-as-motivation**. REE today has only the
latter, well-formed: the SD-039 / MECH-292 / MECH-293 ghost-goal bank stores a goal-value
*snapshot* that persists when its spatial anchor is not perceived. That delivers "I still want
the thing that was over there"; it does not deliver "*that* object is still there, behind the
occluder, and is solid." Baillargeon's drawbridge is precisely a test of the second, and it is
the second that a true permanence pillar (the proposed ARC-080 child) would have to build.

## Limitations and the honest caveat

Two cautions keep this from being a slam-dunk. First, the drawbridge paradigm specifically was
later shown to carry a low-level confound: infants may simply prefer the larger, more novel
180-degree rotation, with no box representation needed at all (Rivera, Wakeley & Langer 1999;
Cashon & Cohen 2000). The permanence conclusion survives because the wider violation-of-
expectation corpus replicates it with cleaner designs — but it does not survive on this study
alone, and I have weighted confidence accordingly. Second, and more important for us: a
looking-time difference tells us *something* about the hidden object is represented. It does
not tell us *what* — a token-instance object-file, a spatial expectation, or a physical-event
prediction are all consistent with longer looking. The paradigm cannot, by itself, adjudicate
the token-vs-type question that the object-representation memo identifies as the central
representational gap. For that, Xu & Carey (1996) is the load-bearing entry.

## Confidence reasoning

I set confidence at 0.70. Source quality is high in the sense of foundational status and broad
conceptual replication, but the specific paradigm's confound holds it below 0.8. Mapping
fidelity is moderate (0.68): the paper establishes that permanence-as-expectation exists very
early, which supports the *existence* of MECH-045's buffer, but the bridge from infant
looking-time to a machine-readable persistence buffer consumed by planning is conceptual, not
measured. Transfer risk is moderate. The entry's job is to license the claim that the
permanence pillar is a real representational primitive worth building — not to specify how.
