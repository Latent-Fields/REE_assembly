# Tucker et al. 2018 — the state-value baseline is what actually reduces policy-gradient variance

ICML 2018 · [arXiv:1802.10031](https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.10031).

**What the paper did.** A wave of 2017–18 papers had proposed baselines that depend on both state *and action*, claiming large variance reductions and sample-efficiency gains over the classical state-dependent (value-function) baseline. Tucker and colleagues decompose the variance of the policy-gradient estimator analytically, reproduce those prior results, and audit the accompanying open-source code. Their finding: the action-dependent baselines do *not* in fact reduce variance beyond a plain state-value baseline on the standard benchmarks; the apparent gains came from subtle implementation deviations, not from the method. They then propose a small, honest change to the value-function parameterisation instead.

**Why it extends the theoretical anchor for MECH-475.** Sutton et al. 2000 (already on file in `targeted_review_actor_critic_action_learning`) gives the claim its foundational statement: the value baseline's *function is variance reduction*, and an uninformative baseline reduces nothing. Tucker sharpens that from a functional statement into a quantified one — it localises the variance reduction *in the state-value baseline itself* and shows that nothing fancier is buying real variance reduction on top of it. Read against MECH-475 this is the other side of the coin the claim is standing on. REE measured `std(V)/std(G) = 0.041` and a pre-reward-vs-far separation of 0.016: a critic that barely moves with the return, i.e. a flat state-value baseline. Tucker says that is exactly the component responsible for subtracting variance from the advantage — so when it is flat, the variance it should have removed stays in the gradient, and the actor is updated by an advantage dominated by noise. "More gradient means more drift" is the behavioural reading of a policy gradient whose variance-reducing baseline has gone silent.

**The honest boundary.** The paper's headline result is a *debunking of action-dependent baselines*, not a direct manipulation of baseline informativeness with competence as the outcome. It grounds MECH-475's variance *mechanism* — the state-value baseline's primacy in variance reduction — but it does not itself demonstrate that flattening the baseline makes competence fall below control. And its benchmarks are dense-reward continuous-control tasks where a good state-value baseline is fittable; REE's harder problem is a critic that is flat *on the policy's own state distribution*, which is a more severe condition than an imperfectly-fit baseline. So I have recorded it as `supports` at moderate confidence, weighted for the theoretical mechanism it secures rather than for a direct empirical hit.

**Confidence.** 0.60, `supports`. Source quality is high (rigorous variance analysis, careful reproduction, well cited) and it is in-domain, so transfer risk is low (0.35). Mapping fidelity is 0.55: it evidences the state-value baseline's variance-reducing role *indirectly*, through the mirage result, rather than by manipulating informativeness head-on. Its pairing with Fujimoto 2018 is deliberate — Tucker supplies the *why* (the baseline is the variance reducer), Fujimoto supplies the *consequence* (a bad critic actively worsens the actor and fixing it reverses the sign).
