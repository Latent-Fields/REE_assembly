# Kidd & Castano 2013, and what happened next

Science 342(6156):377-380, DOI 10.1126/science.1239918, PMID 24091705. The original report was that reading a short passage of literary fiction, as against popular fiction or nonfiction, immediately improved theory-of-mind performance. It was widely covered and is the most quotable single result in the fiction-and-social-cognition literature.

It did not hold up. Panero and colleagues published a direct replication finding no such effect, and their reply -- "No support for the claim that literary fiction uniquely and immediately improves theory of mind", Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 112(3):e5-e8, PMID 28221090 -- records that when Kidd and Castano reanalysed the replication data in the manner they had themselves argued was correct, the original pattern still did not emerge. Subsequent replication attempts across several groups have been mixed at best.

I have banked this at confidence 0.3 with direction mixed, and the low number is the point of the entry. ARC-141's own notes say the framing claim becomes unfalsifiable if every beneficial effect of fiction is counted as evidence for it, and its evidence companion says no literature demonstrates the whole mechanism. This entry is the concrete instance: the most citable beneficial effect of fiction is not reliable, and a future write-up that reaches for it should reach for the replication failure at the same time.

What survives is the correlational literature, principally lifetime print-exposure measures correlating with social-cognitive performance. That is real but it is open to the obvious reverse reading -- people who are better at reading other minds enjoy fiction more -- and nothing in the banked material rules it out. So the package's Tier-2 item about fiction and social cognition should be treated as suggestive association, not as support. ARC-141's empirical content is discharged through MECH-542, which is what its registration already says.
