# Altmann & Trafton 2002 — why a preserved trace still needs a re-priming trigger

This is the paper that gives MECH-524 its clearest formal ancestor, and it comes from a completely different tradition than the sleep and mind-wandering literature elsewhere in this pull — it is a cognitive-architecture modeling paper, fit to human performance data on Tower of Hanoi, of all things.

Altmann and Trafton's move was to refuse the idea that a suspended goal needs a dedicated "goal stack" data structure at all. Instead they model it with the same general memory constructs used everywhere else: activation and associative priming. A goal, once encoded, has an activation level; that level decays over time and is further degraded by interference from other, unrelated goals encoded in the meantime; and it can be boosted again — reactivated — by retrieval cues that match its content. They formalize this into three constraints (interference, strengthening, priming) and fit the resulting model against latency and error data from Tower of Hanoi, a puzzle whose entire structure is repeated suspension and resumption of subgoals as the solver works down through the disk-moving sequence. The fit is good, and critically, the three constraints are separable — you can see interference costing time, strengthening (how well the goal was encoded in the first place) protecting against that cost, and priming from the right cue recovering activation that decay had eaten away.

For MECH-524 this does two things at once. First, it is independent formal grounding for the preserve-don't-erase framing REE already uses for SD-039 — a suspended goal representation is not deleted, it decays. But second, and this is the part I think is easy to miss if you only remember "Zeigarnik-analog, goals persist": decay is real. A parked goal that sits un-reprimed long enough, or that gets heavily interleaved with unrelated intervening activity, becomes progressively harder to retrieve even though nothing was ever erased. That is exactly why MECH-524 proposes an *explicit* trigger — the spare-compute signal or sleep-entry event that re-seeds MECH-293's awake probes and MECH-285's sleep replay toward the interruption_snapshot — rather than assuming the parked slot will simply be noticed again on its own. The priming constraint is the direct analogue of what the snapshot is *for*: it is the cue payload the trigger delivers, and without cue content that actually matches what needs to be retrieved, priming does not happen and resumption stays slow or fails.

Where I want to be careful is the substrate gap. This is a symbolic production-system architecture (ACT-R) fit to a discrete, fully observable puzzle domain; "activation" here is a single scalar memory-strength number, not a structured latent state like z_world or z_self, and there is nothing in this paper that tells me the richer interruption_snapshot payload (CEM stage, rule-arbitration weights, what-was-attempted) is the right content for the cue — that specificity is a REE-side extension the paper doesn't test. What it does robustly establish is the *shape* of the problem: persistence without decay-awareness is not enough, and resumption needs cues, not just presence. That shape transfers cleanly even where the numbers don't.
