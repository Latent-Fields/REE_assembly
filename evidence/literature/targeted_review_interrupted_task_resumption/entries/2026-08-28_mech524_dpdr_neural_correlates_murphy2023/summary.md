# Murphy 2023 — the clearest human case of capacity present, engagement absent

Of the four papers in this batch, this is the one I'd point to first if someone asked "does the dissociative pole actually happen in humans, concretely." Depersonalization/derealization disorder (DPDR) is, on the description in this review, close to a direct existence-proof of the specific phenotype MECH-524's notes already name: work — in the human case, engagement with one's own life and surroundings rather than a specific parked task — that never gets actively resumed even though nothing about the person's basic processing capacity is gone.

Murphy's review synthesizes what's known about DPDR's neural correlates within a trauma-related framework: detachment ranging on a continuum from a transient, ordinary reaction to acute stress up through a persistent, highly debilitating disorder. Importantly, the stress-response machinery itself — autonomic function, the HPA axis, the classic defense cascade — is not obviously offline in DPDR; if anything, dysregulated activation of exactly these systems is part of the proposed picture. What the review foregrounds instead is disintegration at the level of large-scale brain networks and, specifically, of the body schema — the sense that one's own body and experience belong to oneself. That's the part I want to sit with: DPDR does not read as an incapacity story. It reads as a story where the systems that would ordinarily let someone re-engage with what matters to them are present and functioning, and the re-engagement still doesn't happen. That is a remarkably close description of MECH-524's restrictive pole as already written — capacity abundant, resumption absent, indistinguishable from an entry that was never worth resuming in the first place.

I don't want to lean on this more than it will bear, though, and the honest caveat is a structural one rather than a quality-of-evidence one. DPDR, as this and every other paper in this search describes it, is tonic — a continuous, trait-like detachment from self and world, not an episodic failure to reseed one specific parked goal while everything else proceeds normally. MECH-524's trigger is specified at the level of a single interruption_snapshot: a spare-compute or sleep-entry signal that either does or doesn't reseed one particular piece of paused context. If REE's own dissociative pole turned out to look more like DPDR — a global suppression of the reseeding trigger across the entire MECH-292 bank, rather than a per-entry mis-trigger — that would be a meaningfully different and more concerning failure than what the claim's falsifier design currently tests for. The falsifier as written asks whether one recently-interrupted entry resumes differently than a merely-stale one; it doesn't yet ask whether the trigger itself has gone globally quiet. That's a design question worth carrying forward, not one this paper answers.

On source quality, I'll be plain about the ceiling here: this is a single-author narrative review in a mid-tier clinical neuroscience journal, not a meta-analysis, and it says outright that DPDR's precise neurocircuit-to-symptom mapping remains incompletely resolved even for the human case it's actually about. I chose it over a couple of higher-circulation general DPDR reviews turned up in the same search specifically because it's framed around network-level disintegration rather than epidemiology or treatment, which is the more mechanistically relevant angle for this claim — but if this entry is ever worth strengthening, a systematic or meta-analytic DPDR neuroimaging review would be the natural upgrade.
