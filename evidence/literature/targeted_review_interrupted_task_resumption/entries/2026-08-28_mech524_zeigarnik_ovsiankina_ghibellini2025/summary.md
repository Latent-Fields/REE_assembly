# Ghibellini & Meier 2025 — the effect we should actually be naming after

The chip that spawned this pull calls the mechanism "Zeigarnik-analog," and the first thing this paper does is talk me out of that name.

Ghibellini and Meier meta-analyze the modern literature on interrupted-task memory and resumption, and the headline finding is a correction: once you set aside Zeigarnik's own 1927 data, there is no reliable recall advantage for interrupted tasks over completed ones — the ratio of interrupted-to-completed recall across modern studies comes out close to 1.0. The classic "unfinished tasks are remembered better" story does not hold up. What does hold up, and hold up more reliably, is a *different* and less-cited effect: Ovsiankina's (1928) finding that people show a general behavioral tendency to spontaneously resume an interrupted task, given the chance, independent of whether they remember it any better. The authors' framing is sharp: Zeigarnik is a claim about memory; Ovsiankina is a claim about behavior; and in the modern data it is the behavioral claim that survives.

That distinction turns out to matter a great deal for how I should read MECH-524. The mechanism I am registering is not a claim that a parked goal's *trace* becomes intrinsically stickier or more memorable while parked — REE already has a mechanism for that (MECH-292's ranked bank, scored by wanting, staleness, and recoverability). MECH-524 is a claim about whether *spare processing capacity gets spontaneously reallocated* toward the interrupted goal when the opportunity arises — reseeding MECH-293's awake probes and MECH-285's sleep replay toward a preserved snapshot. That is squarely an Ovsiankina-shaped claim, not a Zeigarnik-shaped one. So I am citing this paper as grounding for MECH-524 while explicitly declining to lean on the "Zeigarnik effect" language in the mechanism's own title notes — citing the well-supported resumption tendency and flagging the memory-strength framing as the weaker, largely non-replicating cousin.

I want to be honest about what this paper does *not* give me, too. Its resumption measure is free-choice human behavior during unstructured downtime in a lab task — closer to "given nothing else to do, which task do people drift back to" than to a triggered, capacity-gated reseeding of specific probe and replay subsystems. And the authors note historical moderators — ego-involvement, expectation of resuming, the experimenter's perceived authority, emotional stakes — that inflated the original 1927 effect and are largely absent from modern, more relaxed lab settings. REE has no stated analogue for any of those, and I should not assume they wash out; if the eventual falsifier finds the resumption tendency itself is conditional on something like affective stakes, that is exactly the kind of moderator this literature would have predicted and MECH-524's trigger design should be able to absorb.

Net effect: mixed evidence_direction is the honest call. It weakens the naive memory-based framing this claim family's working name invited, and supports the more specific, behaviorally grounded resumption-tendency reading that the actual mechanism proposes.
