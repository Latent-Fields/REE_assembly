# Lynn et al. 2022 — the restrictive pole is probably a family, not a mechanism

This entry plays the same role in this directory that Ghibellini & Meier 2025 already plays for MECH-524's naming choice: it is here to talk me out of reaching for the first plausible-sounding single mechanism and pretending that settles the question.

Lynn, Polizzi, Merckelbach, Chiu, Maxwell, van Heugten, and Lilienfeld's Annual Review piece surveys thirty-plus years of the dissociation field arguing with itself over two competing accounts — the posttraumatic model (dissociation as a direct consequence of overwhelming trauma) and the sociocognitive model (dissociation as a socially and culturally shaped enactment, not a trauma-caused state). Their contribution is to reject both as sufficient on their own and propose something more encompassing: dissociation and dissociative disorders as failures of ordinarily adaptive systems, with the actual causal texture spread across several interacting factors — sleep disturbance, impaired self-regulation and inhibition of negative cognition and affect, hyperassociation and difficulty shifting attentional set, and deficits in reality testing, source attribution, and metacognition. Read that list next to the structural-dissociation account in this directory's van der Hart et al. 2005 entry, which grounds the restrictive pole in a specific mechanism — capacity chronically captured by a trauma-fixated part — and the contrast is the point of including this paper at all. The trauma-fixation account is not wrong, exactly; it's one candidate among several the field has not converged on.

That has a direct, somewhat uncomfortable implication for MECH-524's own notes. As written, the restrictive pole is a single sentence: the trigger is stuck too restrictive, or the snapshot was never captured. This review's whole argument is that the same observable phenotype — a person who doesn't reengage with unresolved material despite apparent capacity to — can arise from mechanistically distinct routes, and treating it as one mechanism is exactly the mistake the field spent decades correcting itself out of. Translated into REE's own vocabulary: an impaired-inhibition route would look like rule-arbitration-weight capture unrelated to trauma specifically; an attentional-set-shifting deficit maps fairly directly onto a spare-compute/trigger-detection failure; a metacognitive/source-monitoring deficit is a different failure again — not failing to act on free capacity, but failing to notice the capacity is free in the first place. None of that is in this review explicitly as an REE mapping — that step is mine, not the authors' — but the underlying claim that these are genuinely distinct candidate mechanisms is theirs, and it is well-supported.

I want to be precise about what confidence this earns. Source quality is high — this is exactly the kind of comprehensive, well-cited, senior-authored synthesis a governance-level literature grounding should lean on. But its contribution to MECH-524 specifically is corrective and contextual rather than direct positive evidence for any part of the mechanism's actual design — it tells me the restrictive pole is probably a family of failure routes rather than committing to which one REE's own trigger design most resembles. Mixed evidence_direction is the honest call for exactly that reason: it weakens the single-mechanism framing the van der Hart et al. 2005 entry offers, and supports treating the pole as underspecified rather than grounded, which is closer to where MECH-524's own notes should land after this pull than a clean single citation would have left it.
