# van der Hart, Nijenhuis & Steele 2005 — the un-resumed work isn't necessarily unattended, it may be outcompeted

MECH-524's notes describe the restrictive pole in a specific, almost clinical sentence: work that "never gets actively resumed even once capacity is abundant, indistinguishable from a MECH-292 entry that was never worth resuming at all." Reading van der Hart, Nijenhuis, and Steele's account of structural dissociation in complex PTSD, that sentence turns out to be a good description of a real clinical phenomenon — but the mechanism the literature actually proposes is not quite the one I'd have guessed from the sentence alone, and I think that gap is worth stating rather than smoothing over.

The theory of structural dissociation of the personality holds that trauma divides the systems that constitute a person into distinct parts: an "apparently normal part" (ANP) that avoids the traumatic memories and gets on with ordinary daily-life functioning, and one or more "emotional parts" (EP) that stay fixated on the traumatic experience and the defensive action tendencies — fight, flight, freeze — that came with it. In complex PTSD, the authors argue, this division is more elaborate: more parts, more entrenched. What I notice reading this against MECH-524 is that the phenotype — normal life going forward while something stays unresumed — is real and well-attested. But the proposed mechanism is not "capacity never returns to the parked content." It's closer to "capacity is chronically captured by something else." The EP isn't idle while the ANP fails to notice a freed-up moment; the EP is actively, persistently occupying processing resources with its own fixation, at the expense of whatever the ANP would otherwise be free to resume.

That distinction matters for how MECH-524's own falsifier design should be read. The claim's notes propose testing the restrictive pole with a case where "snapshot exists but no active trigger... recently-interrupted entries resume no faster than merely stale entries reached by MECH-292's ordinary cadence." That test assumes the trigger simply under-fires for the entry in question. The structural-dissociation literature describes something structurally different: not under-firing in general, but a trigger (or some unmodeled rival process) that fires abundantly for one dominant, unresolved snapshot while starving every other parked entry of the same spare capacity. If REE's dissociative pole turned out to look like this — one interruption_snapshot monopolizing MECH-293/MECH-285 reseeding while everything else in the MECH-292 bank goes permanently unresumed — the currently-proposed falsifier wouldn't catch it, because it's testing for absence of a signal rather than for a skewed distribution of it.

I want to be careful about what I'm and am not claiming here. This is a clinical theory about dissociation BETWEEN systems that constitute a whole personality, not about a single agent's per-entry task-resumption cadence, and it's a conceptual article by the theory's own originators rather than an experimental test of it — a theory that has real standing in the trauma field and also real critics (see this directory's Lynn et al. 2022 entry for the counter-perspective, which explicitly argues against over-committing to any single-mechanism trauma account). I'm not claiming REE's MECH-292 bank is at risk of spontaneously developing something like a fixated "emotional part." I am claiming that if MECH-524's restrictive pole is ever specified further, the honest reading of this literature is that it should at least ask whether the failure looks like uniform under-triggering or like monopolized capacity — because the clinical literature grounding the pole describes the second, not the first.
