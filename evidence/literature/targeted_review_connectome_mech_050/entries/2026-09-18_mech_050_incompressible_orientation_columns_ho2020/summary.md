# Ho et al. (2020) -- Orientation preference maps in Microcebus murinus: size-invariant design principles in primate V1

**Claim tested:** MECH-050 (functional locality supports attribution without requiring anatomical columns)
**Direction:** weakens | **Confidence:** 0.65

## Why this entry is here

Four entries in this pull support MECH-050. This one is the reason the claim should not be promoted
on the strength of those four, and I have gone looking for it deliberately: MECH-050 was registered
from a thought document, has no experimental evidence, and until now no literature either. A pull
that returned only confirmation would be worth very little as governance evidence.

## What the paper did

The question is well posed. Orientation preference maps are prominent in primates and carnivores
and absent in rodents, who do orientation selectivity salt-and-pepper style. Is that because
orientation columns have a minimum viable size, below which a small V1 simply cannot host them? To
find out, Ho and colleagues went to the grey mouse lemur -- a 60-gram nocturnal prosimian, a primate
roughly the mass of a rat -- and imaged V1 chronically with intrinsic signals.

The lemur has robust orientation columns, arranged in pinwheels. Column size was only *marginally*
smaller than the macaque's, across an enormous difference in brain size, which the authors read as
these circuit elements being nearly incompressible. The pinwheel layout was well described by a
common mathematical design of primate V1 organisation. And the cost: to fit the maps in, mouse lemur
V1 occupies about one fifth of the entire cortical surface, one of the largest V1-to-cortex ratios
of any primate. Their conclusion is that primate-type visual cortical organisation is *constrained
by* a size limitation, and that it may have arisen by disruptive innovation rather than gradual
change.

## How this bears on MECH-050 -- and why it bites

MECH-050's second sentence is the one under pressure: "Columnar spatial bundling in biology is
treated as a metabolic or developmental optimisation, not a computational primitive."

Take that seriously as a causal claim and it makes a prediction. Under severe size and energy
pressure -- a 60-gram nocturnal animal is about as pressured as mammals get -- an optimisation
should be relaxed or abandoned. The alternative was available and demonstrably adequate: rodents of
the same body size do orientation selectivity without any maps at all. If columns were merely a
convenient way of shortening wire, this animal had every reason to drop them.

It kept them. And it paid for them with a fifth of its cortex.

That is a revealed preference, and it points the wrong way for MECH-050's stated reason. Something
other than wire economy is setting a floor on column size, and evolution is spending scarce cortical
area rather than crossing it. Add the authors' own framing -- a size-*constrained* organisation,
possibly of discontinuous evolutionary origin -- and the natural reading is that the map is a
distinct computational strategy rather than an optimised packaging of a shared one. A circuit
element that cannot be scaled continuously is a poor candidate for "contingent packaging".

There is a convergence worth noting too. The pinwheel layout here fits a common mathematical design
of primate V1, which lands alongside Kaschube et al. (2010, *Science*, doi:10.1126/science.1194869)
and their conclusion that self-organisation has canalised orientation maps into a single common
design. Two independent lines now support a common design principle for map layout. "Canalised into
a single common design" is not what biological contingency looks like.

So the honest summary is that MECH-050's *justification* is unsettled at best and probably wrong,
even where its *conclusion* may well survive.

## What this does NOT show, and the distinction matters

I want to be careful not to let the weakening travel further than it should, because the asymmetry
here mirrors the one running through the supporting entries.

What is challenged is the claim that columnar geometry is a metabolic or developmental optimisation.
What is *not* challenged is that a substrate can realise selective representations through functional
locality alone. Rodents still do it. This paper does not dispute that; it asks why primates decline
the rodent route, which is a different question. REE has no wiring cost, no radial developmental
migration, no cortical-area budget, and no evolutionary history to be constrained by -- so the
constraint the paper identifies has no counterpart in the substrate. Transfer risk is the highest in
this pull for exactly that reason.

And the study measures map *structure*. No behavioural or performance consequence of retaining the
maps was assessed. "Evolution declined to give this up" is as far as the inference goes; it is
suggestive of a function and is not a demonstration of one. Small n is inherent to chronic imaging
in a rare prosimian.

The right disposition, I think, is this: record the entry as weakening MECH-050's causal framing,
keep the claim at `candidate`, and treat the framing sentence about metabolic optimisation as the
part needing revision -- it is currently stated with more confidence than the literature supports.
The question REE actually needs answered is not "are columns metabolic?" but "does bounded-update
functional locality deliver error attribution?", and nothing in this pull, supporting or weakening,
touches that. That is an experimental question, and its absence is the real finding here.

## Confidence reasoning

Source quality 0.85: technically demanding chronic imaging in a rare primate, quantitative
cross-species comparison, strong authorship (Wolf, Angelucci, Huber), Current Biology -- discounted
for small n. Mapping fidelity 0.68: squarely on MECH-050's causal clause, only obliquely on its
engineering conclusion. Transfer risk 0.4, the highest here, because the conclusion is explicitly
primate-specific and size-constraint-driven and REE is subject to neither. Aggregate 0.65 in the
weakening direction -- enough to block a promotion that rested on the framing, not enough to
overturn the design decision.
