# Instructional control of reinforcement learning (Doll, Jacobs, Sanfey & Frank, 2009)

MECH-011 asserts, first among its four mechanisms, that language "biases priors during model update." That is the kind of sentence that sounds obviously true and is therefore worth interrogating rather than nodding at. What would it look like if it were false? Presumably: an agent told something inaccurate would be dragged back to the truth by experience, the verbal prior acting as a starting point that data erodes. Doll and colleagues built precisely that test, and the answer is more unsettling than the claim as written.

Participants performed a probabilistic selection task after being told which stimulus carried the highest (or lowest) reinforcement probability. The instruction was false. Experience was available, abundant, and contradictory. Choice behaviour followed the instruction anyway. The interesting work is in the model comparison that follows: two neural-circuit accounts were pitted against each other, fitted with Q-learning and Bayesian methods, and the winner was a confirmation-bias architecture in which a prefrontal/hippocampal system *trains* the striatal reinforcement system — amplifying outcomes that agree with the instruction, damping those that do not.

That distinction is the reason this paper earns a high mapping fidelity for a claim it never mentions. MECH-011 says language biases the prior. Doll et al. show that in the brain, the bias does not sit in the prior at all; it sits in the gate on what counts as evidence. The difference matters enormously for REE. A prior is a quantity that data can overwhelm — it is, in the Bayesian sense, self-correcting given enough observations. A corrupted update rule is not, because the corruption operates on the very signal that would correct it. This is what makes a rationalisation loop a *loop* rather than merely a mistake, and MECH-011's own hedge about "rationalisation loops in purely linguistic agents" turns out to have a concrete neural mechanism behind it that the claim does not currently cite.

For REE the design implication is sharp and, usefully, falsifiable. When a symbol channel is eventually built (and the substrate audit for this same claim confirms none exists in `ree_core/` today), the architectural question is not whether language may write to priors — MECH-011 says it should, and this paper says brains do — but whether it is permitted multiplicative control over the residue or prediction-error term. If it is, the agent acquires exactly the pathology REE says it wants to avoid: an ethical model that cannot be falsified from the inside, because the linguistic prior has already edited the harm signal that would have refuted it. That suggests a concrete invariant worth registering: the linguistic pathway may modulate *what the agent expects*, but must not modulate *what the agent feels as harm*.

Where I would hold back: this is instrumental reward learning over arbitrary stimuli, and MECH-011 is a claim about ethical learning. REE's residue is a care-marker with affective and interoceptive character, not a scalar reward statistic, and it is genuinely open whether an embodied harm signal is corruptible by instruction in the same way an abstract reward tally is. One could argue it should be *less* so, and that this asymmetry is precisely what MECH-011's "not a substitute for harm sensing" clause is gesturing at. Nothing here settles that. The honest summary is that the mechanism MECH-011 names is real, better-specified in the literature than in the claim, and demonstrated in a domain one step removed from the one REE cares about.
