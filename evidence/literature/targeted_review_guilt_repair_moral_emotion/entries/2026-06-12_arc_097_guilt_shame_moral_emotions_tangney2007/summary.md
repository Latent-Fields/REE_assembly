# Tangney, Stuewig & Mashek (2007) — Moral Emotions and Moral Behavior

**Claims grounded:** INV-081 (no-global-self-badness write), ARC-097 (guilt-as-repair routing)
**Direction:** supports · **Confidence:** 0.82

## What the paper did

This is the field's canonical review of the *self-conscious* moral emotions — shame, guilt, and embarrassment — synthesising decades of human developmental, social, and clinical work. Its central contribution is to sharpen, and then defend empirically, the distinction between shame and guilt. The authors organise the evidence around a now-standard claim: the two emotions differ not primarily in the kind of transgression that elicits them, but in the *object* of the negative evaluation. In shame, the object is the global self ("I am a bad person"); in guilt, it is a specific behaviour ("I did a bad thing"). From that single difference, a cascade of different action tendencies, attributional patterns, and downstream behaviours follows.

## Key findings relevant to the claim

The load-bearing finding for REE is the dissociation of *action tendencies*. Guilt, because it indicts a behaviour rather than the self, leaves the self intact and able to act; it reliably motivates approach-oriented, reparative responses — confession, apology, and attempts to undo the harm. Shame, because it indicts the whole self, is painful and self-focused in a way that motivates the opposite: hiding, withdrawal, avoidance, and — defensively — externalisation of blame and even anger toward others. The review further reports that guilt is the more adaptive of the two: it is positively associated with empathy and constructive conflict resolution, whereas shame-proneness predicts a range of poorer psychological and interpersonal outcomes. This is exactly the asymmetry the REE ethics-as-coherence cluster encodes when it insists that self-attributed harm route to repair (the guilt branch) rather than to global self-condemnation (the shame branch).

## How it translates to REE

ARC-097 asserts that self-attributed harm "must open repair and policy-update pathways rather than global self-condemnation," and INV-081 prohibits binding self-attributed harm to "unbounded negative self-worth," requiring instead that it bind to actions, commitments, predictions, and repair obligations. The bad-act/bad-self contrast is, almost verbatim, the design split these two claims make. Tangney et al. supply the biological-psychological warrant the project's own rule (`feedback_biology_before_formal_definitions`) asks for *before* registering such formal constructs: the guilt/shame separation is not a REE invention but a well-established feature of the human moral apparatus, and the reparative branch is the one with the better adaptive profile. The review thus grounds both the *positive* prescription (guilt → repair, ARC-097/MECH-411) and the *prohibition* (no global self-badness write, INV-081) in the same empirical structure.

## Limitations and caveats

Two honest boundaries. First, this is a narrative review of human affect measured by self-report scales and behavioural tasks; it shows the distinction exists and that the reparative branch is adaptive, but it cannot speak to whether REE's *particular* implementation — an E3-generated repair trajectory, a bounded write to a stateful self-model — is the mechanism that yields the benefit. The mapping is conceptual, not measured. Second, the review itself flags that the guilt/shame distinction is fragile in practice: "shame-fused guilt" loses the adaptive reparative signature, and guilt can become maladaptive when it generalises into chronic self-blame without a discharge route. That is a useful cautionary signal for REE rather than a contradiction — it implies the attribution ontology (ARC-096) must genuinely separate bad-act from bad-self tags, and that the repair pathway must *terminate* (the residue-release mechanism, MECH-412) rather than perseverate.
