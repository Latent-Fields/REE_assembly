# Weiner (1985) — An Attributional Theory of Achievement Motivation and Emotion

**Direction:** supports · **Confidence:** 0.70

## What the paper did

Weiner's 1985 *Psychological Review* paper is the canonical statement of attribution theory in the psychology of motivation and emotion. It synthesises a large empirical literature into a compact structural claim: when people experience an outcome (classically, success or failure), they ask *why* it happened, and the *perceived cause* — not the outcome itself — determines the emotional and motivational response. Causes are organised along a small set of dimensions: locus (internal vs external to the self), stability (over time), and controllability (with intentionality and globality as further structures). Different positions in this causal space produce different, predictable emotions, which in turn guide behaviour.

## Key findings relevant to the claim

The detail that matters for REE is Weiner's account of which attributions produce *guilt* and *shame*. These two emotions are distinguished precisely by the *type* of internal attribution. Guilt arises when a negative outcome is attributed to an internal, **controllable** cause — something one did and could have done otherwise ("I chose badly"). Shame arises when the negative outcome is attributed to an internal but **uncontrollable**, often global cause — a stable property of the self ("I am incapable / bad"). Controllability, the dimension Weiner ties most tightly to perceived personal *responsibility*, is the pivot between the two. This is, in the psychological literature, the closest thing to a law of the form *the typed structure of a causal attribution selects the moral emotion that follows it* — and it converges with the bad-act/bad-self distinction from the Tangney review by a different route: controllable-act attributions yield reparable guilt; uncontrollable-self attributions yield withdrawing shame.

## How it translates to REE

ARC-096 asserts that REE *requires a typed causal-attribution ontology* — ownership tags for self, world, body, model, commitment, other, shared, accidental, and repairable outcomes. Weiner supplies the warrant for *why* such typing must come first: in humans, you cannot get the correct affective routing without first locating the cause in attribution space. The controllable-internal vs uncontrollable-internal split is the seed of REE's self/commitment/repairable tags. And this directly conditions ARC-097: guilt-as-repair is *licensed* specifically by a controllable own-act attribution — exactly the ownership tag ARC-096 must produce before ARC-097's repair pathway can fire. The dependency chain the plan encodes (ATTRIBUTION-ONTOLOGY → GUILT-REPAIR) is, in effect, Weiner's "attribution precedes and determines emotion" turned into an architecture.

## Limitations and caveats

The honest boundary is domain. Weiner's theory is built on *achievement* attributions — reactions to success and failure on tasks — and the guilt/shame application, while explicit in the paper, is an extension within that frame rather than a dedicated theory of interpersonal moral harm. REE's intended ontology is also richer (nine-plus typed classes including OTHER and SHARED) than Weiner's locus × stability × controllability cube, so the source supports the *principle* that attribution-type governs moral-emotional routing rather than REE's specific tag set. There is also a known wrinkle the source itself wrestles with: controllability, locus, and "responsibility" are not perfectly orthogonal, so a typed ontology that treats ownership tags as cleanly separable may over-formalise a distinction the psychology treats as partially overlapping. These are reasons to map the *structure* faithfully but not to import the dimensions as if they were a settled, orthogonal basis.
