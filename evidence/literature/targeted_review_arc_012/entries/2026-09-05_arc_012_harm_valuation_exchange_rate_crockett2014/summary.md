# Crockett, Kurth-Nelson, Siegel, Dayan & Dolan (2014) -- harm has a price, and the price is strange

## What the paper did

Subjects traded real money against real electric shocks, with the recipient of the shocks varied
(self versus an anonymous other) and the direction varied (pay to reduce pain, or profit by
increasing it). The authors fitted computational models to recover, per subject, the relative value
placed on pain. The headline is that most people valued others' pain *more* than their own: they
paid more to spare another than to spare themselves, and demanded more compensation to hurt another
than to hurt themselves. The authors call this hyperaltruism. Other-affecting choices were also
slower, which they read as deliberative engagement, and subclinical psychopathic traits predicted
reduced aversion to pain for self and others alike.

## Why I have marked this mixed rather than supporting

The first half of the result is good for ARC-012. Harm to another turns out to be *priced*. It is
recoverable as a scalar weight inside an ordinary value comparison, sitting in the same space as
money, and the models that fit the behaviour are value models rather than rule models. Nothing here
looks like a deontic prohibition or a penalty applied after a decision has otherwise been reached.
If moral behaviour in humans is implemented as an exchange rate, then an architecture that declines
to add an explicit ethical cost term is not obviously missing anything -- it needs a weight, and it
has weights.

The second half is less comfortable. A shared-currency account does not straightforwardly predict
that the weight on a stranger's pain should *exceed* the weight on one's own. The plain expectation
is a discount on the distant other, not a premium. Something is setting that weight, and whatever
it is may itself be the "ethical machinery" ARC-012 wants to do without -- pushed one level up,
from the objective into the weight-setting process, which is not the same as eliminated. The RT
asymmetry compounds the ambiguity: Crockett et al. read slower other-affecting choices as
deliberative engagement, while Hutcherson et al. (2015, the companion entry in this directory)
reproduce exactly that RT signature from a single accumulator with no second process. Two
architectures claim the same observation, so the observation cannot adjudicate between them.

The psychopathy finding cuts back toward ARC-012 and is worth keeping in view. What varied with
psychopathic traits was aversion to pain for self *and* others together -- a general aversion
parameter, not a selectively ethical module that can be lesioned on its own. That is what you would
expect if there is no separate ethical term to damage.

## How this bears on ARC-012, and what it would take to settle it

The honest summary is that this paper establishes that harm-to-others enters human choice as a
quantity rather than a rule, which ARC-012 needs, and simultaneously exhibits two features
(hyperaltruism, RT asymmetry) that a separate-term architecture would happily claim. It moves the
question rather than closing it. The thing that would settle it is not more human pricing data but
a demonstration of where the weight comes from: if the weight on another's outcome can be shown to
arise from machinery E3 already runs for non-ethical reasons, ARC-012 is in good shape; if setting
it requires a representation whose only job is ethical, the term has merely been relocated.

## Confidence

0.58. The methods are strong and the topic is exactly right; the mid-range number reflects mapping
fidelity, not quality. The exchange-rate framing is also partly an artefact of a design that hands
subjects money and shocks as commensurable, so "harm has a price" is a little bit built in. And the
mechanism plausibly setting the price here -- modelling a conspecific's suffering from the inside --
is one E3 has no version of, which is why I have put transfer risk at 0.5.
