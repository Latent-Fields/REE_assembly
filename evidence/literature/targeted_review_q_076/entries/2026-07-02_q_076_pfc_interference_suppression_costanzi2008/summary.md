# Costanzi et al. (2008) — a separable prefrontal suppression operator drives interference-forgetting

**Claim tested:** Q-076 — does the *interaction* of retrieval-readiness gain (MECH-425 Line A) and content-selective suppression (MECH-425 Line B) govern the interference-vs-forgetting tradeoff?

According to PubMed ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuroscience.2008.08.026)), Costanzi and colleagues gave mice two hippocampus-dependent tasks — a place-object recognition task and a step-through inhibitory-avoidance task — that mutually interfere: learning one impairs memory for the other. The informative manipulation is the dissociation. Prefrontal-cortex lesions *completely revert* the interference-induced impairment, whereas hippocampal lesions impair both tasks (as expected for a storage substrate) and *worsen* the interference. The authors read this, following Anderson's inhibitory-control account of forgetting, as evidence that interference-induced forgetting is driven by a prefrontal inhibitory-control mechanism triggered by competition between memories — separable from, and acting on, hippocampal storage.

This grounds MECH-425's *Line B*: a content-selective retrieval-suppression operator, distinct from storage, that decides which competitors get suppressed. Q-076 crosses that operator with the Line-A reinstatement gain (grounded separately by Morishita et al. 2026 in this same directory). Where the Morishita paper supplies the content-blind gain factor, Costanzi supplies the content-selective suppression factor and — crucially — shows it is *dissociable from storage*: knock out the prefrontal operator and the interference goes away without the memories themselves being lost. That double dissociation (PFC lesion → less forgetting; hippocampal lesion → more forgetting) is close in shape to the Line-A-vs-Line-B lesion logic MECH-425's own `what_would_answer` proposes.

I hold confidence at 0.52 for three honest reasons. First, it grounds only one of Q-076's two factors — it does nothing to demonstrate the *interaction* with gain, which is the actual content of the claim. Second, the manipulation is a total lesion, not a graded suppression parameter, so it cannot show how Line B trades off against Line A as their settings vary — it establishes that the operator exists and is separable, not the shape of the interaction surface. Third, it is a mouse two-task interference paradigm scored behaviourally, not a retrieval-time design that tracks individual competing traces, so the read-side regimes Q-076 predicts are inferred rather than observed.

The most useful thing this paper contributes to the REE decision is a *warning about the operator's double-edged character*. The prefrontal suppression here does not protect the wanted memory — it *causes* the forgetting of the interfering one, and lesioning it *restores* that memory. So Line B is not a free lunch: applied without careful gating on genuine competition, a strong suppression operator manufactures forgetting of traces that may later be wanted. That is the mirror image of the high-gain / no-suppression corner Q-076 flags, and it sharpens why Q-076 frames retrieval competence as an *interaction* — too little suppression floods retrieval with intrusions, too much suppression forgets competitors that should have been kept. Neither main effect is safe alone; the competent regime lives in their product.
