# Summary: Aston-Jones & Cohen 2005 -- adaptive gain theory of LC-NE function

**Entry:** 2026-08-11_mech_075_lc_ne_decision_time_gain_astonjones2005
**DOI:** [10.1146/annurev.neuro.28.061604.135709](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.neuro.28.061604.135709)
**Claim tested:** MECH-075 (construct-validity question: is a decision-time readout of LC-arousal effects even plausible in principle?)

---

Ask whether the locus coeruleus can do anything on the timescale of a single decision, and this is the paper the field points to. Aston-Jones and Cohen synthesize two decades of monkey LC single-unit recording into a simple dichotomy: phasic and tonic firing modes. Phasic LC neurons fire in tight, short bursts -- typically three to four spikes, at a latency of roughly 90 to 150 milliseconds -- time-locked to the outcome of a decision process, most often a target-detection or discrimination judgment. This is about as "decision-time" as a neuromodulatory signal gets: it is not a slow drift in baseline tone, it is a burst that arrives within the same trial, shortly before the behavioral response it is proposed to facilitate. The theory's proposal is that this phasic burst transiently boosts the gain of whichever target-neuron population is currently engaged in resolving the decision -- sharpening the signal-to-noise ratio of task-relevant processing at exactly the moment it matters. Tonic mode, by contrast, is a slower, higher-baseline state associated with task disengagement and exploration, operating over many trials rather than one.

For the MECH-075 construct-validity question, this paper matters because it rules out the easy dismissal. One might have expected that LC/NE signaling is inherently a slow neuromodulator, incapable in principle of doing anything within the span of a single decision -- in which case a "decision-time attractor width" readout would be a category error from the start. Aston-Jones and Cohen's evidence says otherwise: phasic LC activity is genuinely fast, genuinely decision-locked, and is proposed (with substantial supporting data) to change the computational properties -- specifically the gain -- of downstream target circuits within that same short window. So decision-time LC effects are not implausible on priors.

But the paper's entire evidentiary base is cortical and oculomotor decision circuitry -- prefrontal, cingulate, orbitofrontal afferents to LC, and LC efferent effects measured in areas like LIP and sensory cortex during target-detection tasks. Nowhere does the theory test, or even discuss, whether the same fast-gain mechanism extends to hippocampal attractor dynamics. The paper licenses "LC can act within a decision" as a general proposition about the LC-NE system; it does not license "LC widens dorsal-hippocampal attractor basins within a single decision," which is the specific claim MECH-075's dorsal leg and V3-EXQ-905a's basin_width metric need. That extension -- from cortical gain modulation to hippocampal attractor geometry -- is exactly the gap this targeted review is trying to locate literature for, and this paper, however foundational, does not close it.

**Relevance to the construct-validity question:** this paper answers "is decision-time even the right order of magnitude for an LC effect, at all" with a qualified yes -- for cortical target-neuron gain, not for hippocampal attractor width specifically. It shifts the burden of proof onto papers that actually measure hippocampal representational dynamics under LC/NE manipulation, which is what the remaining four entries in this batch do -- and none of them find an effect at the same ~100ms-to-seconds timescale this paper describes for cortex. The gap the autopsy flagged survives this paper; it just narrows to "the fast-gain mechanism has not been shown to generalize from cortex to hippocampal attractor geometry," rather than "fast LC effects are implausible per se."
