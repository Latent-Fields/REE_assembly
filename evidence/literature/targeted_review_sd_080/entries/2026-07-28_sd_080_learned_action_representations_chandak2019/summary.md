# Learning Action Representations for Reinforcement Learning (Chandak et al., ICML 2019)

## What the paper did

Chandak and colleagues take the observation that model-free RL happily learns *state* representations but almost always leaves the *action* space structureless -- one-hot, or whatever structure the environment designer happened to hand over -- and ask what is lost by that. Their construction decomposes a policy into two pieces: an inner policy that acts in a low-dimensional space of action representations, and an outer map from that space back to the environment's actual discrete actions. The representation itself is not learned by policy gradient. It is learned by supervised inverse dynamics: they search for estimators such that "a reconstruction of P(A_t|S_t,S_{t+1}) is accurate", minimising the negative log-likelihood over observed transitions. In other words, two actions end up nearby in the representation space precisely when they are hard to tell apart *from their effect on the world*. They provide convergence conditions, and evaluate on a maze with action sets scaled combinatorially from 2^4 to 2^12, plus two commercial recommender domains with thousands of actions.

## Findings relevant to SD-080

The headline is a scaling result. The baselines that do not learn action representations -- actor-critic and DPG -- deteriorate steadily as the action set grows, while the representation-learning agent holds performance roughly flat. On the two real-world tasks the paper reports "significantly higher return, up to 2x and 3x in the respective tasks, much quicker" than standard actor-critic. The mechanism the authors give for this is exactly the property SD-080 says REE's action-object space lacks: the agent can "infer the outcomes of actions similar to actions already taken", which is only possible if similarity in the representation space *means* similarity of outcome.

## How this translates to REE

This is, as far as I can tell, the same object SD-004 asks for. The co-design document describes O as a "learned compression from z_world-level effects to 16-dim action objects", "semantically grounded in world-effects", with `o_t = f(z_world_t, a_t)`. Chandak et al.'s representation is learned from `P(A_t | S_t, S_{t+1})`, which is state-conditioned by construction -- the same action in a different state sits differently in the space, because it did something different. The 2026-07-22 spike measured REE's version of that space and found the opposite: `r2_explained_by_action_alone = 0.9947`, pairwise-distance CV across 120 world states under 1%, and a parameter delta of exactly 0.0 after a 40-episode warmup. REE has built the architecture of a learned action representation and then not connected it to any gradient. What this paper contributes is that the generalisation benefit lives in the *learning*, not in the architecture -- so the frozen head is not a partial version of SD-004's O, it is a different object that happens to have the same shape.

There is a second, less comfortable implication. The representation here is learned by a *supervised transition* objective, deliberately kept off the policy-gradient path because that is what keeps its variance low. If REE were to repair SD-080 by simply routing an existing training path through the head, it would not be reproducing this result -- the existing paths are reward and prediction paths, not an inverse-dynamics objective. The fix that this paper actually endorses is a new self-supervised objective, which is a larger change than "connect the gradient".

## Limitations and caveats

The honest weakness is scale. Every domain here is a large-action-set domain, and the entire argument for learned action representations is a large-action-set argument: you need the structure because you cannot visit every action. REE's `action_dim` is 5. With five actions there is very little to generalise *across*, and a structureless action space may simply be adequate. So this paper does not establish that SD-080's defect costs REE anything measurable; it establishes that what REE has is not what SD-004 specified, and that in the regime where the difference matters, it matters a great deal. That distinction should be preserved when this entry is used in governance -- reading it as "therefore REE's planning is impaired" would be over-claiming.

The consumer also differs. Chandak et al. put a policy-gradient learner inside the representation space; REE puts a CEM planner that refits inside O. Whether CEM is more or less sensitive to a geometrically uninformative search space than policy gradient is an open question and not one this paper touches. If anything I would expect CEM to be *more* sensitive, since it has no gradient to fall back on and relies entirely on the sampling distribution's geometry -- but that is a conjecture, and it belongs in the experiment design rather than in the confidence number.

## Confidence reasoning

0.78. Mapping fidelity is the load-bearing component and it is high: the paper's object is SD-004's object, including the state-conditioning that the spike found missing. Source quality is high (ICML, convergence analysis, real deployments). What holds it below 0.85 is transfer risk from the action-set-size gap, which is genuinely large -- three orders of magnitude -- and which cuts specifically against inferring a performance cost for REE. This entry supports the claim that O is not what it was specified to be. It does not, on its own, support a claim about how much that costs.
