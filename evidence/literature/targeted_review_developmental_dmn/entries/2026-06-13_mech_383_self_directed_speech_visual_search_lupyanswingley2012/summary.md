# Self-Directed Speech Affects Visual Search Performance (Lupyan & Swingley, 2012)

*Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology* 65(6):1068-85. [DOI 10.1080/17470218.2011.647039](https://doi.org/10.1080/17470218.2011.647039). According to PubMed (PMID 22489646).

## What the paper did

This is the study MECH-383 was built on, so it is the right paper to replace the textbook anchor with a direct causal test. Participants searched for a common object among distractors; on some trials they spoke the target's name aloud, on others they searched silently. The manipulation is within-subject, so the comparison is causal: does *producing the label* change perception, holding the target constant?

## Findings relevant to the claim

Speaking the target name **facilitated search** -- faster and more accurate -- and the facilitation was largest when the name-to-object association was strong (a prototypical "chair" benefits more than an atypical one). Crucially, the effect was not monotonic in the direction you might assume from "more information is better": as the discrepancy between the spoken name and the actual visual target grew, speaking began to *impair* search. The authors read this through the label-feedback hypothesis: actually hearing "chair," rather than merely thinking about one, can temporarily make the visual system a better "chair detector" by feeding the label back into ongoing perceptual processing.

## How it translates to REE

This is high-fidelity grounding for MECH-383's core assertion: a self-directed label is a **top-down tuning signal that biases perceptual search**, not a post-perceptual report. The graded, association-dependent benefit maps directly onto MECH-383's framing of labels as control *inputs* to the precision-routing plane (ARC-005) and the distributed precision cluster (MECH-251/MECH-261) -- a "danger-gradient" or "blocked-goal" label would bias perception toward the relevant class of cues exactly as "chair" biases toward chairs. And the impairment-on-mismatch result is the most useful part for design: it shows the control input has real, *sign-dependent* authority over perception. A label is not a free annotation; a wrong one actively degrades search.

## Limitations and caveats

The label here is overt natural-language speech in humans, and REE has no linguistic modality -- it would generate labels as internal control tokens, so the mechanism transfers but the modality does not. More substantively for scope: the demonstrated effect is on template-guided *search for a known target*, which is attentional/template-matching. MECH-383 also claims a "novelty-gradient toward unexplained change" reading -- biasing toward genuinely unexpected cues -- and this paper does not test that. It grounds the cue-biasing half of the claim well and the novelty-detection half not at all (recorded as a failure signature). The no-free-lunch boundary (mis-specified labels hurt) is also recorded, because it constrains any V4 design: label-as-control only helps if label generation is accurate.
