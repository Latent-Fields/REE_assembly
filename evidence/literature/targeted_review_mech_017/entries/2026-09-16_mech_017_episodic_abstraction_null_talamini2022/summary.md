# No evidence for a preferential role of sleep in episodic memory abstraction (Talamini et al., 2022)

This entry is in MECH-017's evidence set precisely because it argues against the claim, and a pull
that gathered only the supporting literature on sleep-dependent abstraction would be
misrepresenting the state of the field. Talamini and colleagues ran two experiments on
hippocampus-dependent face-location associations. In the first, 37 participants were tested across
a twelve-hour interval containing either a night's sleep or a day's wakefulness. In the second, 46
participants were tested across sixty hours, contrasting a first night of sleep deprivation
followed by recovery sleep against a normal sleep-wake cycle. In both, extraction of cross-episodic
regularities and generalisation to novel exemplars proceeded to a similar extent whether the
interval contained sleep or not.

What makes this more than an unremarkable null is the positive control sitting inside the same
dataset. In Experiment 1 sleep *did* do something: it protected temporal-order memory, the
arbitrary episodic detail, from decay. So the paradigm was not blind to sleep, and the participants
were not too few to detect a sleep effect -- one was detected. It was simply not the effect the
consolidation literature predicts. The generalisation measure was near-flat across the retention
interval in both conditions (52.7% immediate against 52.1% delayed in Experiment 1), and
Experiment 2 found no significant condition differences. The authors conclude, in their own words,
that this casts doubt on a long-standing and widely accepted hypothesis.

The dissociation is the uncomfortable part for MECH-017, and it is uncomfortable in a specific
direction. Sleep preserved the trace and did not preferentially improve the model. MECH-017 asserts
the opposite priority -- that the offline phase exists to raise the fidelity of the generative
model, with reality consolidation being something more than trace maintenance. Here the trace
effect is the one that survived. If that generalises, then "sleep improves the fidelity of the
generative world model" would have to be restated as a claim about elapsed offline time, or
relocated to ARC-011's territory of stability and trace protection, and MECH-017's distinctive
content would largely dissolve.

I do not think it should be read as a disproof, and the confidence assigned (0.70, weakens)
reflects that. A null is bounded by its paradigm, and this one has real bounds: a single
generalisation task, categorical rather than sequential regularity structure, and no
polysomnography -- which means it offers no test at all of the slow-wave dose-response that carries
Durrant's positive result, and the two studies could both be right if the effect is phase-specific
and this sample's sleep architecture happened not to vary informatively. The authors themselves
list the missing PSG, the task specificity, and an awkwardness in the chessboard task where maximum
attainable generalisation scores vary by item location. The defensible reading is not that MECH-017
is false but that it is underdetermined by the human behavioural literature as it stands -- which is
itself a finding REE should hold, since it means the claim's support currently rests more on the
computational entries in this directory than on the human ones.
