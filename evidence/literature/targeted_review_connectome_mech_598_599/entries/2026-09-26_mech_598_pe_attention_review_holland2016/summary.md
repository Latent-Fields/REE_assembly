# Mini-review: Prediction errors, attention and associative learning (Holland & Schiffino 2016)

Claims: MECH-598. Direction: supports. Confidence: 0.60. PMID 26948122, DOI 10.1016/j.nlm.2016.02.014.

Holland and Schiffino set out the old fork in associative learning theory. In Rescorla-Wagner, surprise makes the *outcome* a better teacher. In Pearce-Hall, surprise makes the *cues that were present* more learnable later: their associability rises and they enter new associations faster. The review collects the lab's evidence that both happen, and that the associability change is coded, stored and retrieved by a spread-out circuit (central amygdala, substantia nigra, basal forebrain, dorsolateral striatum, parietal cortex), not by one nucleus.

For MECH-598 this is the anchoring citation. The claim is specifically the Pearce-Hall half: an aversive PE should raise the learning rate of candidate causes, and that raised rate should persist. REE already has something like the Rescorla-Wagner half in any regression-trained harm readout. So the review earns its place by saying the two halves separate in behaviour, which is what the claim's matched-broadcast control is built to test.

The caveat is the important part. Nearly all of this work is appetitive, and the associability boost is mostly induced by an expected food pellet failing to arrive. MECH-598 is triggered by a harm that arrives unexpectedly. Those are not obviously the same kind of surprise, and the lab's own data (see the Holland 2006 CeA-lesion entry in this directory) suggest the CeA route is recruited by omissions and not by unexpected presentations. So the review supports the *form* of the mechanism and says little about the *trigger*.

Confidence 0.60. It is the right paper for the computational distinction and the wrong paper for the valence. The aversive grounding has to come from elsewhere; the human reversal and pain-conditioning entries in this directory are the better sources for that.
