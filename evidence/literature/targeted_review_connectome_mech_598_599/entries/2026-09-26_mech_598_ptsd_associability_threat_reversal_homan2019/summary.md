# Neural computations of threat in the aftermath of combat trauma (Homan et al. 2019)

Claims: MECH-598. Direction: supports. Confidence: 0.52. PMID 30664770, DOI 10.1038/s41593-018-0315-x.

Homan and colleagues used threat reversal learning in combat veterans. People with more PTSD symptoms weighted unexpected negative outcomes more heavily, and in the model that raised the associability of threat cues. How well the striatum tracked associability partly mediated the link to symptoms. The amygdala tracked value, and smaller amygdala volume independently predicted severity.

What this adds to MECH-598 is a clinical failure mode. A mechanism that raises the learning rate of candidate causes after aversive surprise has an obvious way of going wrong: too much gain, and threat predictions keep over-updating. The claim already lists over-generalisation (avoid(B) rising with avoid(A)) as a falsifier. This paper suggests an agent built with too high a PE gain on associability should show something like a trauma phenotype, which is worth noting when an arm is designed.

On anatomy it muddies things. Li et al. 2011 found associability in amygdala and prediction error in striatum; here associability is in striatum and value in amygdala. I would not cite either for locus. Both support associability as a real computational variable in human aversive learning.

Confidence 0.52: clinical and correlational, useful as a phenotype constraint rather than as mechanism evidence.
