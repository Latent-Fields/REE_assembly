# Jones et al. (2006) -- Self-awareness of impairment and the decision to drive after an extended period of wakefulness

**Why this paper.** Included for balance and honesty: the two Baranski papers and the Boardman
paper in this pull all point toward null or acute-regime-absent waking confidence drift. This
paper is the one retrieved result that documents a genuine overconfidence-type effect emerging
under extended wakefulness, with a rough timescale anchor -- and it needs to be reported alongside
the null results, with its real limitations stated plainly, rather than cited selectively as
support.

**What the paper did.** Thirty-two participants stayed awake for 40 continuous hours. Every 2
hours, they completed the Psychomotor Vigilance Task (PVT, an objective measure of sustained
attention) and rated, on a 7-point scale, how well they thought they could drive safely, react
quickly in an emergency, and keep in their lane. Critically, they also rated how well they thought
a hypothetical OTHER person in their position could perform the same tasks.

**Key finding.** Self-ratings of driving ability declined significantly in step with the objective
PVT decline, with the first significant qualitative decline appearing around 19 hours of
continuous wakefulness (roughly matching a performance decrement equivalent to a blood alcohol
concentration of 0.05%, the Australian legal driving limit, per the authors' citation of prior
work using the same protocol). But participants CONSISTENTLY rated a hypothetical other person's
driving as WORSE than their own under identical conditions -- a self-other comparative bias that
persisted throughout the wakefulness period.

**How this translates to REE.** Read carefully, this paper is genuinely double-edged for SD-076.
On one hand, it is the only result in this pull demonstrating a real, measurable overconfidence
phenomenon linked to time awake, with an approximate onset timescale (~19h) that could bound the
exposure-budget question alongside the Boardman acute/chronic finding. On the other hand, the
overconfidence documented here is a SELF-VERSUS-OTHER comparative bias, not a failure of
self-calibration -- and the paper's own data show self-ratings tracking objective PVT decline
reasonably accurately throughout, which is closer to the Baranski null-drift findings than it
first appears from the abstract's framing. SD-076 posits an asymmetric running estimate of ONE'S
OWN accuracy history, not a comparison against a hypothetical other person; this paper does not
directly test that construct, and its data arguably weaken, rather than strengthen, the case for a
large self-calibration drift.

**Limitations and honesty about the mapping.** This is the weakest mapping fidelity of the four
SD-076 entries in this pull. The comparative-optimism paradigm (self vs. hypothetical other) is
structurally different from an EMA over one's own error history, and the paper's central concern
-- legal liability and road safety given the self-other rating gap -- is not REE's concern at all.
It should not be cited as strong quantitative support for SD-076's magnitude; its value here is
narrower: it is evidence that SOME kind of wakefulness-linked overconfidence phenomenon exists in
humans, with a rough timescale, even though it is not the specific phenomenon SD-076 encodes.

**Confidence reasoning.** Source quality (0.6) is moderate: reasonable design and sample, a
mid-tier chronobiology venue. Mapping fidelity (0.4) is the binding constraint and is
deliberately low, reflecting the construct mismatch described above. Transfer risk (0.5) is high
for the same reason. Aggregate confidence 0.55 -- filed as "supports" for the general phenomenon
of wakefulness-linked overconfidence, with the mapping caveat doing the real work of qualifying
what it does and does not establish for SD-076 specifically.
