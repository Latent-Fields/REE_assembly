# Baranski (2007) -- Fatigue, sleep loss, and confidence in judgment

**Why this paper.** A direct, larger-scale replication attempt of the same author's 1994 finding
(also entered in this directory), commissioned for the same GOV-FANOUT-1 H3 lit-pull: what
magnitude of waking metacognitive confidence drift should be expected under sleep deprivation, so
that V3-EXQ-794a's small, sub-threshold effect can be judged against a real biological benchmark
rather than against an unexamined assumption.

**What the paper did.** Sixty-four adults were kept awake and cognitively engaged for 28 hours,
performing three tasks chosen to span a range of cognitive demands and susceptibility to sleep
deprivation: a perceptual comparison task, a general-knowledge task, and mental addition. On each
trial, participants gave a confidence rating alongside their response, and also made pre- and
post-task estimates of their own overall performance.

**Key finding.** Subjective and objective sleepiness measures confirmed the expected fatigue
trajectory. But across all three tasks, the traditional indices of the confidence-accuracy
relationship -- calibration, resolution, and over/underconfidence -- remained STABLE across the
28-hour deprivation period, as did the accuracy of participants' pre- and post-task performance
estimates. This replicates the 1994 study's null across a broader task battery and a larger
sample, and the author's own stated conclusion is that people can accurately assess their own
cognitive performance after a night without sleep, independent of how sleepy they subjectively
feel.

**How this translates to REE.** This paper adds real weight to the picture the 1994 study began:
in carefully instrumented human studies that measure confidence about one's OWN response accuracy
(SD-076's actual construct, as distinct from the world-belief papers already in this directory),
the confidence-accuracy relationship does not reliably degrade under a single episode of extended
wakefulness. That is directly relevant to the autopsy's open H3 question. If the best human
evidence says a single wake episode should produce little or no measurable calibration drift, then
V3-EXQ-794a's small, dose-ordered, sub-threshold effect is not obviously the wrong-sized effect
for the mechanism being modeled -- if anything, a LARGE effect would have been the more surprising
finding relative to this literature. This weakens H3 (wrong mechanism form) somewhat further,
alongside the autopsy's own smoke-comparison finding, while still leaving H1 (F1 interaction
damping) and H2 (insufficient exposure) as the live candidates the fanout already queued.

**Limitations and honesty about the mapping.** As with the 1994 companion study, this is a
sleep-DEPRIVATION paper, not a REM-MANIPULATION paper -- it cannot speak to whether REM sleep
specifically is the corrective process MECH-204 names, only to whether waking calibration holds up
under extended wakefulness generally. It is drawn from one research group's paradigm (albeit
extended and improved from the 1994 study), so it is not an independent replication in the
strictest sense. And "confidence in cognitive task performance" is still a step removed from an
RL agent's continuously updated running-variance estimate -- the mapping is closer than for the
world-belief papers, but it is not identity.

**Confidence reasoning.** Source quality (0.78) is higher than the 1994 companion entry: larger
N (64 vs 16), three tasks instead of one, and a higher-tier venue (Journal of Experimental
Psychology: Applied). Mapping fidelity (0.75) and transfer risk (0.35) are unchanged from the
companion entry -- same construct, same domain gap to REE's implementation. Aggregate confidence
0.72.
