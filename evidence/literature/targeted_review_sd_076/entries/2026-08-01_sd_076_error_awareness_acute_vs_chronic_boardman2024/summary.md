# Boardman et al. (2024) -- Awareness of errors is reduced by sleep loss

**Why this paper.** This is the single most decision-relevant paper found in this pull for the
GOV-FANOUT-1 fanout's H2 (insufficient training exposure) versus H3 (wrong mechanism form)
question, because it does something none of the other retrieved papers do: it directly
dissociates ACUTE sleep loss (one wake episode) from CHRONIC sleep loss (repeated nights of
partial restriction) using the same task and measures, in the same research programme.

**What the paper did.** Using the Error Awareness Task -- a go/no-go paradigm where participants
must verbally report awareness of their own errors -- combined with EEG measures of error
processing (error-related negativity, ERN, and error positivity, Pe), the authors ran two
separate studies. Fourteen participants underwent total sleep deprivation (TSD, one full night
without sleep). Twenty-seven participants underwent sleep restriction (SR, multiple nights of
partial sleep loss). Both behavioral error awareness and its EEG correlates were measured in each
group.

**Key finding.** Acute TSD did NOT reduce behavioral error awareness, and did not reduce ERN or Pe
amplitude. Chronic SR, in contrast, DID reduce behavioral error awareness, increased the time
taken to detect errors, and reduced Pe amplitude. The authors' interpretation is that individuals
under chronic sleep restriction specifically are at risk for reduced recognition of their own
errors -- a risk that a single acute night of total deprivation does not, on this evidence,
produce.

**How this translates to REE.** V3-EXQ-794a's design -- 30 training episodes, K=1 single-fire
sleep -- is structurally an ACUTE, single-cycle exposure, not a chronic multi-cycle restriction
regime. If human metacognitive drift specifically requires accumulated exposure across MULTIPLE
sleep-wake cycles to become measurable, and is largely absent after a single acute episode, that
is direct support for GOV-FANOUT-1's H2: the training budget may simply be too short for the
asymmetric-EMA drift source to accumulate into a measurable effect, independent of whether the
mechanism's functional FORM (H3) is right or wrong. This does not adjudicate H1 (F1 interaction
damping) one way or the other, but it gives H2 a genuine biological anchor that it did not have
before this pull, and it is a second, independent line of evidence (alongside the two Baranski
null results) against reading a small acute-regime effect as proof the mechanism form itself is
broken.

**Limitations and honesty about the mapping.** Error awareness in this task is a per-trial
judgment ("did I just get that wrong?"), which is a narrower and more immediate construct than
SD-076's continuously-updated running-variance estimate that is meant to summarize accuracy
across many trials and persist across an episode. The acute-vs-chronic distinction is the
load-bearing part of the mapping here; the specific EEG correlates are secondary and not directly
transferable to REE's substrate. It is also worth being honest that this paper, on its own, cannot
tell us whether REE's specific 30-episode/K=1 budget crosses whatever threshold separates "acute"
from "sufficiently chronic" in the human data -- it establishes that such a threshold exists, not
where it falls in REE's own units.

**Confidence reasoning.** Source quality (0.82) is strong: a recent (2024), well-designed
two-study paper with converging behavioral and EEG measures purpose-built to make exactly the
acute/chronic contrast this pull needed. Mapping fidelity (0.6) is moderate -- the timescale
distinction maps well, but the per-trial construct is narrower than REE's accumulating estimate.
Transfer risk (0.4) reflects that the dissociation itself, not the exact measures, is what
transfers. Filed as "mixed" evidence_direction because the paper both supports the existence of a
real metacognitive drift phenomenon (under the right, chronic, conditions) and weakens the
expectation of a large effect in REE's acute-regime design specifically. Aggregate confidence
0.65.
