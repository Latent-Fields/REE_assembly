# Solway, Diuk, Cordova, Yee, Barto, Niv & Botvinick 2014 -- Optimal behavioral hierarchy

This entry exists to record an absence, and it is filed deliberately rather than omitted. Both of the numeric parameters under review here -- MECH-321's recursive depth cap of 3-4 and MECH-323's chunk-size budget of 2-5 elements per level -- are stated at POLICY grain: subtasks composed of policy primitives. Every source currently cited for either number is measured somewhere else. Sakai 2003 is finger-sequence motor grain; Koechlin and Badre are anatomical grain. Solway et al. is the natural arbiter at the grain REE actually uses, and it declines to bound either quantity.

The paper gives a mathematical account of what makes one action hierarchy better than another, identifies an optimal hierarchy for a given set of tasks by Bayesian model selection over option sets, and validates it against four human behavioural experiments showing that people spontaneously discover the optimal decomposition. If a principled subtask-size budget or depth limit existed at policy grain, this is the paper that would have it. On subtask size it is simply silent -- optimal hierarchies are characterised by WHERE the partition falls, at bottleneck states, not by how many primitive actions land on either side of it. On depth the silence is explicit and instructive: the analysis "assumes that hierarchies are one level deep", a restriction "adopted to assure computational tractability in the present application", with the framework generalising "without any alteration to deeper hierarchies".

That last quotation is worth sitting with, because it is the same move MECH-321 makes and for the same stated reason. Solway et al. cap depth at one because deeper search was expensive, and say so. MECH-321 caps depth at three because unbounded recursion is expensive, and its `implementation_note` says so. This is arguably the correct justification, and it is a respectable one -- but it is a computational-cost argument, not a biological one, and it has a consequence the current framing obscures: a cost-derived cap should MOVE as compute allows, rather than sitting at a fixed constant defended by citation. The same reasoning applies to MECH-323's chunk-size budget, which is why this entry is tagged to both claims.

The second thing the paper offers is a positive redirection. What the normative account does constrain is the partition LOCATION -- bottleneck states, identified by exactly the diverse-density logic that McGovern & Barto 2001 supplies and that is already in this corpus. MECH-321 built an R5 bottleneck trigger mode on top of that literature. So the position we are actually in is that the literature grounds MECH-321's trigger well and its depth budget not at all, which is a reasonable place to be and suggests the campaign's effort is currently pointed the right way.

Marked `unknown` at confidence 0.55. `unknown` because the paper neither supports nor weakens the numbers; it establishes that the normative literature does not commit. The confidence figure reflects high confidence that this source genuinely bears on the question, tempered by the weakness inherent in any null from a single paper -- an absence of evidence in one account is not evidence of absence in the field, and it would be an over-read in the opposite direction to conclude from this that MECH-321's cap is wrong. The behavioural experiments also run on small discrete state spaces, where REE's rollouts are longer and continuous-latent. The narrow claim, which is all the governance record needs, is that neither number can be said to follow from the computational-optimality literature, and both should therefore be carried as calibration parameters with declared ranges rather than as biologically-motivated defaults.
