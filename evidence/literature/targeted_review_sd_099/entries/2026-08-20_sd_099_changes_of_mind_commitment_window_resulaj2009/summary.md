# Changes of mind in decision-making

Resulaj, Kiani, Wolpert & Shadlen (2009), *Nature* 461(7261):263-266. DOI 10.1038/nature08275. PMID 19693010.

## What the paper did

Human subjects judged the net direction of a field of moving dots and reported their choice by reaching to touch one of two targets. The interesting result was in the reaching hand, not the verbal report: on some trials, subjects' hand trajectories visibly curved from one target toward the other mid-reach -- a "change of mind" -- even though no new stimulus information was presented after the reach began. The authors' explanation is that sensory evidence already in the neural processing pipeline at the moment of initial commitment continues to be integrated for a further stretch of time, and if that trailing evidence disagrees with the initial choice, it can still overturn it, visible as a trajectory correction. This window of continued influence -- the time during which a decision remains revisable -- runs to a few hundred milliseconds after the initial commitment; past that, the trajectory is locked in.

## Why this is included, and why it is a deliberate contrast rather than direct evidence

Unlike every other entry in this directory, this paper has nothing to do with defensive behavior, orienting, or threat. I am including it for a specific and narrower purpose: as a calibration anchor for what "fast" actually looks like in the decision-neuroscience literature, so that Option C's proposed 60-125ms window can be checked against a real example of a genuinely sub-second biological commitment process, rather than only against the multi-second defensive-freezing numbers the other entries provide.

The result of that check cuts a specific way. This is, as far as I could find, close to the fastest well-characterized class of post-commitment dynamics in the human decision literature -- and even it runs to a few hundred milliseconds, not 60-125ms. That places it closer to Option A's lower bound (625ms is roughly 2-5x this window) than to Option C's range, which sits BELOW even this fast-decision benchmark. I want to be precise about what that does and does not show. It does not mean SD-099's mechanism should behave like a perceptual reaching decision -- the domains are unrelated, and the paper measures pre-commitment revisability, not post-commitment behavioral bias persistence across separate subsequent choices, which is the quantity SD-099 actually needs. What it does show is that even reaching for the fastest end of the spectrum of well-quantified biological decision dynamics does not land inside Option C's proposed range. Across everything gathered in this pull -- from the slowest (looming-freeze, 21-28s) to the fastest (changes-of-mind, a few hundred ms) -- nothing lands at 60-125ms.

## Limitations

This is an arbitrary two-alternative perceptual-motor task with no defensive, threat, or valence content, in healthy human volunteers, using a fast reaching movement as the readout. It is not rodent, not orienting-triggered, and not a three-way approach/withdraw/resume decision. Most importantly, it measures a genuinely different quantity than the one this pull was commissioned to answer: the window during which an ALREADY-INITIATED decision remains revisable by evidence still in the pipeline, not how long an ALREADY-RESOLVED decision continues to bias SEPARATE, SUBSEQUENT action-selection events. I am treating it as a contrast/calibration data point, not as a direct behavioral analog for SD-099's mechanism, and it should not be read as supporting or weakening the claim on its own.
