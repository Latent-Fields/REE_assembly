# Hypocretin deficiency destabilizes human sleep transitions (Sorensen, Knudsen & Jennum, 2013)

According to PubMed, Sorensen, Knudsen and Jennum (Sleep, 2013; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.5665/sleep.2880)) take the flip-flop model out of the rodent and into the clinic. Across 63 narcolepsy patients, stratified by the presence of cataplexy and by CSF hypocretin-1 status, they counted state transitions on overnight polysomnography. The result is clean: hypocretin-1-deficient patients had significantly more frequent sleep-wake transitions (p=0.014) and more frequent transitions to and from REM sleep (p=0.044) than patients with normal hypocretin. A multivariate analysis teased the two apart -- REM transitions were predicted mainly by hypocretin deficiency (p=0.011), while sleep-wake transitions were predicted mainly by cataplexy (p=0.001). The authors' own conclusion is that narcolepsy serves as a human model of the sleep-wake and REM flip-flop switches.

I included this as the empirical, non-rodent anchor for MECH-286. The two Saper reviews supply the architecture; this paper supplies the human causal signature. If MECH-286 is right that orexin is the gate whose presence holds the system in a state and whose absence lets it flip, then removing orexin in humans should make the switch flip more often -- and that is exactly what is observed. This raises the source quality of the MECH-286 evidence set considerably: the gate is not just a rodent-circuit inference, it is load-bearing for state stability in human physiology, measured quantitatively.

The mapping caveat is the important one for governance, and it is twofold. First, this is a *loss-of-function correlation*. It shows that absent orexin destabilizes transitions, which confirms the gate is real and necessary; it does not show the positive, constructive claim MECH-286 actually makes -- that an override signal gates sleep *onset timing* and, once it permits onset, *recruits the MECH-285 replay-priority readout*. Nothing in a transition-frequency count speaks to replay recruitment. The constructive half of MECH-286 is inferred from the gate's existence, not demonstrated here.

Second, and more pointedly, the dissociation the authors report -- cataplexy predicting sleep-wake transitions, hypocretin deficiency predicting REM transitions -- suggests the "gate" is at least two-dimensional. That sits in mild tension with MECH-286's modelling of a single SD-037 override_signal as the recruitment authority. The honest reading is that REE's single scalar is a useful compression, but the human data hint that the biological gate decomposes along more than one axis (tonus/motor stability versus REM-state control). MECH-286 should hold its single-override formulation as an abstraction to be revisited rather than a fact confirmed.

I score this 0.76. Transfer risk is low because this is human data directly on the construct, and the statistics are clear. I keep mapping fidelity in the low-0.7s -- and the aggregate just above -- because the evidence is loss-of-function and correlational, and the cataplexy-versus-deficiency dissociation actively complicates the single-override picture that MECH-286 currently paints.
