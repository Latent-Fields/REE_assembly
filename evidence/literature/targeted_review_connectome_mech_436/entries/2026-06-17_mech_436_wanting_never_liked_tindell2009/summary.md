# Tindell, Smith, Berridge & Aldridge (2009) — Dynamic computation of incentive salience: "wanting" what was never "liked"

According to PubMed ([DOI: 10.1523/JNEUROSCI.2499-09.2009](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.2499-09.2009), *Journal of Neuroscience*).

**What the paper did.** This is the empirical companion to the Zhang et al. computational model. The authors recorded single units in the ventral pallidum of rats while presenting a Pavlovian cue that had always predicted an intensely salty, *disliked* taste. Ordinarily these neurons fire vigorously to a cue predicting liked sucrose but only weakly to the salt cue. The clever manipulation: they put the animal into a never-before-experienced state of physiological salt depletion and re-presented the salt cue — in cue-only extinction, so the animal never tasted the salt. Ventral-pallidum firing to the salt cue rose dramatically and selectively, *immediately*, the first time it was seen in the depleted state, before the now-appropriate salt had ever been experienced as anything but disgusting, and without any new learning about the cue.

**Why it matters for MECH-436.** MECH-436 is the finer claim, split from MECH-229, that wanting is *additionally drive-state-modulated* — that the most-wanted object tracks the currently-most-depleted drive axis even when the liking/consummatory target does not move. This paper is the clean biological instance of that exact dissociation. The salt cue's liking history is flat-to-negative, yet its wanting signal (ventral-pallidum firing) is driven up by the depletion-axis drive. Liking does not change; wanting does; and what changes it is the homeostatic state. That is the dissociation MECH-436 names, observed at the level of neural firing rather than inferred from behaviour alone.

**The mapping and its limits.** The fidelity here is good but the scope is narrow in a way that matters for REE's current bottleneck. The demonstration uses a single appetite (sodium) and a large value swing — disliked-to-wanted is about as big a drive-driven re-ranking as one can construct. MECH-436's V3 difficulty, by contrast, is in the *graded, small-spread, multi-axis* regime: V3-EXQ-514q found an in-run per-axis drive spread far too small to flip an object-score-dominated `most_wanted`. Tindell et al. establish that the dissociation is real in biology when the drive swing is large; they do not establish that a modest graded drive difference suffices to re-rank candidate objects. Two further features sharpen the constraint on any REE implementation: the amplification was *cue-selective* (only the salt cue) and *immediate* (no re-learning) — so a mechanism that spreads drive gain non-selectively across candidates, or that needs to re-learn before the shift appears, would not match the biology.

**Confidence reasoning.** I set this at 0.78. Source quality is high (≈0.85) — rigorous in-vivo single-unit recording in a strong venue, and the design (cue-only, never-tasted, first-encounter) closes the obvious confounds. Mapping fidelity is faithful (≈0.80): the paper measures precisely the wanting-vs-liking-under-drive dissociation MECH-436 asserts. Transfer risk is moderate (≈0.35): rat ventral pallidum and one appetite axis versus REE's engineered multi-axis drive and per-object token. The net is solid support for the *existence and form* of drive-state-modulated wanting, paired with an honest acknowledgement that the magnitude regime where REE's V3 substrate currently fails is outside what this paper tested.
