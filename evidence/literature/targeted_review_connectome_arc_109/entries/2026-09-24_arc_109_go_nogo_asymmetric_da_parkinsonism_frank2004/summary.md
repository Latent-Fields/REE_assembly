# Frank, Seeberger & O'Reilly 2004 -- "By carrot or by stick"

**What they did.** Parkinson's patients off medication, patients on medication, and matched controls did two probabilistic procedural learning tasks, including the now-standard probabilistic selection task with its later test of choose-A versus avoid-B. Off medication, patients were better at learning to avoid bad options than to pick good ones. Medication flipped this, making them more sensitive to positive than to negative outcomes. The result had been predicted in advance by Frank's neural network model, in which Go (direct, D1) and NoGo (indirect, D2) pathways are separately modulated: dopamine bursts strengthen Go, dips strengthen NoGo.

**Why it matters for ARC-109.** It is the founding human evidence that dopamine acts oppositely on two dissociable pathways, and that where dopamine sits decides which of them dominates behaviour. That is the biological fact ARC-109's "two populations with opposite dopamine sensitivity" rests on. It also grounds the claim's psychiatric ambition. If REE is to earn the Parkinson / impulse-control axis under ARC-106, a dopamine-depleted REE should show a NoGo-dominated profile that a single-scalar lesion cannot reproduce, which is leg (ii) of the claim's falsifier.

**Where the mapping strains.** What the paper measures is a *learning* asymmetry: which outcomes the patients learn from. ARC-109 is careful to say its subject is the selection-layer representation, not the D1-LTP / D2-LTD learning asymmetry, which REE already has via ARC-108. So this paper grounds the claim's premises more than its specific addition. One could, in principle, learn with opponent pathways and then sum them into one scalar at choice. Frank's model doesn't, but the patients' behaviour doesn't prove it mustn't. There is also the usual clinical confound: medication changes frontal dopamine too.

**Confidence.** 0.66. An excellent source, but mapping fidelity is only 0.6 because it evidences the substrate ARC-109 builds on rather than the representational split itself. It sits alongside OpAL (Collins & Frank 2014), which carries the selection-layer argument.
