# Wood, Bruner & Ross (1976) — The role of tutoring in problem solving

**Journal of Child Psychology and Psychiatry 17(2):89-100 · [10.1111/j.1469-7610.1976.tb00381.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1469-7610.1976.tb00381.x) · retrieved via PubMed (PMID 932126)**
**Claim tested: MECH-428 · direction: supports · confidence: 0.52**

## Why this paper is here

MECH-428's notes name it. The claim records that it is grounded by Bandura & Schunk (1981) in `targeted_review_proxy_progress_goal_maintenance` — proximal subgoals *created* intrinsic interest in initially-uninterested learners, a formation result rather than a maintenance one — and then flags that "a developmental-scaffolding / zone-of-proximal-development (Vygotsky) lit anchor would strengthen the formation framing", explicitly as a next lit-pull. This is that anchor. Wood, Bruner and Ross is the paper that introduced "scaffolding" as a term and gave Vygotsky's zone of proximal development its empirical treatment in the developmental literature.

## What the paper did

It is an observational study of adult–child tutorial interaction in problem solving with preschool children. The construct it introduced is that the tutor controls those elements of the task that are initially beyond the learner's capacity, so the child can concentrate on and complete only the elements within their current competence — and that this support is progressively withdrawn as the child comes to perform the whole task unaided. PubMed's indexing (child development, problem solving, motor skills, teaching, social reinforcement, age factors) is consistent with that account.

I should be plain about a retrieval limitation: **no abstract is available through PubMed for this record, and I did not obtain the full text.** What follows rests on the title, the MeSH indexing, and the construct's canonical standing — not on verified reported statistics. No quantitative result from this paper should be cited on the strength of this entry.

## How it maps, and where the mapping breaks

Two things map. First, decomposition into currently-attainable components is what makes an otherwise-unreachable superordinate task performable at all — the developmental statement of MECH-428's antecedent. Second, and more to the point for the formation direction: **the scaffold fades.** Support is withdrawn and the child ends up performing the whole task unaided. That is internalisation — competence at the superordinate level ending up resident in the learner, having been assembled through repeated success at the subordinate level.

Now the break, and it is a real one. The scaffold here is supplied by an **external tutor**. Read literally, this paper is evidence for `scaffolded_sd054_onboarding` — the developmental nursery / feeding curriculum that seeds z_goal externally — and *not* for MECH-428, which the claim defines explicitly as the internal complement, where the agent's own subgoal structure substitutes for direct superordinate seeding. Those are sibling mechanisms, and it would be sloppy to let a paper about one accumulate as grounding for the other without saying so.

The bridge is the fading result. If the superordinate competence survives withdrawal of the scaffold, then subgoal-level success was sufficient to *constitute* it, and the tutor's role was to guarantee subgoal attainability rather than to supply the parent goal. Under that reading the external/internal distinction is about who arranges attainability, not about what does the seeding — which would make this genuinely relevant to MECH-428. But I want to flag clearly that this is my inference, not something the paper tests. It never contrasts tutored acquisition against self-generated decomposition, so it cannot adjudicate whether the child's own emerging subgoal structure or the tutor's arrangement was doing the work.

There is a nice symmetry worth recording for whoever picks this up next. Bandura & Schunk supplies the **motivational** half of the formation story (proximal subgoals create the wanting where none existed); Wood, Bruner & Ross supplies the **structural** half (subgoal decomposition creates the doing where none was possible). Between them they cover the two things a superordinate goal would need in order to be bootstrapped. Neither is an artificial-agent result, which is why the Kulkarni and Florensa entries in this directory carry the computational load.

## Limitations and confidence

Beyond the external-scaffold problem: this is 1976 observational developmental psychology, with evidential standards well short of what a modern claim would be held to, on human preschool children, transferred to an artificial agent — a large jump on every axis at once. The effectiveness of tutoring varied with the child's age and developmental level, which is itself informative (the subgoal grading must match current competence; a mis-graded scaffold does not merely help less, it can fail to engage at all) but also means the phenomenon is condition-dependent in ways the paper does not fully characterise.

Source quality 0.62, mapping fidelity 0.55, transfer risk 0.50 — all three modest, and the aggregate 0.52 sits just above the weak-evidence boundary on purpose. **This entry earns its place by filling the developmental-scaffolding gap the claim's own notes named, not by evidential weight.** It should anchor framing and vocabulary in the MECH-428 discussion. It should not be load-bearing in a promotion decision, and if a governance cycle finds itself leaning on it, that is a signal the computational evidence is thinner than it looked rather than that this paper is stronger than it is.
