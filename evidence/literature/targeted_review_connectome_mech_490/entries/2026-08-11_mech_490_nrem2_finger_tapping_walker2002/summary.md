# Walker, Brakefield, Morgan, Hobson & Stickgold (2002) — the canonical NREM2 anchor

This is the paper that started the modern sleep-and-motor-learning field: participants trained
on a five-element finger-tapping sequence, then retested either after twelve waking hours or
after a night's sleep. Waking retention produced no reliable gain; sleep produced roughly a
20% speed improvement with no loss of accuracy, and the size of that gain tracked how much
stage-2 NREM sleep a participant got, especially in the last quarter of the night — REM amount
showed no such relationship. It is one of the most cited papers in the sleep-memory literature
and the direct ancestor of the chunk-concatenation work the other two entries in this pull are
built on.

Its role here is anchoring, not primary evidence. It cannot, on its own, separate "sleep formed
better chunks" from "sleep made execution of the same chunks more fluent" — that refinement
came fifteen years later from Bottary et al. and Blischke & Malangré, who broke the same broad
phenomenon down to the transition level. What it does contribute independently is a large,
well-replicated, foundational confirmation that the relevant sleep stage for this class of
effect is stage-2 NREM, not REM — converging with, rather than merely preceding, the later
finer-grained work's stage attribution. Read alongside the Bottary entry, this strengthens
MECH-490's `depends_on` finding on MECH-204: two independent lines of evidence, twenty years and
a much finer measurement grain apart, both point away from REM sleep as the relevant stage for
this class of motor-sequence effect.

Confidence is held at a moderate level specifically because the outcome measure here (aggregate
sequence speed and accuracy) is too coarse to speak to MECH-490's specific formation-vs-fluency
distinction, and because a fixed, once-learned, single-session finger sequence is a much simpler
object than a Fishtank agent's continuously replanned, environment-conditioned action sequence.
It earns its place as corroborating canonical context for the stage-specificity question, not as
the primary evidentiary basis for the mechanism itself.
