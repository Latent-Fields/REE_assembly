## Nader & Hardt 2009 -- memory as a principally dynamic process (grounds the contested state and the fallible-operator premise)

According to PubMed, Nader and Hardt's 2009 *Nature Reviews Neuroscience* review ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/nrn2590)) is the strong-form case for reconsolidation, and it speaks to the memory_lifecycle_v4 cluster at two levels. The narrow point: consolidated memories can re-enter states of transient instability following reactivation, from which they must stabilise again in order to persist. The broad point, which is the one I care about, is methodological -- the authors show that the same operational definitions and the same kinds of evidence underpin both consolidation and reconsolidation, and conclude that memory is *principally a dynamic process* rather than a write-once-then-decay one.

This is direct grounding for the `contested` state in MECH-391's lifecycle store: a trace that had settled into `consolidated` can be pulled back into a labile condition by reactivation. Where Dudai & Eisenberg bound that window, Nader & Hardt press the general case -- post-retrieval plasticity reflects processes akin to those that stabilised the memory in the first place. Read together, the two entries give an honest picture: the contested state is real (Nader & Hardt) but its eligibility is gated by recency and reactivation (Dudai & Eisenberg).

The deeper reason this paper matters for the whole cluster is the fallible-operator premise. If a memory's content can be *altered* during post-retrieval re-stabilisation, then a `consolidated` trace cannot be treated as immutable ground truth -- it is something that may have been silently rewritten on a prior reactivation. That is precisely the failure mode the memory-lifecycle store, the provenance layer (MECH-392), and the raw-episode-preservation invariant (INV-080) are designed around. Nader & Hardt give the biological reason those design moves are not paranoia: the brain's own memory operators are corrigible, so an engineered memory system that abstracts and re-writes without tracking provenance is courting the same corruption.

I rate this `supports` at 0.72. The source is a definitive review and the reconstructive nature of memory is well-established; the discount is the usual one -- the discrete state labels and the allocation policy are REE's additions, not the paper's. `exp_conf` stays 0; promotes nothing.
