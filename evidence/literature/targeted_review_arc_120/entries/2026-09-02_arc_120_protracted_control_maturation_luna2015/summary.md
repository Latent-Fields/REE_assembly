# An integrative model of the maturation of cognitive control

Luna, Marek, Larsen, Tervo-Clemmens & Chahal (2015), *Annual Review of Neuroscience* 38:151-170. PMID 26154978, PMC5661874. doi:10.1146/annurev-neuro-071714-034054.

## What the paper did

This is an integrative review rather than a study. Luna's group pulls together cellular, circuit and systems-level evidence on how cognitive control matures from childhood into adulthood, and proposes a model of what actually changes. Their central observation is a careful one: the *component* processes of cognitive control -- inhibitory control, performance monitoring, working memory -- are largely available early. What improves into adulthood is the ability to exert control *consistently*, which they attribute to increasing flexible integration of those components. The substrate story is that maturational changes in brain structure, supported by interactions between dopaminergic and GABAergic systems, raise network synchronisation and signal-to-noise ratio, and those in turn drive the specialisation and strengthening of the networks that support adult-level control. Adolescence, on this reading, is not a deficit period but an adaptive one -- heightened experience-seeking is what the specialisation requires.

## Why this bears on ARC-120

Every other entry in this directory speaks to ARC-120's arbitration half. This one is here for the developmental half -- the assertion that REE's gates are instances of a sequence, existence -> representation -> competence -> authority -> behavioural influence, rather than unrelated ad hoc gates. If that sequence is a real regularity and not just a tidy retrospective story about four REE mechanisms, it ought to be visible somewhere in biology over developmental time. Luna et al. is the clearest place I found it.

The specific correspondence: the computation exists early, is recruitable early, and can produce the right output early. What is withheld is reliable behavioural authority, and what earns it is not the arrival of a new mechanism but the accumulation of *demonstrated consistency*. That distinction -- capacity versus consistency -- is the thing I would take from this paper into REE. A competence test that asks whether a mechanism *can* produce the right output will pass a ten-year-old's inhibitory control system. A competence test that asks whether it does so reliably across contexts will not. ARC-120's instances should be audited against that distinction.

## The caveat that does most of the work

There is a real problem with reading this as evidence for ARC-120, and I want to state it plainly rather than bury it. ARC-120 asserts a *gate* -- authority is withheld, and released on a competence condition. What Luna et al. document is an *ordering*, and the ordering is at least partly imposed by the substrate. Myelination takes years. Receptor systems mature on their own schedule. Signal-to-noise improves because the hardware improves. A system can display exactly ARC-120's sequence for reasons that have nothing to do with any architectural commitment to earning authority -- the sequence would fall out of slow hardware even if nothing in the brain were gating anything.

This matters for how the evidence should be counted. It is genuine support for the *ordering being real and general*, which is the part of ARC-120 that would otherwise rest on four REE mechanisms and a thought-intake observation. It is weak support for the *gate*, and it is no support at all for the claim's normative half ("and should remain"). The arbitration entries (Daw 2005, Lee 2014) carry the gate; this one carries the ordering. The two halves of the claim have different evidence bases and different strengths, and the governance record should reflect that rather than averaging them.
