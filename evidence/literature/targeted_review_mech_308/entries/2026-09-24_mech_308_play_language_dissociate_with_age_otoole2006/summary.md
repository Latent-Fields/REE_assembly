O'Toole and Chiat tested 21 children with Down syndrome (32 to 95 months) on pretend play, a novel symbolic comprehension task, and standardised language and nonverbal tests. With chronological age partialled out, symbolic play and symbolic comprehension correlated with each other and with expressive and receptive language, but not with nonverbal ability. The link was strongest in the youngest children; with age and language growth the measures began to dissociate.

Two things follow for MECH-308. First, the play-language link was specific relative to nonverbal cognition, which is the kind of specificity the claim needs. Second, the link weakened as language developed, which the authors interpret as language becoming domain-specific with experience. That fits a bootstrap story (play scaffolds early language, which then consolidates in its own right) better than the claim's current wording that language develops "not via a parallel language-acquisition substrate".

The sample is small and clinical, the age trend is cross-sectional, and Down syndrome carries its own language profile, so this is weak evidence either way.

Confidence 0.35, direction mixed.
