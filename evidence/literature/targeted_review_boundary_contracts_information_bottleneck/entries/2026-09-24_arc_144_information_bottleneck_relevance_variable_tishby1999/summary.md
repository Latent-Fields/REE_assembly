# Tishby, Pereira & Bialek 1999 -- the information bottleneck

**What they did.** The paper sets up compression as a trade-off: find a representation T of a signal X that is as compressed as possible (low I(T;X)) while keeping as much information as possible about a second variable Y (high I(T;Y)). Y is the "relevance variable": the thing someone downstream cares about. The paper frames this as rate-distortion theory where the distortion measure is not chosen by hand but falls out of Y, and gives self-consistent equations for the optimum.

**Why it matters for ARC-144.** The IB contains no notion of "the important information" apart from a choice of Y. Change the consumer's task and the optimal compression changes. That is the formal core of ARC-144's claim that a handoff's obligation depends on the directed pair (A, B) and not on a universal header. It also backs the claim's warning about value, salience and confidence: under the IB, a variable is relevant only relative to what a particular receiver needs.

**Where it doesn't reach.** The IB is a definition and an optimisation principle, not a finding about any real system. It says obligations *would* differ if consumers' relevance variables differ; whether they actually do across REE's or the brain's interfaces, and how big any overlap is, is the empirical question ARC-144 poses and the IB does not answer. The IB also has no term for what the receiver can reconstruct locally, which is the second half of ARC-144's I_AB definition. For GOV-CONTRACT-3 it counts Shannon information with an ideal decoder, which is the information-theoretic reducibility the rule calls inadmissible. So it is a starting formalism for contract cost, not support for the rule's operational standard.

**Confidence.** 0.45, supports, and mostly motivational. It is right to name it as the anchor for "what must pass depends on the consumer". It should not be cited as evidence that the contract actually is boundary-indexed.
