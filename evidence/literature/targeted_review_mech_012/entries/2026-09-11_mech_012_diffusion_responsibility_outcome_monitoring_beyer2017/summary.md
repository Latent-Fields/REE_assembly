# Beyond Self-Serving Bias (Beyer, Sidarus, Bonicalzi & Haggard, 2017)

If institutions diffuse residue, when does the diffusion happen? There is a deflationary answer --
it happens afterwards, in the telling, when a person who knows quite well what they did assembles a
story in which the group did it. And there is a stronger answer, the one MECH-012 needs: that being
in a collective changes how the harm is registered as it occurs. Beyer and colleagues set out to
separate these, and the design is elegant precisely because it removes the escape route. Participants
made voluntary actions with outcomes of varying negativity while EEG was recorded. On social trials
another potential agent was present. Crucially, it was always obvious who had caused each outcome:
there was nothing to be confused about and nothing to be gained by pretending.

Diffusion happened anyway. The presence of the other agent reduced participants' sense of agency
over outcomes they unambiguously caused, and it reduced the amplitude of the feedback-related
negativity evoked by those outcomes -- an early outcome-monitoring response, not a considered
judgement. The authors' conclusion is that co-presence weakens the neural linkage between action and
outcome. For MECH-012's second limb this is close to ideal evidence. Diffusion of responsibility is
not a story a person tells; it is a change in what gets processed. A REE substrate that maintained
exact per-agent residue accounting under collective conditions would be modelling something no human
collective resembles, and would systematically under-predict institutional harm.

And then the same result turns and takes something away from the claim, which is why I have scored
it mixed rather than supports. There was no utterance in this experiment. No label, no order, no
protocol, no euphemism -- nothing on a symbolic channel at all. One other person who might have
acted was sufficient. MECH-012 asserts that language is the *primary* medium through which residue
is managed or displaced in collective systems, and for the diffusion limb, as stated, that is simply
not what the data show. The honest reformulation, and I think the more interesting one, is that
co-presence opens the gap and language is what makes the gap durable: a bare social context produces
a transient attenuation in one agent, whereas a role, a title and a protocol produce an attenuation
that persists across occasions, transfers to new members, and can be defended when challenged. That
is a claim REE could actually test, and it is a different claim from the one currently written down.

The caveats run in the usual directions. "Presence of another potential agent" is a very thin social
context relative to an institution -- no roles, no norms, no persistence, no shared symbol system --
so the extrapolation to organisational diffusion is long. The outcomes were graded laboratory
penalties, not harm to a person, so this is evidence about outcome monitoring in general rather than
about harm signalling in particular. And the feedback-related negativity is a prediction-error
response; treating its amplitude as proportional to a residue write is an analogy REE imposes on the
data rather than one the data offer.

One further caution, which is the reason Philpot et al. 2020 also sits in this directory. A reduction
in an individual's outcome-monitoring response is not the same thing as a failure of accountability
at the level of the collective, and those two can come apart: a group composed of individuals each
showing attenuated monitoring can still respond reliably to visible harm. If REE derives institutional
non-response by summing individually attenuated residues, it will get the aggregate wrong. I have put
confidence at 0.70 -- the mechanism is real and well measured, but what it licenses is narrower and
more awkward for the claim than it first appears.
