# Engaged Followership and the Milgram Archive (Haslam, Reicher, Millard & McDonald, 2015)

MECH-012 ends with a prediction, and the prediction is the part REE would actually build a detector
around: ethical failure in institutions arises when symbolic language fully decouples from embodied
harm signals and residue tracking. This paper is the strongest reason in this directory to think that
prediction is wrong -- or at least dangerously incomplete -- and I have included it for that reason
rather than despite it.

Haslam and colleagues went to Box 44 of Yale's Milgram archive, where the feedback participants gave
after the obedience studies is held. The debate about those studies has long centred on whether
participants were distressed, a question shaped by the ethical controversy. The authors argue that
the more striking and less explained feature of the record is that participants were, in large
measure, *happy* to have taken part. They read this as the product of the experimenter's success in
convincing them they were contributing to a progressive enterprise, and propose an engaged-followership
model: willingness to do unpleasant things is contingent on identification with collective goals, and
leaders cultivate that identification by making the goals appear virtuous, which incidentally
ameliorates the stress of pursuing them. They are explicit that this is inconsistent with Milgram's
own agentic-state account, and they relocate the ethical problem accordingly -- from the distress the
studies caused to the ideology deployed to relieve it.

Set that against the REE prediction and the conflict is sharp. Decoupling predicts an agent whose
harm signal has been muted: flat affect, attenuated monitoring, residue that was never written. What
the archive describes is an agent whose affective system is fully engaged -- and engaged on the side
of continuing, because the act has been re-described as contribution. The language is not decoupled
from affect here. It is tightly coupled to it, and it has changed the sign. If that is what
institutional harm often looks like, then a REE monitor watching for absent or attenuated residue
will score the most dangerous configuration as the healthiest one in the building, because the
ideologically well-framed agent presents as untroubled and willing. That is an inverted detector, and
an inverted detector is worse than no detector.

The corrected form of the prediction, if this account holds, is not a question about coupling but a
question about write access. It stops mattering whether the symbol channel is connected to the harm
signal and starts mattering whether the symbol channel can write to the valence or goal
representation that the harm signal feeds. That interface is strictly harder to police than a gain
term on a harm input, and it is worth noticing that Caspar et al. 2016, sitting in this same
directory, describes something closer to the gain-term version. The two papers are not obviously
reconcilable, and I would rather record that honestly than let a tidy synthesis paper over it.

How much weight should this bear? Less than an experiment. This is archival and interpretive work on
feedback from studies run in the early 1960s: it shows that an alternative account fits the
historical record, not that the agentic-state account fails. The two mechanisms are not mutually
exclusive either -- Caspar's implicit and electrophysiological measures were taken under active
coercion and are untouched by an argument about retrospective participant satisfaction, so both
processes may operate, in different mixtures, in different institutional configurations. Milgram's
laboratory is a thin and peculiar institution on which to found a theory of institutions. And
"happiness at having participated" is retrospective self-report with every pressure on it one would
expect. I have set confidence at 0.62 and direction to weakens rather than mixed, because the target
is narrow and specific: this bears on the decoupling mechanism, and leaves the claim's first sentence
-- that language is the medium -- entirely intact. If anything, it makes that first sentence stronger.
