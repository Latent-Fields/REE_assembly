# Intervention Is the Norm in Public Conflicts (Philpot, Liebst, Levine, Bernasco & Lindegaard, 2020)

Half a century of bystander research established that an individual is less likely to intervene when
others are present, and MECH-012 leans on that tradition when it says collectives diffuse residue and
lose accountability. Philpot and colleagues asked the question the tradition had somehow not asked:
never mind whether any given individual helps -- if you are the victim, will *someone*? They coded
219 real public conflicts captured on surveillance cameras in Lancaster, Amsterdam and Cape Town.

In nine out of ten conflicts, at least one bystander -- usually several -- did something to help.
Rates were similar across three national contexts that differ enormously in perceived public safety.
And the result that should stop a REE modeller in their tracks: greater bystander presence was
associated with a *greater* likelihood that someone intervened. At the aggregate level the diffusion
prediction does not merely weaken, it inverts.

This does not falsify Beyer et al. 2017, sitting a few directories along, and it is important to see
why not. That study shows an individual-level mechanism that is real and neurally measurable:
co-presence attenuates outcome monitoring. This study shows that the individual-level mechanism does
not determine the collective-level behaviour. Both can be true, because a collective's response to
harm is a disjunction over its members, not a sum of their internal states -- and a disjunction over
many partially-attenuated agents can be more reliable than one unattenuated agent acting alone. For
REE the consequence is a hard modelling constraint on any future multi-agent substrate: residue
attenuation per agent and harm response per collective are separate quantities, and the second must
not be derived by summing the first. Doing so would reproduce exactly the inference the bystander
literature made for fifty years, and get the base rate spectacularly wrong.

What that leaves, I think, is a better version of MECH-012's second limb rather than a refutation of
it. If unstructured collectives meet visible harm with action nine times out of ten, then
institutional failure cannot come from the mere fact of being many. It has to come from something
institutions *add*: roles that assign the response to a specified person and therefore unassign it
from everyone else, protocols that define what counts as reportable harm, language that reclassifies
an event as not-an-emergency. That is a sharper claim, and a testable one. Given the multi-agent
substrate MECH-012 is currently blocked on, it suggests the decisive experiment is not "do many
agents respond less than one?" but "does the same set of agents respond less once a role and protocol
layer is imposed on them?" -- unstructured baseline against structured condition, with the symbolic
layer as the manipulated variable.

The caveats are genuinely double-edged here and I want to state them in both directions. These are
unstructured public crowds facing acute, visible, physical conflict: close to the best case for
intervention, and close to the worst case for studying institutions, since there are no roles, no
hierarchy, no protocol, no career consequences and no institutional language anywhere in the setting.
So the result may be fully compatible with MECH-012, showing what collectives do *before* an
institution gets hold of them. The measure is also behaviour, not residue -- nothing here tells us
what the non-intervening bystanders registered internally, so widespread individual attenuation could
sit invisibly behind a reliable aggregate. And street conflict does not obviously generalise to slow,
distributed, procedurally-mediated institutional harm, which is the case REE actually cares about. I
have scored it weakens at 0.64: its value to governance is that it forbids one specific and tempting
inference, not that it settles the claim.
